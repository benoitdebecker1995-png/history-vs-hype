### 1b. Read the comments — all of them

Never been done in a year. Roughly 600–700 comments across the videos that got real engagement:
Belize 381, Belize ICJ 74, Turkey/152 Greek islands 62, JD Vance 34, Israel/Palestine 30, Kashmir 28,
KGB/Palestine 27, Essequibo 25, Crusades 16, Somaliland 15.

Four things, in descending order of value:

1. **Corrections.** Every factual challenge, checked. This is free expert review and it is also the
   largest live credibility risk — an uncorrected error under a video about how claims get distorted
   is the worst possible failure.
2. **Who these people are.** Country, prior knowledge, personal connection to the subject, and what
   brought them — a news event, a search, an argument they were having.
3. **What they asked for next.** Leads, with the video they came from. Several independent people
   asking the same thing is a real signal; one detailed request is not.
4. **Where the argument actually landed.** Which claim they engaged with, which they disputed, which
   they ignored. The gap between that and what you thought the point was is the useful part.

**Deliverable:** an audience document short enough to read in five minutes, plus a corrections list
with each item checked and a verdict.

**Why this first.** Codex's line: *"The project knows much more about sources than about viewers."*
It is the only one of the five remaining capabilities that needs nothing from you and no new video.

## Day 2 — What the existing catalogue can still earn

Roughly fifty long-form videos, most under 300 views. Some of them have live search demand and bad
packaging. Fixing those is the cheapest growth available: no filming, no research, no new video.

**Retention across the top fifteen.** Pull the curves, map them to the scripts, and look for a
*repeating* pattern rather than a story about one video. Specifically: is there a common opening
failure? The channel has prior work on openers; check whether any finding is new before presenting it
as new. Honest about sample size — most of these have too few sessions to support a confident claim
about a specific beat, and I will say so where that is true.

**Search terms already reaching the channel.** There is search-term data across 34 videos. Which
queries land, on which videos, and whether the title matches what people typed.

**Retitle and re-thumbnail candidates, ranked.** A video qualifies when demand exists, the video
answers it, and the packaging does not say so. For each: the current title, the specific proposed
title, the reason, and what I expect to happen.

**Deliverable:** a ranked list with proposed titles written out, ready to paste. One variable at a
time — title *or* thumbnail, never both, which is why four of your five recorded swaps taught you
nothing. Prediction recorded before each change.

## Day 3 — Choose the next video, properly

Only after Days 1 and 2, because both feed it.

**Inputs:** the recovered August research (`_recovered/`), the audience findings from Day 1, the
demand evidence from Day 2, and the finding that cuts against the whole provenance thesis — single
claim provenance is being commoditised by short-form right now. Eight same-angle Trojan Horse videos
between 24 June and 20 August, all near zero, while an eleven-second Napoleon/Sphinx video did 497,064.

**The kill test, on the exact angle, before anything is recommended.** This is what nearly cost a
production cycle last time. Named competing videos with real numbers, and an honest statement of which
bet you would be taking: *nobody has thought of this* or *nobody has done it properly*.

**Deliverable:** one recommendation with the decisive evidence, the strongest counterargument, what
would reverse it, and how it could fail. Not a menu. Plus the prediction written down before the
outcome is known.
