Avoid staccato sentence fragments used for rhetorical emphasis. They sound unnatural when read aloud.

**Why:** User explicitly flagged this multiple times across script drafts (#54 Spanish Inquisition). "Sounds super unnatural to me."

**How to apply:** When writing or editing scripts, replace fragment triplets and punchy short sentences with flowing prose connected by dashes or conjunctions. Examples of what to fix:
- "Not propaganda. Not a confession. A bureaucratic manual." → "Not propaganda, not a confession — a bureaucratic manual."
- "Same article. Same page. The rule and its escape hatch." → "Same article, same page — the rule and its escape hatch."
- "He was reconciled to the Church. Cleared." → "He was reconciled to the Church and cleared."
- Fragment lists like "The rules for torture. Who had to be in the room. What could and couldn't be done." → flowing list with commas and a connector

**Exception:** Single short sentences for genuine dramatic beats are fine (e.g., "Paragraph fifteen." as a standalone pause). The problem is 2-3+ fragments in a row, not all short sentences.

**Anaphora ≠ staccato.** Three full sentences with a repeated stem ("He wrote rules that... He wrote rules that... He wrote rules that...") is anaphoric rhythm, not staccato. The staccato rule applies to FRAGMENTS (incomplete sentences used for emphasis), not to full parallel sentences. Anaphora is a deliberate rhetorical device that lands as accumulating emphasis. Source: Video #54 §7 close.

**Frequency cap applies to all rhetorical devices.** Staccato is effective sparingly. So is anaphora, declarative two-beat, fragment for dramatic beat, parallel-question, em-dash compound used as an emphasis pivot ("Torquemada didn't invent cruelty — he organized it"). Any rhetorical/emphasis device used 3+ times in a 5-min script becomes filler unless it's a deliberate refrain. Vary the palette — devices lose force with repetition.

**What does NOT count toward the cap:** Structural punctuation in the service of natural spoken cadence. Em-dashes for *apposition* ("Tomás de Torquemada — the first Grand Inquisitor — sat down...") and parenthetical specification ("hot-blooded torture — improvised, vindictive, reserved for whoever seemed guilty") are baseline conversational prose, not rhetorical devices. They appear 10+ times in any natural script and that's fine. Only *emphasis* uses of em-dashes — where the dash delivers a punchline contrast — count toward the cap.
