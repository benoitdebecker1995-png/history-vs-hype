**Date:** 30 July 2026  
**Research status:** preliminary archival verification, suitable for angle adjudication but **not yet cleared for final-script quotation**  
**Discipline:** claims marked **Established**, **Contested**, **Unsupported as phrased**, or **Unknowable**. A locator followed by **[acquire scan]** has been identified bibliographically but the relevant page/image was not inspected in this pass.

## Executive verdict

### Part 1 — Is “referee” an honest posture?

**Yes, but only if the dispute is framed correctly.** The closest of the proposed classifications is **(a), genuine live disagreement among qualified specialists**, with an important element of **(c): the popular prosecution frequently overstates what the archive proves about Churchill personally**.

There is no serious specialist case that the famine was merely an unavoidable natural disaster or that British imperial government had no responsibility. There is also no specialist consensus that Churchill deliberately created the famine, exported Bengal’s food to Britain, or bears *primary* responsibility for every decisive failure. The live disputes concern:

1. the size of the 1942 rice-supply shock versus entitlement/market failure;
2. the relative causal weight of Bengal’s ministry, the Governor, the Government of India, and London;
3. whether London’s shipping decisions were a decisive cause of peak mortality or a culpable failure to mitigate a catastrophe already produced inside India;
4. how much Churchill’s documented hostility toward Indians explains policy, rather than merely revealing prejudice.

The honest referee video is therefore **not** “Did racist Churchill murder three million people, or was he blameless?” That would manufacture two untenable poles. The defensible trial is:

> **Claim on trial:** Winston Churchill and his War Cabinet knowingly withheld relief that could have substantially reduced the Bengal famine death toll.

That claim is serious, specific, documentable, and genuinely contestable. The likely verdict is **partly proved**: London delayed and constrained overseas relief, accepted priorities that left India exposed, and did not treat mass Indian death with the urgency later shown when India’s stability became strategically threatening. But the evidence does not establish a plan to starve Bengal, and it does not erase earlier, independently catastrophic failures by Bengal and the Government of India.

### How much better is one side?

- Against the claim **“Churchill deliberately caused/engineered the famine”**, the exculpatory case is much better supported. Intent to kill is not established.
- Against the claim **“Churchill had no meaningful responsibility”**, the prosecution is much better supported. London controlled overseas shipping and war priorities and received increasingly grave warnings.
- Against **primary responsibility**, the archive does not yield a clean single culprit. The 1945 official inquiry assigned major responsibility to the Bengal government and explicit shared responsibility to the Government of India. London’s contribution is clearest in the late and inadequate external-supply response, not in Bengal’s price controls, procurement, rationing, or rural relief.

## Part 2 — Five audience claims adjudicated

## R1 — “They forced farmers to produce cash crops instead of food, then shipped what was left to England as reserves.”

### Verdict: **Unsupported as phrased**

It combines three different histories:

1. Bengal’s long-run vulnerability, including jute cultivation, landlessness, debt, and dependence on rice markets;
2. the 1942 **rice-denial and boat-denial** measures in coastal districts;
3. wartime grain movements to other Allied-dependent regions.

This pass found **no evidence that Bengal cultivators were ordered in 1942–43 to replace food with cash crops**, and **no evidence that Bengal/Indian grain exports in the famine period went to Britain or Europe**. That is not a claim that no such shipment exists: the sources searched were the Famine Inquiry Commission report, the November 1943 parliamentary food debate/White Paper discussion, and the shipping literature identified below. A destination-level customs table still needs to be acquired before a script says “none.”

### What the primary record does establish

**India’s customs account for fiscal 1942–43 showed net exports, not net imports.** On 4 November 1943, Secretary of State for India Leo Amery told the Commons that recorded foodgrain exports exceeded recorded imports by **361,000 tons** in 1942–43. He immediately cautioned that the import figure excluded grain imported on government account. This is why apparently contradictory tables exist: some measure ordinary customs trade, others add government shipments. See UK Parliament, Commons, 4 November 1943, “India (Food Situation),” [Hansard](https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/1943-11-04/debates/3cbcea16-3749-4798-ab7e-78bc0f3444c2/CommonsChamber).

The often-cited figure for the first seven months of 1943 is **21,000 tons of wheat and 70,000 tons of rice exported**, reportedly to Ceylon, the Persian Gulf, and Arabian ports—not England. I located this figure in later arguments that point to the official White Paper, but did **not** inspect the destination table itself. Treat it as **[acquire White Paper/Table III]**, not cleared narration.

The Commission gives a firmer Bengal-specific pre-famine figure. During **January–July 1942**, Bengal imported 135,000 tons of rice and exported 320,000 tons: a **net export of 185,000 tons**. In the last five months it imported 27,000 and exported 30,000 tons, making the full-year balance approximately **188,000 tons net exported**. The Commission attributes the exceptional demand to areas deprived of Burmese rice and specifically names Ceylon and western India in the surrounding discussion; it does not identify Britain as the destination. Exports except by permit were prohibited on 16 July 1942. FIC, Part I, printed pp. 28–29.

The correct on-screen formulation is therefore:

> India was still exporting some grain to vulnerable Allied territories while Bengal starved. The official customs series even showed net exports in fiscal 1942–43, although it omitted government-account imports. The evidence inspected does not show famine grain being shipped to Britain.

### What the Denial Policy actually did

After the Japanese conquest of Burma, military authorities sought to deny an invading army local transport and surplus rice in Bengal’s vulnerable coastal belt.

- **Rice denial:** surplus rice/paddy beyond estimated local needs was bought and removed in coastal parts of Midnapore, Bakarganj, and Khulna. The total purchased was **no more than 40,000 tons**, completed between April and July 1942. The Commission found no evidence that this amount itself created physical scarcity, but concluded that the operation increased alarm and holding. *Famine Inquiry Commission, Report on Bengal* (Delhi, 1945), Part I, printed pp. 25–26, [scan](https://www.indiaofthepast.org/sites/indiaofthepast.org/files/pdf/1940s/bengalfamine/ficreportpart1.pdf).
- **Boat denial:** the order took effect on 1 May 1942 and covered boats capable of carrying ten or more passengers in the vulnerable delta. **66,563 boats** were registered: 1,613 requisitioned, 3,373 destroyed, 4,143 sunk recoverably, 17,546 placed in reception stations, 19,471 removed elsewhere by owners, and 20,417 left in place. Ibid., p. 26.
- The Commission said the quantitative effect on food movement could not be measured, but the restriction had a **“considerable”** effect. Maintenance and restoration failed: 16,655 boats were still in reception stations on 1 April 1943. Fishermen lost livelihoods, and the Commission believed deaths would have been lower had boats been restored sooner. Ibid., p. 27.

**Responsibility:** the policy was a military anti-invasion measure implemented through the Bengal administration under the imperial war state. It was neither a simple Churchill personal order nor a Bengali-ministry-only initiative. The precise initiating military file should be acquired from the India Office/Pinnell papers before assigning an individual author.

### Cash crops: what can responsibly be said

Jute and the agrarian structure reduced household resilience and tied many cultivators to volatile markets. That is a **structural vulnerability**, not evidence of a 1943 forced-crop order. For acreage and crop composition use M. Mufakharul Islam, *Bengal Agriculture 1920–1946: A Quantitative Study* (Cambridge University Press, 1978), especially the crop-acreage tables **[acquire exact pages]**. Do not narrate a nineteenth-century indigo-style coercion story without a 1942–43 order.

## R2 — “Churchill wrote ‘Why hasn’t Gandhi died yet?’ in the margin.”

### Verdict: **Unsupported as a verbatim marginal note; supported as Wavell’s contemporary paraphrase**

The popular sentence is not the text of a Churchill marginal note inspected in this pass. It comes from Viceroy Archibald Wavell’s private journal entry for **5 July 1944**:

> “Winston sent me a peevish telegram to ask why Gandhi hadn’t died yet! He has never answered my telegram about food.”

Locator: Penderel Moon, ed., *Wavell: The Viceroy’s Journal* (Oxford University Press, 1973), entry 5 July 1944, **p. 94 in the edition cited by the Churchill-document reconstruction; an alternate secondary citation gives p. 78, so inspect the edition before using the page on screen**.

The underlying Churchill message said:

> “Surely Mr Gandhi has made a most remarkable recovery as he is already able to take an active part in politics. How does this square with medical reports upon which his release on grounds of ill-health was agreed to by us? In one of these we were told that he would not be able to take any part in politics again.”

Locator: Nicholas Mansergh and E. W. R. Lumby, eds., *The Transfer of Power 1942–7*, vol. IV, document 584, 5 July 1944, p. 1070; India Office record **L/PO/10/25** **[acquire scan of document]**. A document-based reconstruction is available [here](https://richardlangworth.com/mr-gandhi-hasnt-died-yet), but the edited primary volume must be the on-screen source.

**Adjudication:**

- Wavell’s line is excellent evidence of how Wavell understood the tone and implication of Churchill’s telegram.
- It is **not** the verbatim Churchill wording.
- It was about Gandhi’s recovery and return to politics after release on medical grounds.
- It was **not** written on a Bengal mortality report.
- Wavell’s next sentence contrasts Churchill’s Gandhi message with an unanswered food telegram. Later retellings often fuse them into one document.

### Other remarks: evidentiary tiers

1. **Secure as contemporary participant testimony, not Churchill autograph:** Leo Amery recorded Churchill saying, “I hate Indians. They are a beastly people with a beastly religion.” Locator: *The Leo Amery Diaries*, ed. John Barnes and David Nicholson, vol. II (Hutchinson, 1988), entry **9 September 1942**, approximately pp. 832–33 depending on edition **[acquire page image]**. Context was wartime Indian politics/Quit India, not a famine-relief memorandum.
2. **Secure evidence of Amery’s alarm about Churchill:** Amery recorded telling Churchill that he saw little difference between Churchill’s outlook and Hitler’s, and wondered whether Churchill was “quite sane” on India. Entry **4 August 1944** **[acquire exact page/image before quotation]**.
3. **“They breed like rabbits”: not cleared.** It is frequently attributed through Amery or later biographers, but no exact diary page or original memorandum was inspected here. Do not use it as a Churchill quote card until the diary entry is acquired.

These remarks establish prejudice and are relevant to judgment and urgency. They do not, by themselves, prove that prejudice caused a particular shipping decision.

## R3 — “Australia sent wheat for Bengal and Churchill diverted the ships.”

### Verdict: **Unsupported as phrased; the underlying case of delayed/refused shipping is partly supported**

No inspected document shows an Australian relief cargo already sailing specifically for Bengal that Churchill personally turned around. What the record shows is a sequence of **requests, limited allocations, substitutions, shipping constraints, and later larger shipments**.

### The Canadian offer often misremembered as an Australian diversion

Canada offered **100,000 tons of wheat**. Amery explained that shipping from Australia required less than half the time required from Canada, and that the Canadian offer therefore had largely not been moved; equivalent sourcing from Australia was preferred. This is a refusal to allocate scarce shipping to the Canadian cargo, not diversion of an Australian Bengal ship. UK Parliament, Commons, 11 November 1943, “India (Food Situation),” [Hansard](https://hansard.parliament.uk/commons/1943-11-11/debates/7b8efd07-4205-489d-a601-d7f3d6a4cdec/India%28FoodSituation%29).

The same debate records an overlooked complication: roughly **150,000 tons of wheat** had been imported in the winter/spring of 1942–43; further shipments were suspended in May 1943, with Government of India concurrence, after an excellent Punjab wheat crop. That does not excuse later delay, but it weakens the idea that London rejected an obvious, constant emergency request from the beginning.

### What Bengal actually received in 1943

The Famine Inquiry Commission’s India-wide dispatch totals are not identical to overseas relief:

- rice dispatched to Bengal from the rest of India in 1943: **294,000 tons**; recorded arrivals **264,000 tons**;
- wheat and wheat products dispatched from other provinces and overseas: **373,000 tons**; recorded arrivals **339,000 tons**;
- of wheat dispatches, **222,000 tons** came from Punjab, **85,000** from the United Provinces, **10,000** from Sind, and **93,000** from overseas;
- a further **55,000 tons** of millets arrived.

Locator: FIC, *Report on Bengal*, Part I, printed pp. 54–55 (tables and accompanying text), [scan](https://www.indiaofthepast.org/sites/indiaofthepast.org/files/pdf/1940s/bengalfamine/ficreportpart1.pdf).

These totals prove that aid arrived, but not that it was early or adequate. Quarterly arrival timing is crucial: rice arrivals were approximately **17,000 / 78,000 / 69,000 / 100,000 tons** by quarter; wheat and wheat products **26,000 / 38,000 / 99,000 / 176,000**. Much of the acceleration therefore came after the crisis was visible and mortality was climbing.

### Shipping chronology: what is verified and what still needs the Cabinet files

- **26 August 1943:** an American diplomatic report recorded an Indian official saying six shiploads could remedy the immediate situation, while the local British Ministry of War Transport representative said wheat had already been shipped from Canada. *Foreign Relations of the United States* (FRUS), 1943, vol. IV, doc. 336, [official text](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v04/d336).
- **4 September 1943:** an American message reported acute distress, devitalization, and hundreds dying, and urged Churchill to arrange grain from America, Australia, or elsewhere. FRUS 1943, vol. IV, doc. 340 and note 76, [official text](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v04/d340fn76).
- **8 October 1943:** Churchill instructed Wavell that every effort should be made, “even by diversion of shipping urgently needed for war purposes,” to address local shortages. Locator usually given as Churchill Papers **CHAR 23/11** **[acquire image and full context]**.
- The reported War Cabinet sequence—4 August authorization of Iraqi barley plus a limited Australian wheat shipment, 24 September enlargement, and deliveries from mid-October—must be checked against **CAB 65/CAB 66 and Transfer of Power vol. IV** before exact “requested/refused/allocated” numbers enter narration. Later summaries variously report 130,000 tons of Iraqi barley, 80,000 of Australian wheat, and 10,000 from Canada by January 1944, followed by another 100,000 from Australia. These figures are plausible but **not cleared in this pass**.
- **29 April 1944:** Churchill asked Roosevelt for special ships, stating that at least 700,000 had died, that Wavell wanted about one million tons, and that Britain had arranged **350,000 tons of Australian wheat for the first nine months of 1944** by reducing military shipments and other means. FRUS 1944, vol. V, doc. 281, [official text](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v05/d281).
- Roosevelt declined the special shipping request on military advice. The British reply then reported arrangements for **400,000 tons before the end of September 1944**, additional to 400,000 since October 1943, and admitted that this was less than Wavell requested. FRUS 1944, vol. V, docs. 281–283, especially [doc. 283](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v05/d283).

### What survives of Mukerjee’s argument

Madhusree Mukerjee’s archive-based claim is not that one named ship was physically turned around; it is that the **August 1943 shipping assignments sent Australian flour to Ceylon, the Middle East, and Southern Africa while India’s request was constrained**, and that British stockpile/postwar priorities were preferred. That is a prioritization argument. It could be inculpatory even if every ship followed its original sailing order.

**Required acquisition before a final verdict:** War Cabinet minutes and memoranda for 4 August, 24 September, and November 1943; Ministry of War Transport allocation schedules; the corresponding documents in *Transfer of Power*, vol. IV. Until then, the documentary verdict is:

> No proved Australian “famine ship” diversion; proved limits and delay in allocating shipping; proved later Australian shipments; the counterfactual number of lives an earlier allocation would have saved is unknowable.

## R4 — “The Famine Code obliged neighbouring provinces to release stocks, and they refused.”

### Verdict: **First clause unsupported in the sources searched; second clause substantially supported**

The Bengal Famine Manual/Famine Code governed Bengal’s recognition of scarcity and its relief duties. I found no provision making it an interstate compulsory-transfer law. Indeed, a September 1943 American diplomatic report said Bengal’s Chief Minister admitted that he avoided formally declaring famine because his government could not carry out the resulting Code obligations. FRUS 1943, vol. IV, doc. 337, [official text](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v04/d337). The 1941 Bengal *Famine Manual* must still be inspected before saying categorically that no cross-border clause existed.

### What provinces did

After Burma fell, provinces behaved as “food republics,” protecting their own consumers and overstating deficits. The Government of India’s September 1942 Basic Plan attempted central allocation and “equality of sacrifice.” Bengal said in December 1942 that export was impossible and was uncertain it needed rice imports in the next few months. Bihar was assessed for 200,000 tons of rice and 50,000 of wheat but declared itself a deficit and lacked procurement machinery. The Basic Plan failed because declared surpluses and actual deliveries did not match. FIC, Part I, pp. 50–54.

In 1943:

- a modified/free-trade experiment required the centre to withdraw or suspend provincial movement restrictions;
- Bihar restored its embargo on **31 July 1943**;
- powers to control movement were restored to Assam, Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa with effect from **16 August 1943**;
- under the revised July–November plan, Bengal’s rice allocation collapsed from **216,900 tons** to **15,000 tons**, compensated on paper by wheat, gram, and millet; surplus administrations declared only **79,000 tons of rice** available.

Locators: FIC, Part I, pp. 57–58.

### Was it lawful?

Under ordinary constitutional law, **section 297 of the Government of India Act 1935** prohibited a provincial legislature or government from restricting the entry or export of goods across provincial boundaries. Official PDF: [Government of India Act 1935](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1935/2/pdfs/ukpga_19350002_en.pdf), s. 297.

Wartime emergency law changed the position. **Rule 81(2) of the Defence of India Rules** empowered the central or a provincial government to regulate or prohibit production, storage, movement, transport, distribution, and disposal of essential goods. The relevant text is reproduced in the reported litigation [*Punjab Province v. Daulat Singh*](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1999375/). The centre had delegated these powers and could withdraw or restore them; the FIC explicitly identifies their restoration on 16 August.

So the embargoes were not simple illegal defiance. They were lawful under emergency delegation, and the centre itself bears responsibility for designing, withdrawing, and restoring that regime.

### How much did provincial refusal matter?

It mattered greatly to **timing, confidence, and distribution**, but no honest single tonnage converts directly into deaths. The strongest numbers are:

- original July–November rice plan: 216,900 tons;
- revised rice allocation: 15,000 tons;
- actual 1943 rice dispatches from the rest of India: 294,000 tons;
- actual arrivals: 264,000 tons;
- wheat/wheat-product dispatches: 373,000; arrivals: 339,000.

The actual annual totals include later relief and cannot be used as “what neighbors withheld at the peak.” The counterfactual requires monthly source-province procurement and rail dispatch records.

## R5 — “Bengal was not ruled by Britain; it had self-rule under Bengali Indians.”

### Verdict: **Partly true about day-to-day portfolios; false as a description of sovereignty**

The 1935 Act created substantial provincial autonomy but not independence. Bengal had an elected legislature and Indian ministers, while an unelected British Governor remained head of the province, appointed/dismissed ministers, possessed discretionary and reserve powers, and could assume government. Above Bengal stood the Viceroy/Government of India and London’s control of imperial strategy and overseas shipping.

### Division of authority

| Level | Relevant authority in 1943 | What it controlled / failed to control |
|---|---|---|
| Bengal ministry | Agriculture, intraprovincial trade and supply, procurement, price policy, urban distribution/rationing, Famine Code relief, public health, district administration | Its controlled procurement failed; it decontrolled badly; promoted “No Shortage”; failed to introduce general rationing in 1943; delayed recognition and adequate rural relief. |
| Governor of Bengal | Appointed ministers; could act in discretion/individual judgment; special responsibility for peace; could assume provincial powers under s. 93 | John Herbert shaped ministry formation and had imperial leverage, but the FIC rejected ministers’ claim that he normally removed food policy from cabinet. Reserve government was used only during the March–April ministry transition, not as a famine rescue administration. |
| Government of India / Viceroy | Interprovincial allocation and delegation under emergency powers; central Food Department; rail/strategic priorities; army deployment; representations to London | Started all-India planning too late; Basic Plan failed; shared responsibility for disastrous decontrol; delayed decisive intervention and army-backed distribution. |
| London / War Cabinet | Ocean shipping, imperial war allocation, overseas imports, ultimate political supervision | Constrained and delayed external grain allocation; balanced India against military and other imperial claims; later found more shipping when India’s stability acquired greater strategic urgency. |

Constitutional locators:

- 1935 Act, s. 50: governor ordinarily acted on ministerial advice except in discretion or individual judgment.
- s. 51: ministers were appointed by the governor and held office during his pleasure.
- s. 52: special responsibilities, including peace and tranquility.
- s. 93: assumption of provincial powers if constitutional government could not be carried on.
- Seventh Schedule, Provincial List, entries 20 (agriculture), 27 (trade and commerce within the province), and 29 (production, supply, and distribution of goods).

### Who governed Bengal in 1943?

- **A. K. Fazlul Huq** headed the Progressive Coalition ministry until the end of March 1943.
- Governor **Sir John Herbert** assumed authority under s. 93 on **31 March** during the ministry crisis.
- Herbert invited **Khawaja Nazimuddin** to form a ministry; the proclamation was revoked on **24 April**, and the Muslim League-led coalition took office in late April.
- **H. S. Suhrawardy** became the minister responsible for Civil Supplies in the Nazimuddin government and became the principal political face of supply policy. The definitive 1943 portfolio roster should be acquired from the *Bengal Legislative Assembly Proceedings/Gazette* before placing the exact appointment date on screen.
- Herbert was gravely ill; **Sir Thomas Rutherford** took over as Governor in September. Herbert died in December.
- **Lord Linlithgow** was Viceroy until **20 October 1943**; **Lord Wavell** succeeded him. Wavell visited Bengal almost immediately and brought the army into distribution and relief.

For the ministry chronology see the [West Bengal Legislative Assembly historical overview](https://lalib.wb.gov.in/showOverview); for any on-screen roster, replace this institutional summary with the Gazette.

### Were the Governor’s reserve powers used or withheld?

The FIC directly addressed the allegation that the governor and permanent officials controlled policy. It was **“unable to endorse”** the ministers’ claim: important food questions normally went to cabinet. It nevertheless found friction among ministers, governor, and officials harmful. FIC, Part I, printed pp. 85–86.

Section 93 was used from 31 March to 24 April because of ministry formation, not to impose an expert famine administration. No inspected evidence shows Herbert invoking reserve powers to take over food and relief at the decisive stage. Whether that counts as culpable non-use is a counterfactual judgment, not an archival fact.

The official inquiry’s final allocation of blame is the cleanest referee statement:

- Bengal could, by bold and timely action, have **“largely prevented”** the tragedy.
- The Government of India failed to plan interprovincial movement early and shared responsibility for decontrol.
- By August, “whatever the constitutional position,” the Government of India shared responsibility for saving life.
- Adequate distribution was not organized until after Wavell’s visit at the end of October.

Locator: FIC, Part I, printed pp. 104–107.

## Part 3 — What popular argument misses

### Decisive causal factors that are documentable on screen

1. **A real but imprecisely measured 1942 aman shock.** On 16 October 1942, cyclone, tidal wave, and flooding affected about 3,200 square miles. Crop fungus/root rot was probably more damaging than the storm itself. The crop was seriously short, but official production statistics were too weak to settle the magnitude. FIC, Part I, pp. 33–34; pair with S. Padmanabhan, “The Great Bengal Famine,” *Annual Review of Phytopathology* 11 (1973), pp. 11–24 **[acquire]** and Tauger.
2. **The loss of Burma and the eastern rice system.** Burma’s conquest removed a customary external source and generated refugees and military pressure. Bengal wheat imports also collapsed in late 1942/early 1943—13,259 tons in August 1942, 7,996 in November, 2,514 in January 1943, and 1,620 in February—forcing more consumers onto rice just as the aman crop failed. FIC, Part I, pp. 32–33.
3. **War inflation and unequal purchasing power.** Military expenditure, procurement, war-industry wages, and priority supply protected soldiers and strategic workers while rural laborers, fishermen, artisans, and smallholders could not match rice-price inflation. This is the enduring strength of Sen’s entitlement analysis even if aggregate supply did fall.
4. **Fragmented interprovincial sovereignty.** Provinces hoarded authority and grain; Delhi relied on persuasion and unreliable crop declarations. The Basic Plan came late and failed. FIC, pp. 50–58, 104–107.
5. **Bengal’s price-policy sequence.** Official procurement through selected private agents, inadequate requisition, price controls without sufficient stocks, March decontrol, failed free trade, and later renewed control generated uncertainty and extraordinary profits. The Commission criticized the failure to use requisitioning early enough. FIC, pp. 84–89.
6. **“No Shortage” propaganda and refusal to declare famine.** From April to June 1943, Bengal insisted supply was sufficient; the Government of India supported this line. The FIC judged it unjustified. Distress reports arrived early, but Bengal did not report the full position until June or issue adequate relief instructions until August. FIC, pp. 104–105; FRUS 1943 IV, doc. 337.
7. **No general rationing in 1943.** Calcutta’s protected/priority consumers drew from the market while the rural poor were exposed. The FIC said the failure to introduce rationing added greatly to difficulty. FIC, p. 69.
8. **Boat denial and transport damage.** This impaired fishing, petty trade, evacuation, and local food movement in a riverine economy. The precise food-tonnage effect is unknowable; the human and transport effect is not. FIC, pp. 25–27.
9. **Political breakdown.** Huq’s fall, Herbert’s ministry maneuvering, a month of s. 93 government, the Nazimuddin coalition, party/communal hostility, minister-official friction, and leadership turnover impeded response. FIC, pp. 85–87.
10. **Famine mortality was mostly disease-mediated.** Malaria, cholera, smallpox, dysentery/diarrhea, unsafe water, displacement, and collapsed health capacity killed malnourished people. Food availability improved before mortality normalized. FIC Part II, printed pp. 108–120 and following; Maharatna (1993).
11. **The late army intervention is a natural experiment in state capacity.** Army advance parties arrived 11 November; main bodies 19–27 November. They distributed about 8,000 tons by the third week of November and 24,000 by mid-December. FIC, Part I, approximately pp. 63–64 **[check printed pagination against scan]**. This does not prove the army could have prevented every death in May, but it proves that executive capacity changed sharply when priorities changed.

### Strongest less-circulated evidence against Churchill/London

The strongest *safe* exhibit is not an insult. It is the British official history of wartime merchant shipping, based on Ministry of War Transport files. C. B. A. Behrens records that India asked in December 1942 for 600,000 tons by the end of April, later moderated the immediate demand to 200,000 tons, and received **58,000 tons in spring 1943**. She also explains why erratic Indian forecasts damaged the request's credibility and why the tonnage demand was exceptionally difficult. Her conclusion is nevertheless severe: after other Indian Ocean commitments were met, the North African campaign left a failed-harvest Indian deficit area with almost no shipping safety margin. *Merchant Shipping and the Demands of War* (HMSO, 1955), printed pp. 352–353, [official-history scan](https://www.generalstaff.org/WW2/Hist_UK/MerchantShippingDemandsWar.pdf).

Behrens's Appendix LIX reconstructs grain shipments to India from Ministry files as roughly **30,000 tons in 1942, 303,000 in 1943, 639,000 in 1944, and 871,000 in 1945**; for 1943 the Famine Inquiry Commission's alternative total was about 370,000. Ibid., printed p. 356. This is the strongest single document against both cartoon versions: shipping scarcity was real, but it resulted from strategic allocation choices rather than an absence of grain; imports were not zero, but the large increase came after the mortality crisis. The history's broader causal interpretation is still an official postwar interpretation, not a substitute for the Cabinet minutes.

The second exhibit is the **timing contrast in the diplomatic record**:

- American officials were reporting acute distress and asking for external grain in August–September 1943.
- Wavell later sought roughly one million tons.
- Britain could arrange 350,000 tons from Australia in the first nine months of 1944 by reducing military shipments, and later cancelled three months of military maintenance sailings to create shipping for a further allocation.

FRUS 1943 IV, docs. 336, 340, 343; FRUS 1944 V, docs. 281–283. The evidentiary point is not that the same ships were free in August 1943. It is that “no shipping” was never an absolute physical fact; it was a hierarchy of priorities that changed after Bengal’s mortality peak and after the strategic danger became unmistakable.

The sharper prosecution exhibit may be the August 1943 War Cabinet allocation schedule cited by Madhusree Mukerjee, allegedly showing Australian flour assigned around the Indian Ocean but not India. **Do not use it until the CAB file is acquired.**

### Strongest less-circulated exculpatory evidence

The strongest is the **May 1943 suspension with Government of India concurrence** after a good Punjab wheat harvest, combined with Bengal’s late-1942 uncertainty about needing rice imports. This shows fatal informational and forecasting failure inside India, not a simple sequence in which Churchill was handed a clear famine-relief request and refused it from the outset. Hansard, 11 November 1943; FIC, pp. 50–51.

A second useful correction is the Canadian 100,000-ton offer: it was not simply discarded while identical Australian ships were diverted. London argued that Australian lift consumed less than half the shipping time and sourced grain accordingly. That may still be judged too little and too late, but it is a real logistics decision, not proof of a desire to deny food.

## Part 4 — Deaths: defensible range and method

### Recommended public formulation

> **About 1.5 to 3 million excess deaths is the defensible scholarly range. Roughly 2.1 million is a strong demographic midpoint, but no exact number is recoverable because wartime death registration failed most severely among migrants and the rural poor.**

Do not say “three million starved.” Many victims died from malaria, cholera, dysentery, smallpox, and other diseases after malnutrition and displacement destroyed resistance.

| Estimate | Producer / method | What drives it |
|---|---|---|
| **688,846 recorded excess deaths in calendar 1943** | Bengal Public Health Department: 1,873,749 recorded deaths minus the 1938–42 annual average of 1,184,903 | A registration minimum, not a full famine toll; excludes much continued excess mortality in 1944 and misses unregistered migrant/rural deaths. FIC Part II, printed pp. 108–109; also Amery’s Commons answer, 23 March 1944, [Hansard](https://hansard.parliament.uk/%E2%80%8CCommons/1944-03-23/debates/28eff858-0b8d-43bf-9a42-db4b2671110e/FamineDeathsBengal). |
| **About 1.5 million** | Famine Inquiry Commission: raised 1943 excess to about 1.0 million (about 40% above the recorded excess), then added high excess mortality in the first half of 1944 | Relied on defective vital registration, inquiries about undercount, and observed 1944 excess; conservative about high extrapolations. FIC Part II, printed pp. 108–110. |
| **3.5 million (contemporary claim rejected by FIC)** | K. P. Chattopadhyay extrapolated roughly 10% mortality in badly affected samples to two-thirds of Bengal | Samples concentrated in worst areas; FIC called the provincial extrapolation statistically unsound. This is important provenance for high early figures, not a modern consensus estimate. FIC Part II, pp. 108–109. |
| **Around 3 million** | Amartya Sen: reconstructed excess mortality using adjusted population/vital-statistics series and the 1951 census, extending beyond recorded deaths | Sensitive to assumed normal death rate, under-registration, migration, partition/census back-projection, and time window. *Poverty and Famines* (1981), Bengal chapters/tables **[acquire exact pages]**. |
| **About 2.1 million** | Tim Dyson and Arup Maharatna: demographic reconstruction from Bengal public-health reports, adjusting Sen’s procedure and the pre-famine declining mortality trend | Often treated as the most defensible central estimate. Applying Sen-like assumptions yields about 1.8–1.9 million; allowing for a declining pre-famine death-rate trend raises it to about 2.1 million. See Dyson and Maharatna, “Excess Mortality During the Great Bengal Famine: A Re-evaluation,” *Indian Economic and Social History Review* **[acquire full citation/pages]**; Maharatna (1996). |
| **3.5–3.8 million** | Often associated with Greenough’s broader social-demographic reconstruction | Depends on broader period and inclusion assumptions; exact table/method must be checked before attribution on screen. *Prosperity and Misery* **[acquire exact pages]**. |

### What the disagreement turns on

1. **Baseline:** a flat five-year average versus a declining pre-famine mortality trend.
2. **Time window:** calendar 1943, July 1943–June 1944, all of 1944, or a longer recovery period.
3. **Registration failure:** village watchmen died, deserted, or went unpaid; roadside and migrant deaths disappeared from village registers.
4. **Territorial/census reconstruction:** undivided Bengal, later East/West Bengal, migration, Partition, and use of the 1951 census.
5. **Causal attribution:** all excess deaths versus only deaths directly coded as starvation; disease deaths aggravated by famine belong in a famine toll, but unrelated epidemic variation must be considered.
6. **Sampling:** worst-hit village samples cannot be projected uniformly across Bengal.

The FIC itself warned that its age/sex percentages carried a “false air of accuracy.” That sentence is an excellent methodological on-screen exhibit. Part II, printed p. 112.

## Part 5 — Ranked reading and acquisition order

### A. Scholarly monographs and specialist works

1. **Famine Inquiry Commission, *Report on Bengal* (1945), Parts I–II.** Not independent of the colonial state, but indispensable, unusually self-incriminating, quantitative, and closest to the administrative record. Use its evidence and conclusions, not its authority alone. [Part I](https://www.indiaofthepast.org/sites/indiaofthepast.org/files/pdf/1940s/bengalfamine/ficreportpart1.pdf); [Part II](https://www.indiaofthepast.org/sites/indiaofthepast.org/files/pdf/1940s/bengalfamine/ficreportpart2.pdf).
2. **Janam Mukherjee, *Hungry Bengal* (OUP, 2015).** Best modern historian-led synthesis for war, politics, violence, and imperial structures. Read as an interpretive prosecution, but it is scholarly rather than polemical trade history.
3. **Paul Greenough, *Prosperity and Misery in Modern Bengal* (OUP, 1982).** Essential social history and household-level destruction.
4. **Arup Maharatna, *The Demography of Famines* (OUP, 1996).** Best mortality-method anchor; pair with his 1993 malaria article.
5. **Lance Brennan (1988) and Auriol Law-Smith (1989).** The two most directly useful peer-reviewed articles for provincial relief and Government of India responsibility.
6. **Amartya Sen, *Poverty and Famines* (OUP, 1981).** Foundational, but its Bengal production premise is no longer safe as the lone causal framework.
7. **M. Mufakharul Islam, *Bengal Agriculture 1920–1946* (CUP, 1978).** Quantitative prehistory; required for testing the cash-crop claim.
8. **Mark Tauger (2009), Cormac Ó Gráda (2010), and Peter Bowbrick (1986; later update).** Read together as the real-supply/expectations critique of a simplistic “there was plenty of food” version of Sen.
9. **Tirthankar Roy**, especially the famine discussion in *How British Rule Changed India’s Economy: The Paradox of the Raj* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2019). Useful revisionist synthesis emphasizing real shortage and administrative limits. His remarks on Churchill should be treated as economic-historical interpretation, not a complete Cabinet-file audit.
10. **Sugata Bose, *Agrarian Bengal: Economy, Social Structure and Politics, 1919–1947* (Cambridge University Press, 1986).** Structural agrarian and political context.

### B. Trade books / non-specialist briefs

1. **Madhusree Mukerjee, *Churchill’s Secret War* (2010).** Mine every footnote, then replace the book with the cited CAB/India Office document on screen. Known tendency: centers Churchill and infers a coherent imperial policy from prioritization decisions.
2. **Andrew Roberts, *Churchill: Walking with Destiny* (2018)** and shorter defenses. Use to find counterevidence; known tendency: Churchill-biographical defense, often compressing Bengal and Delhi failures into exculpation.
3. **Arthur Herman, *Gandhi & Churchill* (2008).** Broad political narrative, not famine-specialist adjudication.
4. **Zareer Masani and “History Reclaimed” essays.** Advocacy leads only; never the final authority for tonnage or provenance.

### C. Primary collections and files

1. **Nicholas Mansergh et al., eds., *The Transfer of Power 1942–7*, vol. III, *Reassertion of Authority: Gandhi’s Fast and the Succession to the Viceroyalty, 21 September 1942–12 June 1943*; vol. IV, *The Bengal Famine and the New Viceroyalty, 15 June 1943–31 August 1944*.** Vol. IV is the priority acquisition.
2. **C. B. A. Behrens, *Merchant Shipping and the Demands of War* (HMSO, 1955), especially pp. 352–356.** Official British history based on Ministry of War Transport files; the essential bridge between strategic commitments, shipping feasibility, and actual grain shipments.
3. **War Cabinet conclusions and memoranda:** UK National Archives **CAB 65** (Conclusions) and **CAB 66** (memoranda), especially 4 August, 24 September, October–November 1943, and February–May 1944.

10. **Government of Bengal, *Famine Manual* (1941)** and 1943 Assembly proceedings/Gazette for the Code claim and exact portfolio roster.

### What is superseded?

- Sen is **not superseded** on entitlement collapse; he is insufficient as the only account of food availability.
- The FIC is **not superseded** as a primary official synthesis; its mortality estimate and institutional self-protection require later demographic and archival correction.
- Madhusree Mukerjee is **not superseded** as a source-finding prosecutorial brief; she should not be treated as the final specialist verdict.
- “Churchill saved Bengal by sending grain” and “Churchill exported Bengal’s rice to England” are both superseded by a multi-level, date-specific supply and governance account.

## Proposed evidentiary verdict

### Established

- Bengal suffered a serious crop/supply shock compounded by Burma’s loss, inflation, transport disruption, market panic, and administrative failure.
- Bengal’s ministry controlled most local procurement, prices, rationing, and relief and failed disastrously.
- The Government of India failed at early interprovincial coordination and intervened decisively only late.
- London controlled overseas shipping and limited/delayed external relief while balancing military and other imperial priorities.
- Churchill expressed deep hostility toward Indians in contemporaneous testimony.
- Relief and distribution improved sharply after Wavell and army intervention.

### Contested

- The exact size of the rice deficit.
- Whether overseas grain available in summer 1943 could have arrived early enough and in sufficient quantity to prevent a large share of mortality.
- Whether Churchill’s prejudice causally drove Cabinet allocations or accompanied decisions dominated by war strategy.
- Which layer of government deserves the label “primary” responsibility.

### Unsupported as phrased

- Bengal farmers were forced in 1943 to grow cash crops instead of food.
- Bengal’s remaining grain was shipped to England as reserves.
- Churchill wrote “Why hasn’t Gandhi died yet?” as a marginal note on a famine report.
- An Australian famine-relief ship for Bengal was personally diverted by Churchill.
- The Famine Code itself compelled neighboring provinces to export grain.
- Bengal was no longer under British rule.

### Genuinely unknowable

- Churchill’s private intent beyond the prejudices and priorities the documents reveal.
- A precise number of deaths attributable to any one decision.
- The precise mortality counterfactual from a particular extra shipment.

## Fastest angle-killing test before expensive research

Acquire and place side by side:

1. War Cabinet conclusions/memoranda for **4 August and 24 September 1943**;
2. the associated Ministry of War Transport allocation table cited by Madhusree Mukerjee;
3. Government of India requests and Wavell/Amery telegrams in *Transfer of Power*, vol. IV;
4. actual sailing/arrival dates and tonnages.

If these show no materially feasible shipping option before peak mortality, a Churchill-centered withholding trial should be **REFRAMED** toward the Bengal–Delhi chain of failure. If they show relief lift rejected while comparable lower-priority civilian cargoes sailed, the Churchill/War Cabinet trial earns a **GO**. Without those files, the viral “diverted ships” package should be **KILLED**, even though the broader responsibility story remains viable.

## Current recommendation

**REFRAME, pending the Cabinet shipping test.** The strongest honest proposition is not “Did Churchill cause the Bengal famine?” It is:

> **Bengal Famine: Who Had the Power to Stop It—and Who Didn’t?**

That structure allows the documents to allocate responsibility sequentially: Bengal’s ministry, Herbert, Linlithgow/Delhi, then Churchill’s War Cabinet. It makes the “self-rule” claim the reveal, corrects viral falsehoods without exonerating empire, and preserves a real final trial over late overseas relief.
