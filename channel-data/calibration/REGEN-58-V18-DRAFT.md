### COLD OPEN (0:00–1:15) [VERBATIM]

Everyone knows Saladin. The Muslim sultan who took Jerusalem back from the Crusaders, the most famous Muslim hero of that whole war.

What not a lot of people know is that he wasn't an Arab, and he wasn't a Turk. He was a Kurd.

[SHOW: Ibn al-Athir, *al-Kamil*, p.176 card — "Rawadi Kurds in origin, which lineage is the noblest of the Kurds"]
[SOURCE: Ibn al-Athir 53233ac0 / Boris James d7a20122 / Sharafnama 4534fea8]

Ibn al-Athir, a near-contemporary Arab chronicler, recorded Saladin's family as Kurds — "the noblest of the Kurds," in his words.

Now jump forward eight hundred years, to another ruler obsessed with his own legacy: Saddam Hussein. In 1987, Saddam's regime held a conference in Tikrit titled "The Battle of Liberation — from Saladin to Saddam Hussein." The propaganda styled him Saladin the Second.

[SHOW: 1987 Tikrit conference reference card]
[SOURCE: HNN, web-verified C42 — conference title + "Saladin II" honorific confirmed; cult "stamps" detail CUT per flag, unverified]

There was just one detail the regime quietly left out: that Saladin was a Kurd. And the year after that conference, Saddam's army dropped poison gas on Kurdish towns and killed tens of thousands of the very people his hero came from.

[CAMERA: hold on host, slow]

So here's the thing you've probably heard about the Kurds — that they're the largest people on Earth without a country, that they were carved up at the end of the First World War, too divided to ever build a state of their own.

Almost none of that is what actually happened.

The Kurds didn't fail to build a state. They built dozens of them. And then they watched them taken apart, one by one — starting eighty years before anyone in Paris ever drew a line.

[SHOW: title card — "The Kurds Had Their Own States for 300 Years"]

### ACT 1 — THEY HAD IT (1:15–4:00)

#### The vanishing king [VERBATIM lead, GUIDE context]

[CAMERA: to map of northern Iraq / Soran region]

So let's get the facts straight, starting with a man who came closer to a Kurdish state than almost anyone — and then disappeared.

His name was Muhammad Kor, the ruler of Soran in the early 1800s. People called him the Blind Mir. He inherited a small mountain emirate and turned it into the largest Kurdish-ruled territory anyone had managed in centuries.

[SHOW: map — Soran's reach: Erbil, Koi Sanjaq, Amedi]
[SOURCE: C30 / C23]

And he ran it like a state. He cast his own cannons in his own workshops. He minted coins carrying his own name. And in the mosques, the Friday prayer was read in his name — instead of the Ottoman sultan's.

[GUIDE — decode the two sovereignty marks (don't over-explain; one beat)]
- Those last two — your own coin, your own name in the Friday prayer — were *the* two classic marks of being a sovereign Muslim ruler. A subject never got to do either. Muhammad Kor did both.
- Land it: that's not a tribal chief. That's a king running his own kingdom.

[SHOW: Fraser 1834 quote card — purse-of-gold law]
[SOURCE: Fraser via van Bruinessen 07657ff8 / 5a9ff854]

And the order he kept was its own kind of proof. A British traveller named James Fraser passed through in 1834, and recorded that robbery had been stamped out so completely that if a man saw a purse of gold lying in the road, he wouldn't touch it — he'd report it to the next village, and there it would sit, untouched, until the owner came to claim it.

<!-- TURN MOMENT — the evidence that breaks "they never had a state"; ~18% runtime -->
That's the part the popular story leaves out. Before any of the borders we argue about today existed, the Kurds were already running states of their own.

#### Not one tribe — a string of states [GUIDE + one VERBATIM card]

[GUIDE — Muhammad Kor wasn't unique; he was one of many. Build the "states, plural" case. Concede-first on the framing.]
- The deal that made this possible goes back to 1514. After the Ottomans beat the Persians, they cut a bargain with the Kurdish emirs: stay loyal in name, hold the frontier, and we leave you alone. That arrangement held, more or less unchanged, for more than three hundred years.
- The top-tier emirates had an actual legal status in Ottoman law. The empire filed them as *hükûmet* — a word that literally means "government."
- Decode plainly (in other words): no Ottoman tax, no Ottoman census, no troops stationed there, no inspectors. They appointed their own judges. The empire's writ stopped at the mountains.
- Concede honestly (Magnitude-Bounding / Framing B): I want to be careful here. This was never one united Kurdistan. These were separate states — Soran, Bohtan, Bitlis, the Baban, the Ardalan — and they fought each other as often as anyone else.

[SHOW: Evliya Çelebi card — Abdal Khan's Bitlis library "thousands of books… European books on geography, physics, astronomy and medicine"; 1,200 shops]
[SOURCE: Evliya Çelebi 0e066e9e / 5a9ff854 / 367ed08a]

But "separate" doesn't mean small. Take Bitlis. When the Ottoman traveller Evliya Çelebi visited in the 1650s, he found a court with a library of thousands of books — Persian, Arabic, even European books on geography and medicine — a city of twelve hundred shops, and a ruler who fielded twelve thousand cavalry.

[VERBATIM card-line]
[SHOW: McDowall reference — "vassal state rather than a province"]

That's not a tribe with a good year. That's a functioning state that happened to answer, on paper, to a sultan it mostly ignored.

[ACT-1 TAIL — forward-pointing into Act 2's subject]
So the real question isn't whether the Kurds could build states. They built plenty. The question is what happened to them. And what happened to them came from the empire they'd served for three centuries.

### ACT 2 — THE FIRST DISMANTLING (4:00–6:45)

#### The 1639 myth [VERBATIM-led myth-bust]

[CAMERA: to host]

Now, you'll often hear that the Kurds were first split apart in 1639, by a treaty between the Ottomans and Persia — three centuries before Sykes-Picot ever drew a line through them.

[GUIDE — Rule 3: negate the claim before the evidence. Concede the grain of truth, then bust it.]
- That treaty was real. The Treaty of Zuhab, 1639. But read what it actually says, and the famous "border" isn't there.

[SHOW: Ateş quote card — "did not lay down a precise line but rather designated entire regions… as 'frontier'"; "no exact line of demarcation"]
[SOURCE: Ateş efb3648c]

The historian Sabri Ateş, who studied that border in detail, found the treaty drew no line at all. It named a vague frontier zone and left it at that. The hard border we know today wasn't drawn in 1639 — it was drawn between 1843 and 1914, by Russia and Britain. The idea that one old treaty split the Kurds, Ateş says plainly, is "a myth."

[GUIDE — so what DID divide them? Bridge into the real mechanism.]
- So if 1639 didn't do it, what did? The answer is the opposite of what the myth says. For three hundred years the thing holding Kurdistan together wasn't a border — it was the emirates themselves. And in the span of about twenty years, the empire destroyed them.

#### The second conquest [VERBATIM lead + GUIDE]

In the 1830s, the Ottoman Empire started modernizing — pulling power back to the center, ending the old hands-off deals. And the self-governing Kurdish emirates were exactly the kind of deal they meant to end.

So they took them apart. Soran fell in 1836 — Muhammad Kor, the blind king from the start of this video, surrendered on a Quran sealed with a guarantee of safe conduct, sailed for Istanbul, and on the voyage home, he simply disappeared. Almost certainly murdered. The emirate died with him.

[SHOW: Bedirxan coin legend card — "one account records a coin inscribed 'Emir-i Bohtan Bedirhan'"]
[SOURCE: Klein via McDowall 367ed08a — ⚠️ single-source, present as "one account records"]

Then Bohtan, in 1847. Its ruler, Bedirxan, had gone further than anyone — he'd had the Friday prayer read in his name, and one account records a coin struck with his name on it: "Emir of Bohtan, Bedirxan."

[VERBATIM — the near-miss + the internal betrayal]
And Bedirxan did the one thing the Kurds almost never managed. He built a coalition. By the mid-1840s nearly every chief in the north had come to pledge to him. For a moment, there really was something like a unified Kurdish power.

It lasted until his own cousin defected to the Ottomans and guided their army straight in. Bedirxan was beaten in 1847.

[GUIDE — Rule 38 micro-concession; Klein's engineered-division refinement, C17. Avoid victim-blaming.]
- And that betrayal was the oldest weakness the Kurds had, showing up again: rival chiefs, rival tribes, each one reachable by an empire that knew exactly which one to buy.
- But here's the part that's easy to get backwards. That disunity wasn't just in the Kurdish character. A lot of it was engineered. The historian Janet Klein showed how the state deliberately turned one Kurdish group against another — arm this chief, and you've automatically made an enemy of his rival.
- Concede the honest balance (auditor's edge): the feuds were real — one Kurdish historian calls tribalism "the main barrier" to a national consciousness. But the empires didn't just find that division. They fed it.

[SHOW: Eppel card — destruction "wiped out any potential nuclei… that could have become a nucleus for Kurdish statehood after the First World War"]
[SOURCE: Eppel 367ed08a / Ateş "re-clanization" / van Bruinessen]

[VERBATIM verdict — Rule 19 artifact-led; this is the act's thesis landing]
And this is the part that matters most. When you destroy the states, you don't get a tidy modern province. You get a power vacuum. The historian David Eppel put it directly: wiping out the emirates wiped out the very nuclei that could have become a Kurdish state after the First World War.

[ACT-2 TAIL — forward into the WWI window]
So by the time the great powers finally sat down to carve up the Middle East, the Kurds didn't show up fractured by accident. They showed up fractured because someone had already taken apart everything that held them together — forty years before anyone in Paris drew a map.

### THE TWIST NOBODY MENTIONS (6:45–7:30) [VERBATIM]

And it gets stranger than that. Because when the First World War came, the Kurds didn't even fight against the empire that was about to abandon them.

They fought *for* it.

[SHOW: McDowall / Cambridge History card — "the greater part of the Ottoman forces in the region were Kurdish"]
[SOURCE: McDowall 5ab96a08 / Cambridge History 23945b11 / C22]

Most of the Ottoman forces in the region were Kurdish. Somewhere between three hundred thousand and eight hundred thousand Kurds died fighting in the Ottoman armies — for the sultan, against the Allies.

[GUIDE — why, plainly. Religion thread, C16. Don't sermonize.]
- And the reason is the thread running under this whole story. The Kurds were Muslims, and the sultan was the caliph — the leader of the faith. So when he called, most of them came.
- Land the irony flat (consequence-anchored): which means that when the war ended, the Kurds had to walk up to the victors and ask for a state — having spent four years dying for the empire those victors had just defeated.

### ACT 3 — THE WINDOW THAT CLOSED (7:30–10:45)

#### No seat at the table [GUIDE + VERBATIM humor beat]

[CAMERA: to Paris 1919 imagery]

So Paris, 1919. The map of the world is being redrawn. Arabs are getting states. Poles, Czechs, Serbs — getting states. And the Kurds send a representative.

[VERBATIM — the literal-absurd fact, deadpan]
[SHOW: McDowall card — "Parisian pamphleteer"]
[SOURCE: McDowall 5ab96a08 / MacMillan 68c36cea]

His name was Sherif Pasha, and he was a man who wrote essays in a café in Paris. A British official waved him away as a "Parisian pamphleteer." He had, in the official's view, no real constituency back home — nobody who'd actually agreed he could speak for them.

[GUIDE — and that's the whole problem, named. Comparative mechanism, C22. Magnitude-bound.]
- And that wasn't snobbery so much as a fact. The Arabs had Sharif Hussein of Mecca — a descendant of the Prophet, with an army that had actually revolted against the Ottomans and helped the British win. The Kurds had no single leader the way the Arabs did, because the men who could have been those leaders had been killed off in the 1840s.
- Lloyd George, the British prime minister, said he found it impossible to identify any one Kurd who represented more than his own clan. He even forgot to list Kurdistan among the Ottoman territories to be settled, and later admitted his geography had been "faulty."

[SHOW: MacMillan card — Lloyd George "found it impossible to discover any representative Kurd"]

#### Sèvres — the promise that was never real [VERBATIM, document-led]

Now, here's where the famous version of the story comes in. Because in 1920, the Kurds really were promised a state — in writing.

[SHOW: Treaty of Sèvres, "SECTION III. KURDISTAN" facsimile]
[SOURCE: Treaty text 7200a9c0 / a7887219 / Radpey 683369c8]

The Treaty of Sèvres. There's a whole section, headed "Kurdistan." It laid out local autonomy, and then, within a year, a path to full independence.

[GUIDE — but read the fine print. Rule 40 baseline-then-exception. Two halves of the catch.]
- But read the fine print, and that promise was hollow from the start. It was conditional: the Kurds would only get independence if the League of Nations decided they were "capable" of it — a test the great powers applied only to people they considered, in their own words, civilized.
- And the bigger problem: Sèvres was signed by an Ottoman government that was already finished. Mustafa Kemal Atatürk's nationalists were winning the war on the ground, and they rejected the treaty completely.

[VERBATIM — the verdict, scoped]
[SHOW: McDowall card — "void before the ink was dry"]

So Sèvres was never a real promise that got betrayed. It was stillborn. As David McDowall puts it, it was void before the ink was dry. The place it actually died was a different treaty, three years later.

#### Lausanne — the erasure [VERBATIM-heavy, the keystone, document-led]

[CAMERA: tight, deliberate]

1923. Lausanne, Switzerland. The Allies and the new Turkey sit down to write the treaty that's still the legal foundation of Turkey's borders today. And this time, the Kurds get nothing.

[GUIDE — but HOW they got nothing is the point. The religion clause. Set up İnönü's move.]
- And the way it happened is the most revealing part of the whole story. It came down to one word: minority.
- The treaty protected minorities — it guaranteed them their own schools, the right to keep their own language. So the question was simply whether the Kurds counted as a minority.

[SHOW: İnönü Lausanne Proceedings card, p.1018/1023 — "Kurds and Turks form a single unit"]
[SOURCE: Lausanne Proceedings 7d37c3f5 / Tusan e2ef8ae7]

Turkey's negotiator, İsmet İnönü, had a counter-move. He stood up and insisted that the Kurds and the Turks were one and the same people. One nation. And if the Kurds weren't a separate people, then they weren't a minority — and there was nothing to protect.

[VERBATIM — Curzon, the concede-and-pivot; Britain argued FOR the Kurds and lost. Auditor's edge.]
[SHOW: Curzon Lausanne Proceedings card, p.1045/1047 — "the Kurds… ought to be an autonomous race"]
[SOURCE: Lausanne Proceedings 7d37c3f5 / McDowall 5ab96a08]

And to be fair to Britain — Lord Curzon, leading the British side, actually argued against this. He told the room the Kurds were a people apart, with their own history and customs, who ought to be an autonomous race. He was right. And he lost.

[GUIDE — why Britain folded. Mosul. The chain, not the smoking gun (C9 + C27 honesty guard).]
- Because there was something Britain wanted more than Kurdish autonomy: the province of Mosul. Oil-rich, and claimed by both Turkey and British-run Iraq. And Mosul was overwhelmingly Kurdish.
- So Britain made a strategic bargain. Drop the Kurds, keep Mosul. A British official actually proposed telling Turkey, in writing, that Britain had "abandoned the idea of Kurdish autonomy." Three years later, Turkey signed away its claim to Mosul — in exchange for ten percent of the province's oil royalties for twenty-five years.

[SHOW: Sluglett card — Dobbs cable, "abandoned the idea of Kurdish autonomy" + 10% royalties]
[SOURCE: Sluglett 11c2d1ec / Pedersen 5ed5ec15]

<!-- SMOKING GUN — ~78% runtime, the artifact-led climax (Rule 19, Rule 47C statistic-as-payoff) -->
[VERBATIM]
[SHOW: Treaty of Lausanne full text — search highlight returns zero results for "Kurd"]
[SOURCE: Lausanne treaty text 89088552 / 7200a9c0 — confirmed Radpey + McDowall]

And here's the thing you can check yourself. In the entire Treaty of Lausanne — the document that fixed these borders, the one that's still in force today — the word "Kurd" does not appear a single time. Not once. The protected minorities are listed by one test only: whether they're Muslim or not. The Kurds were Muslim. So in the eyes of the treaty, they didn't exist.

That's not history happening by accident. That's a choice, written down.

[ACT-3 TAIL — forward into the pattern]
And it set the pattern for everything that came after. Because over the next century, the same thing happened again and again — the Kurds would be useful to somebody, right up until the moment they weren't.

### ACT 4 — THE PATTERN ON REPEAT (10:45–12:30)

[GUIDE — the armed-and-discarded pattern, flowing enumeration, each member with its concrete move (VOICE-PROFILE: the universal must fit each one). Mahabad first.]
- In 1946, the Kurds did finally get a state — a real one, the Republic of Mahabad, in Iran. It lasted eleven months. The Soviets had backed it as a bargaining chip for an oil concession, and the moment they got their deal, they pulled out. The tribes who'd backed it deserted. Its president, Qazi Muhammad, chose to stay and was hanged in the same square where he'd declared the republic.

#### Armed to bleed, not to win [VERBATIM, document-led — Pike Report]

[CAMERA: to host]

But the clearest case came in 1975. The United States and the Shah of Iran were arming Kurdish fighters in Iraq. And years later, a US congressional committee — the Pike Committee — investigated exactly what that operation had been for.

[SHOW: Pike Committee Report card — "Neither the foreign Head of State nor the President and Dr. Kissinger desired victory for our clients."]
[SOURCE: Pike Report via Vanly 75e966ef — VERBATIM confirmed C39/C41]

Read what the committee actually concluded. Neither the Shah, nor President Ford, nor Henry Kissinger — and I'm quoting — "desired victory for our clients," meaning the Kurds. They wanted them fighting just hard enough to drain Iraq, and no harder.

[VERBATIM — decode, plainly, what it means]
In other words, the people handing the Kurds guns did not want them to win. They wanted them bleeding. And when the Shah cut his own deal with Iraq that same year, the weapons stopped overnight, and the Kurds were crushed.

[SHOW: Kissinger card — "covert action should not be confused with missionary work"]
[SOURCE: Gibson 01a62b01 — quote only; betrayal framing on McDowall + Pike]

Kissinger's only defense, when the committee pressed him, was that "covert action should not be confused with missionary work" — about an operation that had just left his allies to be destroyed.

#### Anfal [VERBATIM — atrocity, plain fact + attributed culpability, no sensory dwelling]

And then the worst of it. In 1988, as the Iran-Iraq war ground to an end, Saddam Hussein set out to punish the Kurds for backing Iran during it. The campaign was called Anfal.

[SHOW: Anfal figures card — ~4,000 villages destroyed; ~100,000+ killed]
[SOURCE: McDowall 5ab96a08 / C35]

His army destroyed around four thousand Kurdish villages and killed well over a hundred thousand people. At Halabja, in a single day, poison gas killed roughly five thousand.

[GUIDE — Western complicity, attributed and bounded (C35/C39 single-source guard). Named who and why.]
- And the world mostly stayed quiet, because Saddam was the West's man against Iran. A British minister doubled export credit to Iraq the year after the gassing. And Britain and West Germany had supplied the materials to make the gas in the first place — in Britain's case, one historian writes, "knowingly."

[SHOW: McDowall card — "done knowingly" (Britain, precursor materials) — ⚠️ single-source]

#### The cat's paw [VERBATIM — synthesis, cold start, no runway (Rule 13.3 Meta-Framing avoided), thesis felt not stated]

[CAMERA: tight on host]

So look at what these all have in common. The Ottomans armed the Kurds to hold the Persian frontier. The British traded them away for Mosul's oil. Baghdad promised them self-rule, then gassed them when they turned to Iran. Different masters, every time — and the same move.

[SHOW: McDowall synthesis card, p.2 — Kurds "useful as a cat's paw"]
[SOURCE: McDowall 5ab96a08, p.2 — VERBATIM confirmed C42]

A Kurdish people with no state of their own was useful. You could arm them, point them at a rival, and walk away the day it suited you. Give them a real country, and you lose all that — now they have their own army, their own borders, their own agenda. So nobody around them ever actually wanted them to have one. They were, as McDowall writes, useful as a "cat's paw" — and the way to keep a cat's paw useful is to keep it stateless.

### THE RHYME (12:30–13:00) [VERBATIM close — chronological, Rule 23]

And it isn't only history.

[CAMERA: to host, plain]
[SHOW: Syria 2026 — Decree No. 13 reference + SDF/Damascus integration map]
[SOURCE: MEC 40d1c0e6 / Al Jazeera 30 Jan 2026 / C40 — web-verified]

In January 2026, in Syria, the last Kurdish self-rule was dismantled. The new government in Damascus took back the territory the Kurds had held, and the oil fields went to the state. And the Kurds were handed a consolation prize: a decree — Decree Number Thirteen — granting citizenship to stateless Kurds, recognizing their language, making their new year a holiday.

[VERBATIM — the rhyme, stated flat, distinct-processes honesty]
Rights from above. The oil to the capital. A language law instead of a state. A hundred years apart, the same shape as Lausanne — the deal made over their heads, by outside powers.

[SHOW: McDowall card — "nations are built in the imagination before they are built on the ground"]
[SOURCE: McDowall 5ab96a08 / C36]

The historian David McDowall has a line about the Kurds that stays with me. He writes that "nations are built in the imagination before they are built on the ground."

[VERBATIM — thesis spoken ONCE, in full, for the first time (Rule 36 v18)]
The Kurds built theirs in the imagination centuries ago. They built it on the ground, too — real states, real coins, real laws. And every empire around them needed them divided and stateless to stay useful, so every time they built one, somebody had a reason to take it apart.
