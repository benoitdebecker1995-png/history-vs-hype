## The Writing Style You're Learning

Based on the authors you admire (Harari, Diamond, Bryson, Pinker, Dawkins, Mukherjee, Frankopan, Graeber, Rosling), here are the 7 techniques they ALL share. This is what makes writing feel human, not AI slop:

### 1. DETONATING ANECDOTE (Opening)
Never open with your thesis. Open with a person, a place, or a provocation.
- Diamond: Yali's question on a beach in 1972
- Graeber: A cocktail-party argument at Westminster Abbey
- Rosling: "You scored worse than chimps"
- YOUR Tordesillas: "In 2023, a group of economists drew a line through Brazil..."

### 2. ONE REFRAMING LENS (Structure)
Teach readers a single conceptual tool early, then apply it to everything.
- Harari: "imagined realities"
- Dawkins: "selfish genes"
- Rosling: cognitive instincts
- YOUR approach: "the document says something different than the narrative"

### 3. EVIDENCE AS NARRATIVE (Citations)
Don't present evidence like a bibliography. Evidence arrives as story, as surprise.
- BAD: "According to Harris (2009), Roman literacy was 10-15%."
- GOOD: "William V. Harris spent a career counting who could read in ancient Rome. His number? Between 10 and 15 percent. That's the baseline everyone forgets."

### 4. SENTENCE RHYTHM VARIATION (Prose)
Build with longer sentences. Land with short ones. The short sentence carries the weight.
- "They have come a long way, those replicators. Now they go by the name of genes, and we are their survival machines." (Dawkins — the last sentence is the one you remember)

### 5. STRONG OPINIONS, HELD OPENLY (Voice)
Take a position. Don't hedge everything. The "Calm Prosecutor" has a verdict.
- AI slop: "Some historians argue that the Dark Ages were not entirely dark..."
- YOU: "Both sides are wrong. The evidence shows something more complicated — and more interesting."

### 6. THE FAMILIAR MADE STRANGE (Hook)
Take something readers think they understand. Show them it's weirder than they thought.
- "You think the Treaty of Tordesillas was about land? It was about wind."

### 7. PERSONAL VULNERABILITY (Anti-Slop)
Admit what surprised you, what you got wrong initially, what you're uncertain about.
- "I assumed the French imposed this on Haiti. Then I read Boyer's actual letters. He helped them do it."
- No AI can be vulnerable because it has no self. This is your unfakeable ingredient.

## MASTER PROMPTS

### PROMPT 1: SCRIPT-TO-ARTICLE CONVERSION (The Main Pipeline Prompt)

Use this when converting an existing video script into a newsletter article. Paste the full script after the prompt. This is your primary production tool — use it for every script conversion.

```
You are helping me convert a YouTube video script into a newsletter article for my Substack "History vs Hype." I am learning to write articles — I have no article-writing experience but I've written 47 video scripts.

CONTEXT ABOUT MY NEWSLETTER:
- Free to read — I believe research should be accessible
- Audience: educated adults (25-44) who want depth, not clickbait
- Competitive edge: real quotes from academic sources with page numbers, translated primary documents in 6 languages (French, German, Latin, Greek, Spanish, Dutch)
- Published in English (Dutch editions later)
- Voice: "Calm Prosecutor" — emotionally low, intellectually high, evidence-based

MY VOICE MODEL (from studying 9 authors):
I write in the tradition of these authors. Apply their specific techniques:

1. OPENINGS — Use a "detonating anecdote" (Graeber: casual moment that explodes into civilizational argument, Mukherjee: one real named person on the day everything changed, Rosling: confront the reader with data that proves them wrong, Bryson: direct existential address). NEVER open with a thesis statement.

2. EVIDENCE — Present evidence as NARRATIVE, not citation (Bryson: tell the story of the discovery, not just the finding. Mukherjee: embed data in human stories. Graeber: use anthropological counterexamples). The reader should never feel like they're reading a bibliography. Evidence arrives as story, as surprise, as "here's something you didn't know."
   - BAD: "According to Harris (2009), Roman literacy was 10-15%."
   - GOOD: "William V. Harris spent a career counting who could read in ancient Rome. His number? Between 10 and 15 percent. That's the baseline everyone forgets."

3. REFRAME — Every article needs ONE "I never thought about it that way" moment (Harari: invert what seems natural. Diamond: "invention is the mother of necessity, not the other way around." Frankopan: "the real center of the world was not the Mediterranean"). State the assumption. Invert it. Land the verdict. 2-4 sentences.

4. SENTENCE RHYTHM — Build with longer sentences (evidence, context, nuance). Land with SHORT ones under 8 words (verdict, insight, reframe). The short sentence carries the weight. Never write 5 sentences of the same length in a row.
   - Model: "They did what humans have done for centuries... They left." (Pinker/Wilkerson)
   - Model: "They are inventing a myth." (Graeber — the prosecutor's verdict)
   - Model: "All life is one." (Bryson — reducing 3.8 billion years to four words)

5. STRONG POSITIONS — I take positions backed by evidence. I don't hedge everything. The Calm Prosecutor has a verdict. (Harari: "The Agricultural Revolution was history's biggest fraud." Graeber: "Our standard account of monetary history is precisely backwards.")

6. REAL NAMES ONLY — If the script mentions a specific person, KEEP THEM. If you need to add a human element, suggest where I should research a REAL named individual (clerk, cartographer, translator, local official). NEVER invent fictional characters or scenes. If no real name exists, the data alone is enough.

7. PERSONAL VULNERABILITY — Flag 2-3 spots where I should insert what surprised ME during research, what I assumed wrong initially, or what remains genuinely uncertain. These are the anti-AI ingredients. Mark them as [PERSONAL: suggest what to add here].

CONVERSION RULES:
1. Strip ALL video-specific elements: [B-ROLL], [TALKING HEAD], timestamps, {retention triggers}, production notes, // comments
2. Keep EVERY quote, citation, page number, and primary source reference — these are the value proposition
3. Rewrite spoken-delivery phrasing into written-delivery phrasing (but keep contractions and conversational tone — "it's" not "it is", "don't" not "do not")
4. Add subheadings where act breaks were (readers scan articles differently than they watch videos)
5. Where the script said "here on screen" or "as you can see" — embed the quote as a blockquote or describe what the document shows
6. Keep article between 1,500-3,000 words
7. End with a synthesis that connects back to the opening — the "why this matters" payoff
8. Use blockquotes (>) for primary source quotes displayed on screen — these are the visual B-roll equivalent in article format

DO NOT:
- Add generic transitions ("In this article, we will explore...", "Let's dive into...", "It is worth noting that...")
- Summarize what you're about to say before saying it
- Hedge every claim ("some historians argue...", "it could be argued that...")
- Add emoji, bullet-point listicles, or "5 things you didn't know" formatting
- Make it sound like a Wikipedia article or a textbook
- Add any claims, quotes, or facts that weren't in the original script
- Use these AI-slop phrases: "In the tapestry of history", "This raises important questions", "It is important to note", "throughout history", "the annals of", "a complex interplay of"
- Invent scenes, characters, or sensory details that aren't sourced

OUTPUT FORMAT:
1. The converted article (ready to paste into Substack)
2. [PERSONAL] markers where I should add my own voice
3. [RESEARCH NAME] markers where finding a real named individual would strengthen the passage
4. 5 subject line options scored by curiosity gap strength (1-10)
5. A "slop check" — flag any paragraph that still sounds AI-generated so I can rewrite it

Here is the script to convert:

[PASTE SCRIPT HERE]
```

### PROMPT 5: NOTEBOOKLM RESEARCH SESSION (For New Articles)

Use this when starting research on a topic you haven't scripted before. Paste into NotebookLM as custom instructions after uploading your 15-20 academic sources.

```
I am researching [TOPIC] for a newsletter article on "History vs Hype." I need to build a verified research document with real quotes and page numbers.

MY ARTICLE APPROACH:
- Every claim must have a specific source with page number
- I use real quotes, not summaries ("According to X in [Book], page Y: '[exact quote]'")
- I need one counterintuitive "reframe" — something my reader assumes that the evidence contradicts
- I need a modern data payoff — how is this historical event still measurable today?
- I need real named individuals at the edges of the big events — not just kings and generals, but clerks, translators, cartographers, local officials, civilians

PLEASE HELP ME FIND:

1. THE REFRAME: What is the most common popular belief about this topic, and where do these sources contradict it? Give me the specific quote with page number.

2. NAMED INDIVIDUALS: Who are the specific named people in these sources who were present at or directly affected by these events — NOT the famous figures everyone knows, but the people in the footnotes? Clerks who filed documents, translators who chose specific words, local officials who implemented orders, civilians who recorded what happened. Give me names and page numbers.

3. PRIMARY SOURCE QUOTES: Find 5-10 direct quotes from primary documents cited in these sources. I want the original language if possible, with the author's English translation. Page numbers required.

4. CONTESTED CLAIMS: Where do these sources disagree with each other? What's genuinely debated among scholars? I want to acknowledge what the opposing side gets right (intellectual honesty).

5. MODERN DATA: Do any of these sources connect the historical events to measurable modern consequences? (Census data, economic studies, legal precedents still in force, borders still contested)

6. TIMELINE: Give me a precise chronology with dates. I need "On June 7th, 1494" not "in the late 15th century."

7. SURPRISE: What's the single most surprising thing in these sources that even a well-read person probably doesn't know?

Format each finding as:
- CLAIM: [statement]
- SOURCE: [Author, Book, Page]
- QUOTE: "[exact text]"
- STATUS: Verified by [X] sources / Contested by [Author] / Single source only
```
