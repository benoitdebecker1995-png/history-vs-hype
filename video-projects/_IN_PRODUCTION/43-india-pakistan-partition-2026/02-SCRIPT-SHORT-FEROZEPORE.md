## HOOK (0:00–1:00)

[VISUAL: Map of Punjab showing Ferozepore district highlighted]

On August 8th, 1947, a draft map was sent from the Viceroy's office in New Delhi to the Governor of Punjab. It showed the new border between India and Pakistan. Ferozepore — a district that was 55% Muslim, containing the canal headworks that controlled irrigation for millions of acres of Pakistani farmland — was on the Pakistani side.

[DIRECTOR'S NOTE: Sound design — typewriter key strike on "Eliminate salient." Single percussive hit, then silence.]

Three days later, a single coded message arrived. Two words. "Eliminate salient." In military cartography, a salient is a bulge — a piece of territory that juts into enemy lines. Ferozepore, on the draft map, stuck out into India like a thumb. The message meant: cut it off. Flatten the border. Give it to India.

[B-ROLL: Text on screen — "eliminate salient" — typed letter by letter]

And with Ferozepore went the valve controlling Pakistan's water supply.

No census data changed. No infrastructure moved. No new evidence was submitted. The man who drew the border — a British chancery lawyer who had never been east of Gibraltar, who was simultaneously drawing a second border for Bengal from a desk in Simla, using maps he later admitted were incorrect — burned every working paper and left India forever.

So I read the actual documents. The Radcliffe Award. The Boundary Commission proceedings. The Transfer of Power files from the British National Archives. And what they show is a three-day window where the most consequential border in modern South Asian history was redrawn — and nobody can prove why.

## THE SETUP — WHO WAS RADCLIFFE? (1:00–2:50)

[VISUAL: Portrait of Radcliffe]

Here's who they chose to draw this line.

Cyril Radcliffe. Forty-eight years old. A chancery lawyer — his career was trusts, estates, corporate structures. His wartime job was Director-General of the Ministry of Information. Government censorship. He'd never adjudicated a border dispute. He'd never studied demographics. He'd never set foot in Asia.

According to Stanley Wolpert in *Jinnah of Pakistan*, published by Oxford University Press:

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen]

"Radcliffe, who had never even visited India and expressed no known opinions on its problems, was unanimously accepted and would soon decide the destiny of millions of Hindus, Sikhs, and Muslims."

That was the qualification. Total ignorance. A man with no attachments, no opinions, no stake — supposedly impartial precisely because he knew nothing.

He arrived on July 8th. He had until mid-August. Thirty-six days to draw a border for 400 million people.

And here's the part that rarely gets mentioned. He wasn't drawing one border. He was chairing two boundary commissions simultaneously — Punjab and Bengal. He couldn't physically attend the Punjab public hearings in Lahore because he was processing Bengal paperwork from Delhi. As Yasmin Khan writes in *The Great Partition*, the border was "devised from a distance; the land, villages and communities to be divided were not visited or inspected by the imperial map-maker."

[DIRECTOR'S NOTE: Cut to map animation — pull out from Radcliffe's desk in Simla to reveal the 1,200-mile border below. Scale contrast: one desk, one continent.]

He later told the journalist Kuldip Nayar: "Even district maps were not available and the ones there were, were incorrect."

His instructions — the official Terms of Reference — said this:

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen — Terms of Reference]

"The Boundary Commission is instructed to demarcate the boundaries on the basis of ascertaining the contiguous majority areas of Muslims and non-Muslims. In doing so, it will also take into account other factors."

"Other factors." Never defined. Could mean railways, canals, economic viability, anything. Nehru was asked to clarify. He refused.

So the mandate was: follow the demographics, unless you decide not to. And the demographic data was from the 1941 census — six years out of date.

## THE DRAFT MAP — AUGUST 8TH (2:50–4:30)

[VISUAL: Map of Ferozepore district showing tehsil boundaries with Muslim population percentages]

Radcliffe chaired a four-member commission. Two judges nominated by the Muslim League. Two nominated jointly by Congress and the Sikh representatives — Justice Mehr Chand Mahajan and Justice Teja Singh. The Sikhs didn't get their own seats. They shared Congress's side of the table. They deadlocked on every issue. Muslim League judges voted one way, Congress-Sikh judges voted the other. Two against two, every time. So the Indian Independence Act was amended so that "award" meant the decision of the Chairman alone. One man making all the calls.

Patrick French — in *Liberty or Death* — documents what happened on August 8th:

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen]

"On 8 August, George Abell sent a rough map featuring the Ferozepur salient to the private office of Sir Evan Jenkins. An accompanying letter signed by Abell said: 'There will not be any great changes from this boundary.'"

The "salient" — that bulge of Muslim-majority territory jutting into India's side of the map. On this draft, it belonged to Pakistan.

Abell was Lord Mountbatten's Private Secretary. Jenkins was the Governor of Punjab. He'd been begging for advance information so he could deploy police before the border was announced.

Now here's why the August 8th map made geographic sense.

[VISUAL: Tehsil map — Muslim % overlay, zoomed to Ferozepore and Zira]

Punjab was divided into districts, and each district was divided into tehsils — administrative subdivisions, roughly the size of a large county. And the tehsil was the unit that mattered, because that's the level where you could actually count who lived where.

Ferozepore tehsil: 55.25% Muslim. Zira — the tehsil directly south of it: 65.26% Muslim. Two-thirds of Zira's population was Muslim. The 1941 census said these were contiguous Muslim-majority areas. The demographic mandate pointed clearly to Pakistan.

But these tehsils weren't just farmland. They contained the headworks of the Sutlej Valley canal system — the physical infrastructure that controlled water flow to millions of acres of irrigated land downstream. Land that, after partition, would be in Pakistan.

[VISUAL: Canal map — headworks at Ferozepore, canals flowing southwest into what becomes Pakistani Punjab]

Think of it as a valve. Whoever held Ferozepore held the tap. And the August 8th draft gave the tap to the same country that needed the water.

The letter said: "There will not be any great changes."

## THE REVERSAL — AUGUST 10-11TH (4:30–6:15)

[Beat]

Then something changed.

Two or three days later, Jenkins received an encrypted phone call from the Viceroy's House — sent over a Secraphone, a scrambled military telephone line the British used for classified communications. French again:

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen]

"Jenkins received a Secraphone message from Viceroy's House telling him to 'eliminate salient', meaning that the whole of Ferozepur district would now remain in India."

[DIRECTOR'S NOTE: Map animation — the border line redraws in real time. The Ferozepore salient flips from Pakistan's side to India's side. Hold for 3 seconds in silence.]

The Zira tehsil — that subdivision south of Ferozepore city. 65% Muslim. 48,000 Muslim residents according to the 1941 census. Now Indian — erased from Pakistan's side by a single phone call. And with them, the canal headworks. The valve shut. Within months, India would unilaterally cut off water to Pakistani canals, triggering an irrigation crisis that took twelve years and a World Bank treaty to resolve.

What happened between August 8th and August 10th?

### The August 9th Meeting

On August 9th, Radcliffe met Lord Ismay — Mountbatten's Chief of Staff — for an evening drink at the Viceregal estate. The meeting is documented. But as Khan writes:

"There is no contemporary record of it and the recollections of the three participants differ."

Years later, Christopher Beaumont — Radcliffe's own private secretary — gave testimony. In 1989 and again in 1992, Beaumont stated that:

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen]

"Mountbatten, allegedly under pressure from Nehru, persuaded Radcliffe to change the Punjab borderline in India's favour, so that Ferozepur tehsil was awarded to India instead of Pakistan."

And there's more. John Christie — Mountbatten's joint private secretary — kept a diary. His entry for August 9th says Mountbatten was in a "tired flap" and had to be "strenuously dissuaded from asking Radcliffe to alter his award."

Strenuously dissuaded. Meaning someone had to talk him out of it. Which suggests he was trying to do it.

## THE OTHER SIDE (6:15–7:45)

[Beat]

If we're being fair, the evidence against intervention is real.

Mountbatten maintained that he and Radcliffe were "mutually most careful never to have any discussions about the boundaries."

Radcliffe himself called his award "the result of his own unfettered judgement." In 1948, he addressed the change directly:

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen]

"Earlier drafts — and there were quite a few — were drafts and no more."

And the "other factors" clause does provide a legitimate rationale. In the final award, Radcliffe wrote:

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen]

"I have hesitated long over those not inconsiderable areas east of the Sutlej River in which Muslim majorities are found. I have come to the conclusion that there are factors such as the disruption of railway communications and water systems that ought in this instance to displace the primary claims of contiguous majorities."

Canal headworks. Railway networks. These are real infrastructure concerns. And there's an argument that keeping the headworks and the downstream canals under one government — India's — prevented a worse fragmentation of the water system.

So you could read this two ways. Either Mountbatten pressured Radcliffe to change the line — and Radcliffe retrospectively justified it with infrastructure arguments. Or Radcliffe genuinely reconsidered his draft — as he said he often did — and reached a different conclusion on legitimate grounds.

[Beat]

And we can't settle it. Because of what happened next.

## THE DESTRUCTION (7:45–8:30)

[VISUAL: Radcliffe Award document]

Radcliffe signed the completed award on August 12th. He submitted it to the Viceroy's office on August 13th.

Pakistan celebrated independence on August 14th. India on August 15th.

The award was published on August 17th. Two days after both celebrations. People in borderland villages didn't know which country they lived in until two days after their countries already existed.

And then Radcliffe destroyed everything. Aloys Arthur Michel, writing in *The Indus Rivers* — published by Yale University Press — confirms:

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen]

"Sir Cyril's personal notes, memoranda, and drafts were all destroyed at that time."

Every working paper. Every draft map. Every calculation that might explain why a district where 65% of the population was Muslim switched sides in three days. Gone.

He refused his salary — £5,000 plus £2,000 expenses. He never returned to the subcontinent. Months before his death in 1977, someone asked if he'd ever go back. His answer:

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen]

"God forbid. Not even if they asked me. I suspect they'd shoot me out of hand — both sides."

## WHY IT STILL MATTERS (8:30–9:15)

[VISUAL: Map showing Gurdaspur corridor to Kashmir]

Now, Ferozepore gets the most attention. But the bigger consequence may be Gurdaspur — another Muslim-majority district Radcliffe awarded to India. Gurdaspur contained the Pathankot road. The only non-mountainous land corridor from India to Kashmir.

Michel is direct:

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen]

"Had Radcliffe awarded Gurdaspur to Pakistan, there would have been no land communication between India and Jammu-Kashmir."

That road didn't start the Kashmir conflict. But it enabled India to sustain a military presence there. And Kashmir has been the cause of wars in 1947, 1965, 1971, and 1999.

In 1965, eighteen years after drawing the line, Radcliffe said:

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen]

"I wouldn't dream of defending some of the decisions in detail I made at the time. I was not aware of Kashmir thing at all."

He says he wasn't even thinking about Kashmir. If that's true, the road corridor was an accident. If it's not, it was the most consequential geographic decision in modern South Asian history.

---

## CLOSE (9:15–9:45)

[Beat]

The scholarly consensus — from Khan, from Chatterji, from French — is that this wasn't a grand conspiracy. It was what Khan calls a "shoddy implementation" of a "wafer-thin plan." Exhaustion, haste, and a desire to escape liability.

But here's what bothers me. We'll never know for certain what happened on August 9th, 1947. Because the only man who could have settled it burned the evidence and took the answer to his grave.

Those are the documents. What you conclude from them is up to you.
