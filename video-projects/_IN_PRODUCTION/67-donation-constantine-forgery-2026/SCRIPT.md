## ACT 1

You've probably heard that the Donation of Constantine was a forgery. What's talked about a lot less is how long people went on defending it, and what they had to change to keep doing it.

The document says that Constantine, the Roman emperor who converted to Christianity, handed land and political authority to the pope. As it circulated by the fifteenth century, it claimed Rome, all Italy and the western provinces.

And in the summer of 1440, a scholar named Lorenzo Valla decided to test it.

He started with the words. The charter calls Roman officials *satrapibus*, satraps, and that wasn't an office in Constantine's administration. It uses *frygium* for the pope's ceremonial headwear, a term Valla argued didn't belong in the Latin of Constantine's time. The document claimed to come from the fourth century, but its vocabulary came from a later world.

The second test wasn't Valla's at all. Seven years earlier, Nicholas of Cusa had asked why the popes needed the Frankish kings Pepin and later Charlemagne to reconquer territory for them. If Constantine had already handed them the West, why did they need kings to win it back?

Then Valla turned to coins. Whoever ruled Rome would have minted their own, and he could point to plenty struck under Constantine, several of which he owned himself. So he asked where the papal ones were.

> "An infinite number of such would be found for the popes, if you had ever ruled Rome. None are found — not gold, not silver, nor is any recorded as seen by anyone."

[CL] And this wasn't a document nobody used. Popes had been citing it since the eleventh century, and it had worked its way into collections of Church law. By the time Valla read it, people had been leaning on it for about four hundred years.

The words, the record and the coins all pointed the same way. So what did the people defending the charter do?

## ACT 2

[CL] The first answer was a different version of the document.

In 1447, seven years after Valla wrote, a Venetian humanist named Lauro Quirini sent Pope Nicholas V a Latin translation of what he presented as an earlier Greek version. This version didn't contain the Latin mistakes Valla had pointed to, so it looked like it preserved an earlier form of the Donation, perhaps one closer to the original.

Where the version Valla criticised has *satrapibus*, Quirini's translation has *provinciarum prefecti*, meaning provincial governors. The clause about putting an imperial strap around the pope's neck is gone, and so is the description of the footwear.

Every one of those was a place Valla had used to date the language.

Quirini never says he changed them because of Valla, so we have to be careful about claiming intention. But the historian Wolfram Setz went and compared Quirini's translation against its Greek source. He found that Quirini normally followed it closely, and that the departures land exactly where Valla's criticism would have hurt most. Setz is careful about what that proves. He says the conclusion only "seems permitted," but that Quirini appears to have made these choices "to take away the basis of Valla's criticism."

[CL] So the Greek version looked like a second, independent witness, except that its history was circular. The text had travelled from the medieval Latin tradition into Greek, then back into Latin through Quirini. Instead of two witnesses confirming each other, we have one tradition making a round trip and coming back without the words Valla had attacked.

[CL] And that Greek tradition didn't go away. A century later Agostino Steuco, by then the Vatican librarian, answered Valla directly, and he built his defence on the same family of texts. Seventeen years before that, in a different book, he had already printed the Greek and discussed where his copy came from.

It carried a colophon, which is a note recording who copied a manuscript and when. It identified a copy made for a Roman cardinal during the reign of Innocent the Third, and the date corresponds to December 1206.

Steuco knew the copy was medieval. He explained its provenance, printed the colophon, and worked out the date himself, and his reasoning was sound: the date of a surviving copy doesn't tell you when the underlying text was written.

His method was fine, but his family tree ran backwards. He believed the Greek preserved an older version of the Donation, when the Greek had actually descended from the medieval Latin.

## ACT 3

[CL] Before Steuco answered Valla, a lawyer had already taken a different route, one that didn't depend on saving the text.

Giovanni Antonio Sangiorgi taught canon law, the Church's own legal system, at Pavia, and he later became a cardinal. His argument appears in his commentary on Gratian's *Decretum*, the great medieval collection of Church law, and he dedicated that commentary to Pope Alexander VI. It was the most influential answer to come from the canon lawyers.

Sangiorgi's argument turned on a single idea: *restitutio*, restitution, the return of property to its original owner. In his words, Constantine was acting "to release what had been wrongly occupied by him and his predecessors."

Legally, this is serious. Showing a document is forged means the document can't prove ownership, but it doesn't tell you who owned the land.

[CL] And the answer became something later writers could simply point at instead of making the argument themselves. One of the men who cited it was also the man whose Greek translation became a mainstay of the later debate. By 1539 another writer was reprinting that translation and repeating Sangiorgi's argument in the same book. The legal line and the Greek line were now travelling together.

## ACT 4

[CL] Sangiorgi's restitution argument allowed the ownership claim to survive a forged charter. It still left Valla's evidence unanswered, so he dealt with that separately.

Valla had pointed to the silence in the historical record. Sangiorgi said the later emperors suppressed the story, and that they saw to it "that these things were concealed and abolished, and no mention made of them in the histories."

Valla used Eusebius against the Donation. Sangiorgi said enemies of the Church had altered his text, and that it wouldn't be surprising if "rivals of the Roman Church had corrupted and debased Eusebius's book on this point."

Then there was the language. Valla had argued that the bad Latin showed a later period.

Sangiorgi's answer: "If the Roman Church had wanted to fake this donation, it would have composed it all more elegantly and with greater ceremony."

In each case Sangiorgi accepts the evidence, then gives it a new meaning. The histories are silent because the story was suppressed. Eusebius contradicts it because his work was corrupted. The Latin is bad because a forgery written in Rome would have been better.

[CL] Valla's coin argument could have been killed by a single find. If somebody had turned up a papal coin from those centuries, he was finished, and he'd said as much himself when he compared the two jobs:

> "We tell counterfeit coins apart, separate them out, throw them away — and shall we not tell counterfeit doctrine apart?"

[CL] The thing Valla used to prove the Donation was fake became the thing Sangiorgi used to prove it was real.

## ACT 5

[CL] Sangiorgi now had an answer for the silence, Eusebius and the bad Latin. Why work this hard to keep the Donation standing? In Bolognini's case, we can answer that.

In 1491, a law professor named Lodovico Bolognini published a commentary on the founding charter of the University of Bologna, where he had studied and where he taught.

The charter presented itself as an imperial document issued by Theodosius in 423. Modern editors date it to between 1226 and 1234. It entered the university's official history in 1437, and Bolognini took it as genuine.

His problem was holding that charter and the Donation as genuine at the same time, because their claims about Rome couldn't both stand. The whole conflict sat in a single word. The charter has Theodosius call Rome *nostra*, our city, and if Constantine had already handed Rome to the pope, Theodosius couldn't call it his.

Bolognini worked through several ways of explaining it, and when he reached the answer that would weaken the Donation, he refused it. He wrote: "This last answer does not please me, for I have always held and do hold that that donation was wholly valid."

He didn't answer Valla briefly. An index compiled by his own pupil claims he laid flat 103 of Valla's arguments across forty columns. A hundred and three answers to one man, built around a university charter forged roughly two centuries before Bolognini was born.

[CL] This wasn't something only modern scholars could see. Bursellis, a chronicler living in the same city who died in 1497, thought it remarkable that so great a man had failed to detect the document's fabricated falsity.

## ACT 6

[CL] Bolognini tried to keep the Donation standing. Piccolomini accepted that it was forged and kept defending the pope's territorial power without it.

Enea Silvio Piccolomini defended the Donation in 1436. He raised doubts about it in 1443, and by 1453 two of the objections in his own writing were the same ones Valla had made.

He based that power on spiritual authority and on the idea that imperial power could be transferred from one ruler to another. He later became Pope Pius II, and the power outlived the charter it had rested on.

[CL] Valla's criticism was available to the people answering him.

Twenty-five manuscripts of Valla's treatise survive. It went into print in 1506, and before that it circulated in manuscript copies. The claim that the Church had blocked the work came from Valla's own publishers, and they gave no witness for it. To this day no testimony is known that documents a church intervention. The first documented ban came in 1559, when the treatise went on the Index, and that's about a century after Valla died.

He left out an ancient mosaic inscription at Saint Peter's that weakened part of his case. The inscription said Constantine built the original basilica.

Then in 1444, when the apostolic origin of the Creed was at stake, he went further. He argued that a passage in Gratian's *Decretum* was corrupt and needed changing, and that reading was forced. It sits at odds with a disinterested philology.

Camporeale, the scholar who documents that second case, is Valla's greatest admirer.

The man who dated a document by its language declined to do so where the answer would have cost him.
