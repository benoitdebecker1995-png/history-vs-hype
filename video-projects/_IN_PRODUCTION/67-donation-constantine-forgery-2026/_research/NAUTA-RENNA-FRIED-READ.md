## VERDICT (5 lines)

1. **Nothing here breaks the thesis.** Both men did catch a forgery, and both readings hold.
2. **Fried constrains it hard, on two axes:** Valla convicted the *Bologna canon-law version*, not the
   original *Constitutum*; and the "transfer of the Western Empire" Valla demolished was a
   12th-century reinterpretation, not what the 9th-century forger wrote.
3. **Nauta constrains the "science" register, not the finding:** he calls the treatise an *oration* and
   "polemical propaganda," yet exempts Valla's philology as where his strength actually lay.
4. **Two things strengthen the script:** Brandes (in Fried, 2007) confirms Valla's *satraps*
   objection still stands and was the only serious study of it before 2007; and Fried places Cusa
   among Valla's protectors, which is a better and truer relation than "Valla built on Cusa."
5. **Renna is more than background** — he is the loudest voice against calling the treatise good
   argument — but he is C-grade and partly novelistic; use him only where Nauta or Fried agree.

# SECTION N — Nauta, *In Defense of Common Sense* (Harvard UP, 2009)

## N1. Nauta accepts the forgery verdict, and never analyses the treatise

Searched the full 409-page volume for `Donation`, `Constantin`, `falso credita`, `forger`. Matched
forms found: "Donation of Constantine" (pp. 1, 205, and n. 64 to p. 208), "De falso credita et
ementita Constantini donation[e]" (n. 63 to p. 199-area, and bibliography p. 390), "forgery/
forgeries" (pp. 1, 208, and n. 49 to p. 205). **Every hit is a passing mention or a bibliographic
entry. There is no section, sub-section or sustained argument about the treatise in this book.**

> "While primarily known for his studies of the Latin language, the Greek text of the New Testament,
> and the Donation of Constantine (which he famously showed to be a forgery), Valla is also the
> author of a highly interesting but difficult and poorly understood work on philosophy and
> dialectic."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 1 (PDF 13); search of pp. 1–401 returned only the forms listed above

*For the script:* Nauta is a **guard**, not a source on the treatise. Do not cite him as assessing
Valla's method against the Donation — he never does. Cite him for what Valla was and was not.

## N2. Nauta calls the treatise an oration written as patron-service, and names it propaganda

> "after a protracted war in which Valla had lent active support to his patron by writing treatises,
> such as the oration on the Donation of Constantine and the *De professione religiosorum*, against
> various claims made on behalf of the pope and the religious orders. As political allegiances had
> shifted and the king no longer needed the polemical propaganda of his militant secretary, Valla's
> room for antipapal and antireligious writing had shrunk."

**[CORROBORATED]** · locator: Nauta p. 205 (PDF 217, image-verified); corroborates Camporeale (C10g) and Renna R1/R4

*For the script:* This is a second university-press voice for "oratio, not treatise." It is stated as
fact, not hedged. Nauta uses *the oration* as the work's descriptor without argument — he takes the
genre as settled.

## N3. Nauta's own anti-anachronism guard — and the line the video should quote against itself

> "One may regret the fact that Valla, in spite of his growing expertise in Greek and wider reading
> of the Aristotelian corpus, gave such a tendentious and selective account of the matter, but of
> course that would be an anachronistic complaint. Scholarship was (and is) not always a
> disinterested activity to serve solely the wider community of scholars and society. It was also an
> instrument to serve one's own goals, though both aims could, of course, go hand in hand."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 204 (PDF 216, image-verified)

Nauta names the trap in the Conclusion too:

> "This is a dangerous undertaking, as anachronisms and distorted readings lie in ambush."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 273 (PDF 284)

> "Such comparisons often tell us more about the background and interests of the historian than about
> the historical agent"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 7 (PDF 19)

*For the script:* This is the cleanest available answer to Grafton's "hagiographical anachronism."
Nauta's version is stronger than a bare warning: he says demanding disinterest of a 15th-century
scholar is *itself* the anachronism. That reframes the Camporeale guard from "Valla fails a modern
standard" to "the modern standard is not the right ruler."

## N4. But Nauta puts the philology on the credit side — explicitly including forgery-detection

> "It is difficult to discern a comprehensive or consistent method of theology in his works, but it
> is not difficult to see where the strong sides of his 'theological' scholarship lie, viz., in his
> humanist study of words and texts, a knowledge that he put to good use in studying the New
> Testament and in exploding forgeries, legends, and false ascriptions."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 208 (PDF 220, image-verified)

And on the New Testament annotations:

> "the annotations to the New Testament testify to his formidable philological skills, his critical
> acumen, and his historically sensitive exegesis."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 208 (PDF 220, image-verified)

*For the script:* This matters because of **who** is saying it. Nauta's whole book is a demolition of
Valla-as-philosopher; the fact that the demolisher carves out the philology as the strong side is a
real endorsement, not a courtesy. "Historically sensitive exegesis" is Nauta's own phrase.

## N5. The "poor, inconsistent, and unfair" verdict — and its exact scope

> "The outcome of this analysis and evaluation is not always flattering for Valla: his arguments
> against Aristotle, Boethius, and the scholastics are often poor, inconsistent, and unfair."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 4 (PDF 16, image-verified)

**Scope warning.** The sentence names three targets: Aristotle, Boethius, the scholastics. It is a
verdict on the *Repastinatio*, not on the *Donation* treatise. Quoting it as "scholars say Valla's
arguments were poor" without the scope would misrepresent Nauta.

**[CONTESTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 4 vs. Nauta p. 208 — the same author rates Valla's philology and Valla's philosophy oppositely

*For the script:* Usable only with the scope attached. The honest form is: "The scholar who took
Valla's philosophy apart says his arguments there were poor, inconsistent and unfair — and in the
same book says the word-work is where he was formidable."

## N6. Valla is interested in words, not in concepts

> "Although Valla's work shows an impressive sensitivity to semantic nuances, to the way language
> works and can mislead us, his investigations are not equivalent to the conceptual analysis of
> modern ordinary language philosophers. To put it succinctly: Valla is interested in words, not in
> concepts."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 284 (PDF 295, image-verified)

> "at the level of arguments, Valla did not show himself to be a deep and consistent thinker, but
> behind this inconsistency lies a consistent program"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 272 (PDF 283)

*For the script:* This cuts **both** ways and is the most useful single sentence in Nauta for this
video. It caps any claim that Valla was doing systematic theory. It also explains, in his own
scholarly enemy's words, exactly why his instrument worked on a document: a man interested in words
is precisely the man who catches a Latin word that cannot be in the room in 315.

## N7. What Valla thought he was doing — Nauta's answer

Nauta's Valla is an *anti-philosopher* whose criterion is common linguistic usage:

> "Even though Valla presented himself as an antiphilosopher and his work as an antidote to
> philosophical speculation, his critique is still philosophically significant."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 7 (PDF 19)

> "his critique itself is shot through with philosophical assumptions, e.g., that language can present
> a reliable picture of reality, that only classical Latin can properly fulfill this role, that
> ordinary linguistic practice establishes the meaning of words, that the use of ancient classical
> style is not just a matter of aesthetic preference but the only way to present things in their
> truest style"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 7 (PDF 19)

*For the script:* "Language can present a reliable picture of reality" is the load-bearing premise
under the Donation argument. If words are a reliable index of the world, then a word out of period
is a fact out of period. That is the bridge from Valla's grammar to Valla's forensics, in Nauta's own
list of Valla's assumptions.

## N8. *Res* and *verba* — and Nauta's refusal of the "language constitutes reality" reading

> "Our detailed analysis of Valla's remarks on thing and truth, however, suggests that these claims
> are far-fetched. The underlying assumption of much of what Valla wrote is that language refers to
> the world"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 274 (PDF 285)

> "Valla's own struggles with terminology […] also reveal some basic realist assumptions, 'realist'
> in the sense of assuming that there is a world of things independent of our linguistic
> representation of it"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 275 (PDF 286)

Nauta explicitly rejects Camporeale's formula here:

> "It seems, therefore, too sweeping to say, with Camporeale, that for Valla 'non si dà res senza
> sermo'"

**[CONTESTED]** · locator: Nauta n. 61 to ch. 2, p. 312 (PDF 312), against Camporeale 1986, p. 227

*For the script:* Do **not** say Valla thought language constitutes reality (a common pop-history
line). Nauta, having read the whole *Repastinatio*, says the opposite and names the scholars who got
it wrong. Also note: this is Nauta disagreeing with **Camporeale**, our own C10g source. Two of our
B-grade sources are in live conflict about what Valla thought words were.

## N9. "Ordinary language" was not ordinary — but Nauta complicates the standard rebuttal

The received objection:

> "Valla's 'ordinary language' was hardly ordinary; it was the classical Latin of the great authors,
> not the Roman *volgare* of Valla's own time."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 7 (PDF 19)

Nauta then argues this is not quite right either:

> "It is therefore not correct to state categorically that for Valla *consuetudo* always means what
> the learned — the orators and the cultured — say"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 280 (PDF 291)

*For the script:* Background only. Relevant only if the script were to claim Valla judged the
Donation's Latin against "how Romans actually spoke." He judged it against the classical literary
norm, with the boundary fuzzier than usually stated.

## N10. The counter-example that cuts hardest: Valla did *not* apply historicity when it hurt him

In 1444 Valla argued that a passage of Gratian's *Decretum* underpinning belief in the Apostolic
origin of the Creed was corrupt and needed emendation. Nauta reports the scholarly verdict:

> "Both Fois (1969, 370-371) and Fubini (1999, 311, n. 61) call Valla's emendation forced, the latter
> adding that it is at odds with 'una disinteressata filologia.'"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta n. 49 to ch. 6, p. 349 (PDF 361)

And Camporeale's observation, as Nauta reports it:

> "Camporeale is less negative and notices that Valla, who otherwise is so sensitive 'alle variazioni
> e alle dimensioni storiche della cultura,' fails to apply this insight of the historicity of texts
> to the formulation of the creed"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta n. 40 to ch. 6, p. 348-9 (PDF 360), citing Camporeale 1972, 269-270

*Translation:* "una disinteressata filologia" = "a disinterested philology." "alle variazioni e alle
dimensioni storiche della cultura" = "to the variations and to the historical dimensions of culture."

*For the script:* **This is the strongest single item against the "disinterested method" framing** —
and it changes what the video can claim, because it is Valla using textual criticism selectively,
four years after the Donation, against a document he needed to be corrupt. Camporeale — the scholar
who most admires Valla's rhetoric-as-philology — is the one who points out he did not apply his own
insight when it cost him. If the script wants an honest concession beat, this is it.

Note also, in the same chapter's notes, Vives' 16th-century verdict as Nauta quotes it:

> "Valla was full of passion and rushed to conclusions … his arguments are not shored up with strong
> reasons, nor were they accepted by anyone as doctrines of dialectic."

**[SOURCED]** · locator: Nauta n. 13 to Introduction, p. 296 (PDF 305), quoting Rummel 2000, 158

## N11. Boethius

Nauta's index lists Boethius across pp. 21, 27, 38, 92, 96–99, 172, 225–228, 302n51, 305n89, 320n33,
321n43, 343n69, 359n66 — i.e. Boethius is a constant target in the *Repastinatio*. Nauta's preface
records Boethius as "one of Valla's *bêtes noires*" (**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. xii, PDF 11).
On Aristotle, Nauta reports Valla's own self-positioning:

> "Not to be endured, then, are the modern Peripatetics who forbid me, a person belonging to no group,
> the liberty of disagreeing with Aristotle, as if he were the sage, and not they, and as if no one
> had done this before."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Nauta p. 271 (PDF 282), translating *Repastinatio* 1:17

*For the script:* "as if no one had done this before" is a usable one-line proof that Valla knew he
was not inventing criticism from scratch — it corroborates the existing guard (Julius Africanus,
Theopompus) from Valla's own mouth, in a different context.

# SECTION R — Renna, *Expositions* 8.1 (2014), pp. 1–28

**Verdict on load-bearing status: NOT background only.** Renna is the most hostile assessment of the
treatise's *argumentative* quality in the whole source set, and he is the one who most directly
addresses "what did Valla think he was doing." But his evidence base is weak (see R8) and his method
includes invented interior monologue (see R5). **Use him only where Nauta or Fried independently
agree.**

## R1. Renna's own thesis: the Discourse as an open letter to Frederick III

> "It is argued here that Valla wrote the Declaration in part to encourage greater imperial
> involvement in the papal schism and especially in the affairs of Italy and the City of Rome."

**[ASSERTED]** · locator: Renna p. 1, Abstract

Renna's hedging is heavy and consistent throughout ("we contend," "it is argued here," "may have,"
"in the opinion of the present author"). He never claims direct evidence; he concedes the emperor is
absent from the text:

> "Valla himself virtually ignores contemporary emperors (Frederick III is not even mentioned)"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Renna p. 2

*For the script:* Do not use. The thesis is Renna's own speculation, argued from silence, and he says
so. It is not needed by the video and would import a contested reading.

## R2. Renna on the treatise as a piece of argument — the sharpest dissent in the source set

> "Modern historians often praise the Donation as a compelling work which definitively demolished the
> counterfeit document. In fact, the piece is sometimes a rambling collage of disparate arguments only
> loosely connected."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Renna p. 2

> "Accordingly, many of his arguments against the forgery are irrelevant, even trivial, and often
> strain a point. Valla's heavy resort to sarcasm makes some of his 'arguments' appear more like witty
> remarks than as serious objections to the Donation."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Renna p. 8

And his endnote 11, the fullest indictment:

> "Sometimes the descriptions of Valla's Discourse seem almost like panegyrics, with little heed to
> his exaggerations, overstatements, petty asides, sarcasms, ad hominem barbs, irrelevancies,
> superficial digressions, overblown rhetorical speeches, facile witticisms, meandering arguments, a
> priori judgments, anticlericism, and hostile tone. My sense is that it was left unrevised."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Renna n. 11, p. 20

*For the script:* This is the counter-voice the video needs and currently lacks. It is C-grade alone,
but it converges with Nauta's "oration / polemical propaganda" (N2) and with Fried's "circumspect
scholar … only criticised the younger Bologna version" (F9). The convergent claim — *the treatise is
a mixed bag with real hits buried in invective* — is safe to state.

## R3. Renna's "five straw men" — Valla overstates what the Donation was doing in 1440

Renna argues Valla exaggerates the document's live role, and that Eugenius IV is a literary construct:

> "But, in point of fact, Eugenius never claimed — Valla is not consistent here in describing this
> Pope's temporal assertions — extensive power over the empire or all of Italy as the Discourse
> declares."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Renna p. 9

> "Popes have always used the Donation to prop up their temporal authority. […] Clearly not true.
> Innocent IV, expanding upon Innocent III, was in fact the first pope to explicitly claim the fullness
> of power on the basis of the Donation. Most fourteenth-century pontiffs gave little attention to the
> Donation"

**[CORROBORATED]** · locator: Renna p. 11; corroborated by Fried p. 25 ("The 'Palea Constantinus' (D. 96 c. 14) was not dealt with in ordinary lectures at universities")

*For the script:* Corroborated and usable. It supports a real beat: **the document Valla attacked was
already half-dead in the schools.** Two independent sources say the *palea* was not taught and that
the popes' use of it was intermittent. That makes the video's stakes question ("why did it matter?")
answerable honestly rather than inflated.

## R4. Valla did not investigate what the document actually was

> "Strangely, Valla does not speculate on the circumstances of the forgery itself, except to imply
> that it was made some centuries after the time of the first Christian emperor."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Renna p. 2

But Renna's own endnote qualifies this, and is more precise:

> "Valla seems to think that the counterfeit document was composed sometime between the Acts of
> Sylvester and the Carolingian kings. For sure, a pope is the culprit; Donation, 66 (§81), etc. […]
> But he is unwilling to speculate on a Carolingian origin, even though he could have suspected this,
> given his chronicle sources."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Renna n. 15, p. 22

*For the script:* **This is a correction to the Levine guard as currently held.** Levine's "Valla
never asked what it is and when it was written" is too strong. Renna says Valla did place it in a
window (post-*Actus Silvestri*, pre-Carolingian) and did name the culprit type (a pope). Fried
independently supplies the Latin: see F11 below. Reframe the guard from "he never asked" to "he asked
and stopped one step short of the answer" — which is both truer and a better beat.

## R5. ⚠ Renna reconstructs Valla's interior monologue. Do not treat as evidence.

Renna narrates Valla's motives in the first person for a full page:

> "Lorenzo Valla may have reflected: If the Donation can be shown to be counterfeit, there is a
> reasonable chance that the New Constantine of the North can bring unity of purpose and common cause
> to Christendom"

**[ASSERTED]** · locator: Renna p. 13 — this is Renna's own invented monologue, flagged only by "may have reflected"

The same applies to the Cusa-reading narrative, which carries no citation at all:

> "Sometime after 1433, Valla came upon a version of Nicholas of Cusa's Catholic Concordance, which
> referred to the Donation of Constantine. […] he was impressed and wished he had written the piece.
> The cantankerous Valla found it hard to resist a confrontation."

**[ASSERTED]** · locator: Renna p. 7 — no source cited on that page for Valla's reading of Cusa or his reaction

Renna does source the *fact* of Valla having read Cusa elsewhere, but only by assertion:

> "In Cusa's Catholic Concordance, which Valla read, the Empire has a close connection with the church
> in Italy."

**[ASSERTED]** · locator: Renna n. 53, p. 27, citing Sigmund 1963 ch. 8 for the Empire/church point, not for Valla's reading

*For the script:* **Hard stop.** If the video wants "Valla read Cusa," it must come from Fubini
(A10, our C9d dependence claim), not from Renna. Renna's version is a novelist's reconstruction.

## R6. Renna concedes the field is divided on whether Valla had a political motive at all

> "In general Wolfram Setz, in his masterful study and edition of Valla's treatise, seems to assume
> that Valla's contemporaries gave little importance to the Donation's legal and historical
> significance. Setz does not believe Valla had much if any political motive in writing his Discourse."

**[CONTESTED]** · locator: Renna n. 5, p. 18, characterising Setz 1975 chs. 1 and 3

*For the script:* Note this against our own use of Setz (A5, the suppression refutation). Renna and
Setz disagree about motive. The script should not assert a settled motive.

## R7. ⚠ Renna, writing in 2014, reports that Fried's dating has NOT won the field

> "A near-consensus points to the time of Pope Paul I (757-67), although several versions may have
> circulated. […] A strong case for the 820s has been advanced by Johannes Fried, *Donation of
> Constantine and Constitutum Constantini* […] While many reviewers remain unconvinced by the new
> dating, the debate continues."

**[CONTESTED]** · locator: Renna n. 3, p. 18

*For the script:* **Load-bearing.** This is an independent, seven-years-later check on Fried. Whatever
the script says about the document's date must carry this modality: the mid-8th-century dating is
still the near-consensus; Fried's 9th-century Frankish dating is a strong minority case. Do not
present Fried's answer as "the modern answer" without that qualifier.

## R8. ⚠ Renna's own evidence standard is low

His endnotes cite, as substantive references: the English and German **Wikipedia** articles on
Frederick III, with page counts, in n. 23 (p. 23); and a **blog post** by Roger Pearse, quoted
approvingly, in n. 13 (p. 21) — "As Pearse puts it: 'Valla's text is not a modern scholarly study, but
a declamation — a piece of rhetoric.'" He also uses a Wikipedia article on Valla as a foil in n. 28.

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Renna nn. 13, 23, 28 (pp. 21, 23, 24)

*For the script:* Confirms the existing Grade C. Renna's *reading* of the treatise (R2, R3, R4) rests
on the primary text and is checkable; his *historical apparatus* is not to be relied on.

# SECTION F — Fried, *Donation of Constantine and Constitutum Constantini* (de Gruyter, 2007)

The dating/origin argument occupies chs. V (pp. 53–68), Va (69–72), VI (73–110) and VII (111–114),
with Brandes' appendix at pp. 115–128.

## F1. Fried's answer, in one sentence

> "instead of assuming that the forgery had its origins in the Roman Patriarchium, it is attributed to
> the milieu of the opposition to Louis the Pious about 830/833 (which does not exclude the following
> years), to Frankish reform of the empire instead of Roman self-interpretation and presumptuousness."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 112 (PDF 122)

> "The forged decree was compiled among the circle of Franks who, late in the reign of Louis the
> Pious, sought to reform the empire. It was the same circle that initiated the magnificent forgeries
> of Pseudo-Isidore."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 111 (PDF 121)

*For the script:* Crisp, quotable, and 40 words. This is the modern answer to the question Valla did
not finish asking — carried with R7's modality.

## F2. The four *termini post quos*

> "The first – a short quote from the Roman synod of 769 that in its brevity is typical of the
> forger's methods – renders a date during the pontificate of Paul impossible; the second, the absence
> of the 'Patricii' in the 'Sanctio' places it after Charlemagne's coronation as emperor; the third,
> the quote from the so-called 'Divisio regnorum' of 806 discussed above, and which probably refers to
> the so-called 'Regni divisio' of 831, is surely also an indication of a 9th century date; finally we
> have the repeated reference to the papal *Palatium Lateranense* (ll. 121, 189-90, 219), which is
> never called *sacrum* in the 'Constitutum Constantini' as should be expected if the document had
> been composed in Rome."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 73 (PDF 83)

The first is the phrase *uno ex eadem sancta Trinitate* at l. 3, traced to the Roman synod of
12–14 April 769 (**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried n. 230, p. 73). Note the line numbers — they are
Fuhrmann's MGH line numbers, which we hold as P1, so every one of these is checkable on screen.

Fried's own summary of which two he thinks are decisive:

> "There are two 'termini post quos' for its date that quite clearly point to the 9th century: as far
> as we can tell, the name *Palatium Lateranense* for the papal palace belongs to the years after Leo
> III and Charlemagne, and Constantine's triumphal title probably did not become current until after
> 830/831."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 113 (PDF 123)

*For the script:* Two of these are the same *species* of argument Valla ran — a word that cannot be in
the room at the stated date. Fried's method is Valla's method with 570 more years of comparanda. That
is a genuinely earned closing beat, and it does not require calling Valla a modern.

## F3. The *Palatium Lateranense* argument, compressed

The papal residence was called *Patriarchium* (from the 8th c.) or *Episcopium Lateranense* (from the
mid-7th c.), never *Palatium*, until Leo III:

> "It is first referred to as a *Palatium*, as we have already seen, in 813 in a judgement of Leo III
> for the (Frankish influenced) monastery of Farfa, and again in 829 in another judgement for the same
> monastery."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 85 (PDF 95)

> "It is generally claimed by scholars that the term *Palatium* was taken from the 'Constitutum
> Constantini'. But in fact it was the other way round, for no Roman before Leo III had called the
> *Patriarchium* a *Palatium*."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 86 (PDF 96)

> "In fact things must have happened the other way round: Frankish influence will have resulted in the
> centre of papal secular power on occasions being referred to as a *Palatium* […] The forger will
> quite naturally have regularly used a term that was part of the standard vocabulary of the Franks,
> and avoided the normal term, *Patriarchium*."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 88 (PDF 98)

*Fried's modality:* he uses "will have," "must have," "as far as we can tell" — inference from usage,
not a document. Carry the hedge.

## F4. The other non-Roman tells (ch. V, pp. 57–60)

Fried's list of departures from Roman practice, each argued as evidence of a non-Roman author:
*frygium* for the papal headwear where Rome said *regnum* or *corona* (p. 57–58); *superhumerale*
instead of *humerale* for the imperial *loros*, conflating *pallium* and *humerale* (p. 59); undifferentiated
ceremonial shoes for all clerical ranks where Rome distinguished *campagi*/*udones* from *subtalares*
(p. 59); and the emperor serving as *strator* before the pope's coronation, which is not the Roman rite
(p. 60).

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried pp. 57–60 (PDF 67–70)

Fried states the methodological rule he is applying:

> "A conclusion like that would require informations to which nobody had access outside the city. This
> is a methodological requirement that has all too often been neglected"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 57 (PDF 67)

*For the script:* A usable one-line contrast: Valla asked *is this word possible in 315?*; Fried asks
*is this detail possible outside Rome?* Same instrument, different axis.

## F5. Who made it — Fried says he does not know

> "But who did the deed? The answer is, we do not know, although here and there we can get close to
> him. What has been presented here, and how could it be otherwise for a period with such poor
> sources, is to a great extent hypothesis. To this extent it is no worse than other theories about
> Pseudo-Constantine's constitution."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 112 (PDF 122)

On the named candidates:

> "Could it be that perhaps Wala of Corbie or Hilduin of St-Denis – either one of them alone, or both
> together – could have been the author of the 'Constitutum'? I would not like to exclude this
> possibility, even if any answer can be no more than hypothesis and speculation, and the origin of
> the document has left only indistinct traces."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 106 (PDF 116)

> "even if it is not yet possible to reconstruct exactly how and when this cooperation took place, and
> the man who actually penned the pseudo-Constantinian text remains anonymous."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 112 (PDF 122)

*For the script:* **Do not name a forger.** Fried triple-hedges. The honest line is: the best modern
case points to a circle — the Frankish opposition to Louis the Pious, the same circle that made
Pseudo-Isidore — and to two abbots, Hilduin of St-Denis and Wala of Corbie, without naming a hand.

## F6. The *palea* and the canon-law transmission — Fried engages both directly

Gratian left it out; a decretist put it in:

> "Only the most important of the early canonists, the famous but enigmatic Gratian, was unwilling to
> include the 'Constitutum Constantini' in his 'Concordantia discordantium canonum' […] This was
> probably the work of Paucapalea (about 1148) […] He (or whoever) dressed up a long excerpt from the
> 'Constitutum' as 'Palea Constantinus' (D. 96 c. 14), and added a summary or explanatory preface at
> the beginning (probably drawing on Anselm) in the form of a further Palea (D. 96 c. 13)"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 19 (PDF 29)

The manuscript statistic, and Valla noticing it:

> "Only about a third of the 12th and 13th-century manuscripts of the 'Decretum' followed Paucapalea's
> amendment and included the 'Constitutum Constantini' in the text. This was noticed by the humanist
> Lorenzo Valla when he proved it was a forgery."

**[CORROBORATED]** · locator: Fried p. 28 (PDF 38); independently supports Fubini's *palea* correction (A10 / C9e)

And the *Glossa ordinaria*:

> "The 'Glossa Ordinaria' of Johannes Teutonicus, the standard commentary on the 'Decretum', notes
> laconically (about 1215): 'This Palea is not read in the schools'."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 28 (PDF 38)

Fried prints both *paleae* in Latin and English in Appendix B IV/V, pp. 148 and 151.

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried pp. 148, 151 (PDF 158, 161)

*For the script:* This is an on-screen exhibit we already hold, in a Grade-B volume, with the Latin
and a translation on facing appendix pages. The *Glossa ordinaria* line — a canonist's shrug that the
text was not taught — is a gift for the "was it even live?" beat.

## F7. ⚠ CONSTRAINT — Valla attacked the Bologna canon-law version, not the original

> "Even a circumspect scholar like Lorenzo Valla, who in 1440 exposed the Donation of Constantine as a
> forgery, only criticised the younger Bologna version and not the original; even had he done so, his
> task would probably not have been any easier. The canonistic version reflected knowledge that was
> generally held, but hardly the intentions of the actual author of the forgery."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 30 (PDF 40, image-verified)

*For the script:* The single most consequential correction in this read, and what it changes is the
frame of the whole "he tested it against its own Latin" beat: Valla's target text was the truncated,
emended canonistic *Donation*, not the *Constitutum Constantini* as its 9th-century author wrote it.
Note that Fried calls Valla "a circumspect scholar" and adds that having the original would not have
made the job easier — he is not scoring a point off Valla, he is describing what was on the desk.

## F8. ⚠ CONSTRAINT — the "Donation" Valla demolished is a 12th-century reinterpretation

> "the central Carolingian terms *potestas* and *dicio*, as the joint term *potestas et dicio*, which
> was just as old, no longer referred to Episcopal jurisdiction and papal protection as it had done,
> but assumed instead another meaning that was derived from Roman law, dominion and property."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 111 (PDF 121)

> "great care has been taken to distinguish between Constantine's deplorable 'Donation', an invention
> of the 12th century, and the original wording of the 'Constitutum Constantini', which is not
> normally the case."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 112 (PDF 122)

> "although the forger's Constantine had granted the pope the universal episcopate as well as
> patriarchal authority in the West of the Roman Empire, and as far as secular legal power was
> concerned, only 'power' over the City of Rome."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 113 (PDF 123)

He also documents one of the small manipulations:

> "nobody was bothered when instead of 'property on various islands' ('praedia […] in […] diversis
> insulis') without nearer specification of which ones, as in the original text of the
> pseudo-Constantinian charter, suddenly 'all islands' ('omnes insulae') were claimed as the property
> of the Apostolic See; in other words all the islands of the earth."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 28 (PDF 38)

*For the script:* Fried's implication, stated plainly at p. 113, is that Valla and Luther helped fix
the misreading in place:

> "such a new interpretation should not let itself be irritated by the medieval exegetes, whose voices
> were heard at the beginning and who, via the works of Lorenzo Valla and Martin Luther, even to the
> present day justified the exegetic prejudices which made of the Frankish 'Constitutum Constantini'
> the infamous 'Donation of Constantine'."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried p. 113 (PDF 123)

This is the sharpest thing any of our B-grade sources says against Valla's *result* (not his method):
he demolished a claim the document's author may never have made, and his demolition helped make that
misreading permanent. It does not make the document genuine. It does complicate "he caught it."

## F9. STRENGTHENS — Fried places Cusa among Valla's protectors, not merely his predecessors

> popes, Nicholas V and Calixtus III, who were his friends; as well as King Alfonso and King Ferdinand
> of Naples, in whose service he had written his work. Without their support the courageous scholar

## F10. STRENGTHENS — Brandes (2007) says Valla's *satraps* objection stands, and stood alone

Wolfram Brandes, a Byzantinist, contributes the appendix on the three occurrences of *satrapa*
(ll. 119, 158, 282):

> "The title (the description of office) of *satrapa* is used here as a synonym to *optimates*. This
> circumstance already would seem dubious, and indeed, Lorenzo Valla has taken offence to the usage,
> emphasising the fact that this is unthinkable for the time of Constantine. However, it is also
> highly unusual for later centuries."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried/Brandes p. 116 (PDF 126), citing Valla ed. Setz pp. 102-104 at his n. 7

> "Even until the end of the tenth century did the Roman Empire possess no satraps."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried/Brandes p. 117 (PDF 127)

> "As far as has come to my knowledge, no-one has yet attempted a special study of this blatant
> violation of Roman-Byzantine administrative reality (with the exception of the above-mentioned
> Lorenzo Valla)."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried/Brandes p. 118 (PDF 128)

Brandes' own conclusion redirects the point — Frankish/Saxon usage, not forgery *per se* — and he

> "they are further indication (as opposed to evidence or proof in the stricter sense of the word) for
> the 'Constitutum Constantini' as not having originated in Rome."

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried/Brandes p. 127 (PDF 137)

*For the script:* This is a clean, dated, checkable strengthener: in 2007 a Byzantinist writes that on
this specific word, Valla was right and — for 567 years — alone. It is also honest about where the
two men part: Valla read the anachronism as proof of forgery; Brandes reads it as proof of a Frankish
hand. Same observation, two conclusions, and the video can say so in one sentence.

## F12. Fried's confidence, in his own words

Fried is unusually explicit about his epistemic status: "to a great extent hypothesis" (p. 112);
"no more than hypothesis and speculation" (p. 106); "it cannot be proved either" (p. 80); "Even if it

this was collected and arranged" (p. 72, on the St-Denis formulary). He also concedes the field:
"about 750/770, when the 'Constitutum Constantini' is generally supposed to have been composed"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: Fried pp. 72, 78, 80, 85, 106, 112 (PDF 82, 88, 90, 95, 116, 122)

*For the script:* Combine with R7. The correct on-screen modality is: *the leading modern case dates

# WHAT I COULD NOT ESTABLISH

1. **Whether Fried's dating has gained ground since 2014.** Renna n. 3 (2014) is the latest
   assessment held. Nothing in this repo's source set post-dates it on the dating question. The
   Rojas Donat & de Toro (2023) piece is held and unread on this point — check it before the script
   asserts anything about the current state of the dating debate.
2. **Whether Cusa actually protected Valla.** Fried p. 31 asserts it without a source (F9). Not
   corroborated anywhere in Nauta or Renna. Needs a third source or the claim must be dropped.
3. **What Valla actually wrote at §79 / §81.** Fried and Renna cite different sections for the same
   claim (F11, R4). I did not open P2/P2b or the Coleman text to check. This must be verified against
   the Latin before it goes on screen.
4. **Whether Nauta anywhere assesses Valla's *historical* method.** Searched `philolog*`, `anachron*`,
   `historical criticism`, `historical method`, `Camporeale` across all 409 pages. Nauta discusses
   Valla's philology only in the Trinity chapter's closing pages (pp. 206-208) and in notes; he
   explicitly declines the historiographical debate — "It goes beyond the self-imposed limits of this
   chapter to enter into this historiographical minefield" (p. 207). There is no Nauta verdict on
   Valla-as-historian to be had from this book.
5. **The identity of the "some scholars" Nauta cites at p. 207** who argue Valla's philological work
   "did not always live up to its high ambitions." His n. 58 points to Perosi, Bentley 1983 and
   Celenza 1994 on the New Testament work generally; I could not pin the specific criticism to a
   named argument.
6. **Fried's engagement with Cusa's *arguments*.** Fried mentions Cusa three times (pp. 3, 31, and
   index p. 194) and never analyses *De concordantia catholica*. Searched `Cusa`, `Cusanus`, `Kues`
   across all 212 pages; those are all the substantive hits. Fried is not a source on Cusa's method.
