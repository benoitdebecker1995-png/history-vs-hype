## VERDICT

1. **Recent scholarship CONSTRAINS two things and CHANGES one.** It does not overturn the project.
2. **CONSTRAINT — the date.** Mureşan (2019, open access) counts **six** live models placing the
   *Constitutum*'s genesis anywhere between **750 and 850**, Fried's being the fifth. The
   Geschichtsquellen repertory records Fried's thesis as *umstritten*. **The script must not assert
   a date**; it should assert the disagreement, which is better material anyway.
3. **CONSTRAINT — Cusa did not shield Valla.** The most recent authoritative life (Nauta, SEP, rev.
   6 March 2026) credits **King Alfonso** with rescuing Valla in 1444; Cusa and Bessarion appear a
   decade later as backers of the *Annotationes*. Fried's unsourced p. 31 claim looks like a fusion
   of the two. **Cut it.**
4. **CHANGE — a fourth critic.** Hiatt (*Historical Research*, 2023) documents John Whethamstede's
   1430s *Granarium* doubting the Donation on grounds distinct from Cusa, Valla and Pecock, and
   argues the scepticism was broader than assumed. A three-lone-geniuses framing is now dated.
5. **Recency gate: passed.** Six items in the 2018–2026 band, three in the 2023+ priority band
   (Hiatt 2023, den Haan 2023, Bertrand 2025), plus the 2022 CUP reference volume. **Largest known
   hole: RI-OPAC returned 403 and was never queried** — see NEGATIVE FINDINGS 6.

## Q1 — THE DATING DEBATE, 2026 STATE OF PLAY

**Answer: still contested, and Fried has not won.** The traditional papal-Roman dating remains the
default in reference works; Fried's 820s/830s Frankish thesis is carried as a named, attributed
minority position, not as the state of the art. Renna's 2014 report (n. 3, p. 18) still describes
the field accurately in 2026. **The script cannot assert a single date. It can assert the two
camps, and it can name the disagreement as itself the interesting fact.**

Evidence, in order of weight:

### Q1-a. *Geschichtsquellen des deutschen Mittelalters*, entry "Constitutum Constantini"

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: `geschichtsquellen.de/werk/1329` — entry "Constitutum Constantini", read 2026-08-03

The Bayerische Akademie der Wissenschaften's authoritative repertory of medieval German-realm
sources. Its own header calls the text **"a forgery of the 8th or 9th century"** — i.e. the
reference database declines to commit to a century. It then lays out exactly two positions:

| Camp | Claim as the repertory states it |
|---|---|
| Traditional | Rome, under **Pope Hadrian I (772–795)** |
| Fried (2007) | The **Frankish realm under Louis the Pious (814–840)**, associated with Hilduin of St-Denis and Wala of Corbie, in the Pseudo-Isidorian forgery context |

And on Fried it records that the interpretation **"remained contested"** (*blieb umstritten*).

⚠ **Nuance the script should carry:** the "traditional" camp is not one date. Renna reports the
near-consensus as **Paul I (757–67)**; the Geschichtsquellen repertory gives it as **Hadrian I
(772–95)**. So the mainstream itself spans roughly **750s–790s**. "Mid-to-late eighth century,
papal Rome" is the safe formulation; "757" or "Paul I" is not safe.

### Q1-a′. Mureşan 2019 — the historiographical survey that answers Q1 directly, and it is free

**Why this is the answer to Q1.** Mureşan opens with a historiographical review and finds
**six competing interpretive models placing the document's genesis between 750 and 850** — a
hundred-year spread, still live. As read on OpenEdition:

| # | Dating | Associated with |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 753–754, Stephen II's Frankish mission / Pepin as *patricius* | Von Döllinger; Heinrich Böhmer |
| 2 | 757–767, under Paul I, against Byzantine iconoclasm | Scheffer-Boichorst; Wilhelm Levison |
| 3 | 776–778, under Hadrian I (his 778 letter to Charlemagne) | Josef Langen; Edgar Loening |
| 4 | 800–812, legitimating Charlemagne's coronation | Joseph Hergenröther |
| 5 | 830–850, Pseudo-Isidorian workshop, Corbie / Saint-Denis | **Johannes Fried** |
| 6 | Byzantine/oriental monastic authorship, hagiographic not ideological | Raymond Loenertz; Pietro De Leo |

**What it does to the script.** It converts a liability into material. We do not have to pick a
date and hope; we can say — accurately, and on the authority of a 2019 École française de Rome
survey — that after six centuries of work scholars have proposed **six** origins spanning a
century, and Fried's is the fifth of six. That is a stronger and more honest beat than any
asserted date, and it is on-brand: it is a HOW claim about the limits of the method, not a WHY
claim about the Church.

⚠ Note model 6: an Eastern/Byzantine origin thesis (Loenertz, De Leo) exists and we have nothing on
it. If the script says "Frankish or Roman", that is a false binary by this survey's reckoning.

### Q1-d. The Pseudo-Isidore plank under Fried has been pulled

**[SOURCED]** · locator: DOI 10.1086/689411
Eric Knibbs, "Ebo of Reims, Pseudo-Isidore, and the Date of the False Decretals", *Speculum* 92,
no. 1 (January 2017): 144–183.

**[INSPECTED]** — the MGH report of it, not the article · locator: `mgh.de/en/blog/post/answer-who-was-pseudo-isidore`, posted 2022-05-03
It reports Knibbs's MGH lecture of 28 April 2022.

**Why this matters more than its date suggests.** Fried does not date the Constitutum in a vacuum:
he places it in **the Pseudo-Isidorian forgers' workshop c. 830/833**, following Zechiel-Eckes's
Corbie/Paschasius Radbertus reconstruction. Knibbs argues against all three legs of that:
Pseudo-Isidore was **the 840s**, not the 830s; **clergy connected to Archbishop Ebbo of Reims**, not
Paschasius Radbertus; and **several houses across northern France**, not Corbie alone. The MGH — the
institution that publishes the critical edition — was platforming this position in 2022.

**Effect on Fried:** this does not disprove a Frankish Constitutum, and it is not a paper about the
Constitutum. But the specific workshop-and-date Fried anchors to is now itself contested by the
scholar the MGH invited to speak on it. Fried's dating therefore rests on a foundation that has
moved since 2007.

## Q2 — THE CUSA-PROTECTED-VALLA CLAIM

**Answer: nobody has sourced it, and the balance of recent scholarship points elsewhere. Fried
(p. 31) appears to have merged two different episodes a decade apart. Do not put this on screen.**

### Q2-a. What the most recent authoritative account says

**[INSPECTED]** · locator: `plato.stanford.edu/entries/lorenzo-valla/` § "Life and Works", read 2026-08-03
Lodi Nauta, "Lorenzo Valla", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. First published 14 May 2009;
**substantive revision 6 March 2026** — five months old, and by the author of our held A7.

On the 1444 Naples proceedings, Nauta writes that powerful men at court staged an inquisitorial
trial over Valla's works, and that Valla "was rescued from this perilous situation by the
intervention of the king" — Alfonso of Aragon. The trigger is given as Valla's denial of the
apostolic origin of the *Symbolum Apostolicum*.

**Cusa and Bessarion do not appear in Nauta's biographical section at all.** Nor do Nicholas V or
Calixtus III in the role Fried assigns them; the entry records only that Valla "made peace with the
pope" in 1447, became an apostolic *scriptor*, and a papal secretary in 1455.

### Q2-b. Where Cusa and Bessarion genuinely do appear — and it is not the Inquisition

**[SOURCED]** · locator: Renæssanceforum 11 (2016), pp. 23–39
Annet den Haan, "Valla on Biblical Scholarship: Metadiscourse at the Court of Nicholas V", in
*The Metadiscourse of Renaissance Humanism*, ed. A. den Haan, *Renæssanceforum* 11 (2016): 23–39.
Open access. **Read:** the article's own abstract/summary and the search-surfaced description, not
the body — the PDF would not render locally. Also mirrored at Utrecht:
https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/387671

The relationship den Haan documents is **Rome in the 1450s**, around the *Annotationes in Novum
Testamentum*: Valla, seeking backing for a contentious piece of biblical philology, **names
Cardinals Bessarion and Nicholas of Cusa among his supporters.** That is an intellectual
endorsement in a scholarly preface, roughly a decade after Naples — not a shield against an
inquisitor.

### Q2-c. Verdict

Fried's n. 94 documents Cusa's *arguments*, and that is all it documents; his protection sentence
is unsupported in his own apparatus, and no one found here has since supplied a source for it. Two
separate true things sit on either side of it — **Alfonso rescued Valla in 1444**, and **Cusa and
Bessarion backed Valla's biblical work in the 1450s** — and the claim reads like those two fused.

**Script consequence.** Cut any line in which Cusa shields Valla from the Inquisition. If the
protection beat is wanted, the defensible version is the king: the Donation treatise served
Alfonso's quarrel with the papacy over Naples, so Valla's protection was **political, from a
secular ruler with a territorial motive** — which is a better beat for this channel anyway, because
it is a mechanism rather than a sentiment. ⚠ That motive attribution is itself currently sourced
only to encyclopedic summaries; get it from Camporeale or Nauta's *In Defense of Common Sense* /
2009 book before it goes on screen.

**Cusanus-side search: negative.** Nothing found in Cusanus scholarship that sources the protection
claim. See NEGATIVE FINDINGS.

## TIER A — would change a claim

### A-1. Hiatt 2023 — a FOURTH contemporary critic the script does not have

**[SOURCED]** · locator: DOI 10.1093/hisres/htac031
Alfred Hiatt, "The *Donation of Constantine* in John Whethamstede's *Granarium*",
*Historical Research* 96, no. 272 (May 2023): 263–276. Oxford University Press / Institute of
Historical Research.

**Read:** abstract only (paywalled; £44 / 24h, or DeepDyve rental). Author page confirms the
citation independently (QMUL research publications, Alfred Hiatt).

**Why it is Tier A.** Our story is a three-critic story: Cusa (1433) → Valla (1440) → Pecock
(1449–55). Hiatt documents a **fourth, English, and earliest-band** critic: John Whethamstede,
abbot of St Albans, whose encyclopedia the *Granarium* (compiled in the **1430s**) contains a list
of reasons to doubt the Donation which — per the abstract — "differ in certain ways from" the
criticisms of Cusa, Valla and Pecock. Hiatt's stated conclusion is that scepticism about the
Donation "was more widespread and more deeply rooted at this time than has previously been
appreciated."

**Which claim it hits.** Any framing that treats the exposure as a small number of lone geniuses,
or that treats Cusa (1433) as the opening move, is now **at risk**. A 1430s St Albans encyclopedia
entry is contemporaneous with Cusa and predates Valla by roughly a decade. It does not refute our
narrative — Valla's philological demolition is still the decisive one — but it changes the
*shape* of the claim from "three men noticed" to "a diffuse scepticism, of which three men wrote
the sharpest versions."

**Also relevant to Q3 (Pecock):** Hiatt is the leading scholar of medieval English forgery
(*The Making of Medieval Forgeries*, 2004) and this is the newest work touching Pecock's Donation
material. It is the closest thing found to an update on Levine 1973.

**Acquire:** yes — item 2 in the acquisition table, and the only paid item that changes a claim.

**Read:** publisher/announcement descriptions and the Geschichtsquellen citation; not the book.
The Geschichtsquellen repertory cites **pp. 409–418** for the Constitutum specifically. Reviewed in
*Annales. Histoire, Sciences Sociales* (Cambridge Core) and in *Études* (Cairn, 2025 no. 11,
p. 131) — both reviews located, neither read.

**Why B not A.** It does not change a claim we make; it updates the frame. Bertrand's move is to
drop the fixed category "medieval forgery" in favour of **"régimes de fausseté"** — regimes of
falseness — and to read the false donation of Constantine alongside apocryphal gospels, false
relics, counterfeiters and falsified accounts as a single problem about knowledge, writing and
power. That is a live 2025 restatement of the terrain Grafton mapped, in French, from a
diplomatist rather than a classicist. If the script has a "forgery was normal, detection was the
novelty" beat, this is the current citation for it.
