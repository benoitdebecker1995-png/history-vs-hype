### (1) TIMELINE
- **1493:** Pope Alexander VI issues the Papal bull *Inter caetera*, dividing the New World between Spain and Portugal.
- **1494:** The Treaty of Tordesillas sets the dividing line; the Falklands fall on the western (Spanish) side.
- **1690:** Captain John Strong makes the first recorded landing and names the Falkland Sound.
- **1764:** Louis Antoine de Bougainville establishes the first permanent settlement, Port-Saint-Louis, for France.
- **1765:** Captain John Byron lands on Saunders Island and claims the archipelago for Great Britain.
- **1766:** France agrees to hand over Port-Saint-Louis to Spain; Britain establishes Port Egmont.
- **1767:** Spain takes control of Port-Saint-Louis, renaming it Puerto Soledad.
- **1770:** Spanish forces expel the British from Port Egmont, nearly precipitating a war.
- **1771:** An Anglo-Spanish agreement allows the British to return to Port Egmont without either side relinquishing sovereignty.
- **1774:** Britain withdraws from the islands due to economic pressures but leaves a plaque asserting sovereignty.
- **1811:** Spain withdraws its garrison from Puerto Soledad due to the Peninsular War and South American revolutions.
- **1820:** Colonel David Jewett claims the islands for the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata (Argentina).
- **1828:** Luis Vernet establishes a new settlement at Puerto Luis with both Argentine and British permission.
- **1829:** Buenos Aires appoints Vernet as Military and Civil Commander; Britain formally protests.
- **1831:** The USS *Lexington* raids Puerto Luis in retaliation for the seizure of American sealing vessels.
- **1833:** HMS *Clio* arrives; Captain James Onslow re-establishes British rule and the Argentine administration departs.
- **1850:** The Arana-Southern Treaty is signed, restoring "perfect relations" between the UK and Argentina.
- **1884:** Argentina resumes formal protests after a period of silence.
- **1965:** UN Resolution 2065 invites both nations to negotiate a peaceful solution to the dispute.
- **1982:** Argentina invades the islands on April 2; Britain recaptures them by June 14 (Falklands War).
- **1994:** Argentina amends its Constitution to include the recovery of the islands as a "permanent and unwavering goal."
- **2013:** Falkland Islanders vote 99.8% in a referendum to remain a British Overseas Territory.

### (2) KEY FIGURES
- **John Strong:** English captain who made the first recorded landing in 1690 and named the Falkland Sound.
- **Louis Antoine de Bougainville:** French explorer who founded the first settlement (Port-Saint-Louis) in 1764.
- **John Byron:** British captain who claimed the islands for King George III in 1765.
- **David Jewett:** American-born privateer who first raised the Argentine flag on the islands in 1820.
- **Luis Vernet:** Merchant-governor who led the Argentine settlement efforts from 1828 until the 1831 American raid.
- **James Onslow:** Commander of HMS *Clio* who oversaw the re-establishment of British control in 1833.
- **Lord Shackleton:** Author of a 1976 report that highlighted the islands' economic potential and self-sufficiency.
- **Leopoldo Galtieri:** Argentine General and dictator who ordered the 1982 invasion to bolster his regime.
- **Margaret Thatcher:** British Prime Minister who dispatched the task force to retake the islands in 1982.
- **Cristina Fernández de Kirchner:** Argentine President (2007–2015) who aggressively pursued the sovereignty claim in international forums.

### (3) KEY CLAIMS
- The UK asserts sovereignty based on first discovery, the 1765 settlement, and continuous administration since 1833.
- Argentina claims sovereignty as the successor state to Spain (*uti possidetis juris*) following its 1816 independence.
- Argentina argues the 1833 British return was an illegal act of force that violated its territorial integrity.
- The UK maintains that the principle of self-determination gives the islanders the right to choose their own government.
- Argentina contends that self-determination does not apply because the islanders are an "implanted population" of British citizens.
- The UK cites the 1850 Arana-Southern Treaty as a point where Argentina tacitly abandoned its claim by not mentioning it.
- Argentina points to its proximity to the islands and their location on its continental shelf as a basis for ownership.
- The UK argues that Spain's 1811 withdrawal left the islands effectively abandoned, allowing for the British return.

### (4) DEBATES
- **Acquiescence:** Historians debate whether the Arana-Southern Treaty (1850) constituted a legal "acquiescence" by Argentina to British rule.
- **Nootka Sound:** There is a historiographical disagreement over whether the 1790 Nootka Sound Convention effectively barred Britain from future settlements in the South Atlantic.
- **Succession:** Legal scholars dispute the validity of *uti possidetis juris* in this case, specifically whether a new state automatically inherits uninhabited colonial territories.
- **Population Status:** There is ongoing debate regarding the status of the population; Argentina views them as "transplanted," while the UK views them as a distinct "people" with rights.
- **Discovery:** Some historians question the "first discovery" claims, as early sightings by various European explorers are poorly documented and contested.

### (7) MODERN RELEVANCE
- **Constitutional Mandate:** The dispute remains a central pillar of Argentine foreign policy, enshrined in their 1994 Constitution as a permanent goal.
- **Resource Conflict:** Tensions frequently flare over natural resources, particularly oil drilling rights in the North Falkland Basin and fishing concessions.
- **Referendum Legitimacy:** The 2013 referendum continues to be used by the UK as a primary moral and legal justification for its presence, while Argentina dismisses it as a publicity stunt.
- **Regional Support:** Regional blocs like Mercosur and CELAC frequently issue statements supporting Argentina's call for sovereignty negotiations.
- **Strategic Positioning:** The islands' strategic importance has evolved with modern Antarctic claims and the management of South Atlantic maritime security.

## C6 ⭐⭐ — THE SAME SENTENCE, TWO OPPOSITE READINGS (candidate referee spine)
Both camps quote **Palmerston to Moreno, 8 August 1849** — the identical sentence — and read it in
opposite directions.

**The document (agreed by both camps):** "I have the honour to inform to you whatever the Newspapers
may have represented me as having said on the occasion above referred to, I have always understood
the matter in question to stand exactly in the way described by you in your letter."

- **Pascoe & Pepper, p.23:** "Palmerston did not say the question was 'unsettled' – indeed quite the
  reverse; he said it had been ended by Argentina's acquiescence." (Reading: Moreno's letter had
  quoted Palmerston's own "acquiescence of one party and the maintenance of the other" phrase, so
  "as described by you" ratifies the acquiescence.)
- **Kohen & Rodríguez, p.149:** the same sentence shows "there had been no Argentine consent or
  acquiescence. Therefore, the issue was not closed, but rather pending." (Reading: Moreno's letter
  was a *protest denying* acquiescence, so "as described by you" ratifies the protest.)

**Why the ambiguity is genuine, not manufactured:** Moreno's letter of 31 July 1849 contained BOTH
(a) a verbatim quotation of Palmerston's acquiescence phrase AND (b) Moreno's own denial of
acquiescence — "the silence of this Office should not be taken at any time as confirmation of the
wrongful assertion" (Kohen p.148). So "exactly in the way described by you" can genuinely point at
either. **The sentence is structurally undecidable on its own text.**

## C8 ⭐⭐⭐ — ONSLOW'S OWN DESPATCH, 19 Jan 1833 ✅ [P] T1 — PRIMARY, READ DIRECTLY
**Source:** G.T. Whitington, *The Falkland Islands* (London, 1840), printed **pp.16–17**, reproducing
Onslow's report to Rear-Admiral Sir Thomas Baker, Monte Video, 19 January 1833.
**File:** `_research/exhibits/Whitington-The-Falkland-Islands.pdf` (PDF page 14 = printed 16–17;
scanned two-up, no OCR layer). **Rendered with PyMuPDF at 170dpi and read directly** — this is a
first-hand read of the document, NOT an NLM synthesis and NOT a scholar's paraphrase.
Also in notebook as `ab32a0c1`.

### Verbatim (transcribed from the page image)
- Arrival: *"I arrived on the 2nd January, 1833, and found a settlement under a Buenos Ayrean flag,
  with **twenty-five soldiers**, and also a national schooner of war under the same flag."*
- The demand: *"I acquainted him civilly with the object of my mission, and requested him to embark
  his force and haul down his flag on shore, he being in a possession belonging to the crown of
  Great Britain."*
- His stated purpose, put in writing: *"I came to these Islands **to exercise the right of
  sovereignty over them**, and declined any further written communication on the subject."*
- ⭐ Pinedo's request: *"On the morning of the 3rd instant, at 5 A.M. he visited me, to request me to
  allow the Buenos Ayrean flag to be kept flying on shore till Saturday the 5th, when he would
  finally sail, taking with him **the force and such of the settlers as expressed a desire to leave
  the Island**."*
- The flag: *"Finding he wavered, and was reluctant to strike the flag, I immediately landed, hoisted
  the Union, and caused it to be lowered, sending it with a civil message on board the national
  schooner."*
- ⭐⭐ Onslow's own count-language: *"great insubordination existed in this settlement, which paralyzed
  the settlers; and had we not arrived might have led to worse consequences, as there were **a great
  many bad characters who left in the national schooner**."*
- Earlier Port Egmont visit (Dec 1832): *"Not finding any inhabitants on Saunders Island or in the
  vicinity of the harbour, I caused a signal-post to be erected, and repaired what appeared to me to
  have been Fort George"*; inscription left: *"Visited by H.B.M. sloop Clio, for the purpose of
  exercising the right of sovereignty over these Islands, 23rd December, 1832."*

### Why this matters — it cuts BOTH camps
1. **Against Pascoe (C2/C3):** Pascoe reconstructs from this same despatch that *"only four"* genuine
   residents left. But Onslow himself writes that **"a great many bad characters who left in the
   national schooner"**, and records Pinedo sailing with *"the force **and such of the settlers as
   expressed a desire to leave**."* The commander's own contemporaneous language describes a
   settler departure, not a garrison-only removal. Pascoe's "only four" is a *reconstruction from
   name-lists*, not what the despatch says in its own words.
2. **Against Kohen (C4):** Onslow's phrasing is *"such of the settlers as expressed a desire to
   leave"* — i.e. framed as departure by election, which is evidence for the "free choice" reading
   Kohen dismisses as meaningless. And the settlement is described as already collapsing
   (mutiny, the commander Mestivier shot, *"great insubordination… which paralyzed the settlers"*)
   **before** the Clio arrived.
3. **Numerical discrepancy:** Onslow says **twenty-five** soldiers; Pascoe says **26**. Trivial alone,
   but it shows the counting problem starts in the primary source itself.

### The Act-1 beat this unlocks
The crop-test writes itself: **one despatch, three incompatible readings.**
EVIDENCE = Onslow's despatch → BRITISH crop ("only the garrison; only four residents left") →
ARGENTINE crop ("53 sailed; 70% of the population evicted") → **THE DOCUMENT ON SCREEN**, where the
British commander writes "a great many bad characters who left" and "such of the settlers as expressed
a desire to leave" → the QUALIFICATION both sides cut: the settlement was already in armed collapse,
its commander murdered by his own soldiers, weeks before any British ship appeared.

### ⭐⭐⭐ C9a — THE DECISIVE PASSAGE (p.1195). Neither camp quotes this.
The Argentine government's own contemporaneous report of what Onslow told Pinedo. Onslow said he had
positive orders:

> "to hoist the British Flag thereon within 24 hours, as he had already done in other Ports of the
> said Islands ; **to give a passage in one of his Vessels to the Officer and Troops stationed there,
> and to the other Inhabitants**; and to cause every thing belonging to Buenos Ayres to be embarked
> and sent away"

**Why this is decisive:** the whole British case (C2) rests on "only the garrison was removed." Here,
in the *Argentine government's* record of the *British commander's own words*, Onslow offers passage
to the troops **and to the other Inhabitants**, and undertakes "to cause every thing belonging to
Buenos Ayres to be embarked and sent away." That is a stated intention to clear the settlement, not
merely the garrison — and it is reported by the side Pascoe accuses of distortion, in 1833, in the
British government's own published State Papers.
⚠ HONESTY GUARD: this is Pinedo's account of Onslow's *spoken* words, relayed via his government —
not Onslow's own text. Onslow's written note (C9b) says only "withdraw your Forces." **The spoken and
written versions differ, and that gap is itself the finding.** Do not present C9a as Onslow's writing.

### C9b — Onslow's written note to Pinedo, 2 January 1833 (p.1197), verbatim
> "I HAVE to acquaint you that I have received directions from His Excellency the Commander-in-Chief
> of His Britannic Majesty's Ships and Vessels of War, on the South American Station, **to exercise
> the rights of Sovereignty over these Islands**, in the name of His Britannic Majesty. It is my
> intention to hoist, to-morrow morning, the National Flag of Great Britain on shore ; when I request
> you will be pleased to haul down your Flag, and **to withdraw your Forces, taking with you all the
> stores, &c., belonging to your Government**."
> — J. J. ONSLOW, Commander
✅ **This independently CONFIRMS Kohen's citation** (he cites BFSP 1832-33 T.XX p.1197 — exact match).
Kohen's sourcing checks out.

### C9c — Both sides agree the settlement was in armed collapse (p.1195)
The Argentine government states it itself:
> "at a time when, **owing to the insubordination of a few Soldiers of the Garrison, the Chief of the
> Establishment had been killed**, and good order disturbed (in the restoration of which the Naval
> Commander was occupied when the Clio arrived ;)"
→ The mutiny and the murder of Mestivier are **not** a British invention (cf. C8, Onslow's
"great insubordination… which paralyzed the settlers"). Both governments record it contemporaneously.
This kills any framing of a peaceful settlement violently interrupted — while equally not excusing the
seizure.

### C9d — Pinedo appointed a successor IN WRITING before leaving (p.1196)
> "conferring the command of the Establishment, **in writing**, on the Superintendant of the
> Establishment, **Don Juan Simon**, who was to remain, with some others."
→ Directly complicates Pascoe (C2), who says of the same man: "Whether Simon agreed to this or not,
he certainly never attempted to act as such." The Argentine record says the appointment was formal
and written. **Whether Simon ACTED is a separate question from whether he was APPOINTED** — Pascoe

### C9e — The flag sequence (p.1196)
> "Commandant Pinedo… adopted, among other measures, prior to setting sail, that of **prohibiting the
> People on the Island from lowering the Argentine Flag**"
> "At 9 o'clock in the morning of the 3rd, 3 boats manned with Seamen and Marines, from the English
> Sloop, landed at the point of Port Louis, and, placing a Flag-staff **at the house of an Englishman,
> about 4 squares distant from the Commandantcy**, they hoisted thereon the British Flag, and then
> proceeded to strike that of the Republic"

## C10 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ — THE DE BERNHARDT MEMORANDUM: the FO fact-checks its own Foreign Secretary ✅ [P] T1
**Source:** *Memorandum respecting the Falkland Islands*, **CONFIDENTIAL (9755)**, by **Gaston de

### ⭐⭐⭐⭐ C10a — THE FOOTNOTE (p.15). The single best document beat in the project.
The main text reproduces **Lord Aberdeen's despatch to Mr Parish, 8 August 1829**, asserting British
rights over the islands — *"the claims which His Majesty had **constantly\*** asserted to the

De Bernhardt attaches a footnote to the word **"constantly"**:

> "\* **I have been unable to trace any previous claim to the sovereignty of the whole group of the
> Falkland Islands put forward by His Majesty's Government either to the Spanish or Argentine
> Governments**; but on the 15th March, 1771, Lord Rochford requested the Admiralty to instruct
> Captain Stott, who commanded the expedition sent to retake possession of those islands, that
> 'if the Spanish commander should make any protest against His Majesty's right to Port Egmont or the
> Falkland Islands he should answer the same by a counter protest, in proper terms, of His Majesty's
> right to the whole of the said islands, and against the right of His Catholic Majesty to any part of
> the same.' As Captain Stott reported that the transaction had been 'effected with the greatest
> appearance of good faith without the least claim or reserve being made by the Spanish officer in
> behalf of his Court,' **there does not appear to have been any occasion for Captain Stott to
> deliver his message**."

**What this is:** the Foreign Office's own researcher, working inside the FO archives in 1910,
footnoting his own Foreign Secretary's 1829 claim and reporting that **he cannot find it** — and that
the single instruction he *can* find (Rochford → Stott, 1771) was **never actually delivered**,
because Spain never protested.
→ Britain's internal record does not corroborate Britain's public assertion. Perpetrator's own
paperwork. Filmable: a printed CONFIDENTIAL memo where the footnote undoes the sentence above it.

### C10b — Britain's own careful abandonment language, 1829 (pp.15–16)

> "it could not be maintained that **the abandonment of the islands in 1774** by His Majesty's
> Government was in any degree to be considered as tending to invalidate His Majesty's just
> pretensions to them, as the naval force had been withdrawn and the settlement at Port Egmont
> relinquished **in pursuance of a system of economical retrenchment** adopted at that time."
> "all the symbols of possession and property had been left upon the islands; that when the Governor
> took his departure **the British flag had remained flying**"
> whether to "resume possession of settlement, which had been **relinquished for a time, although
> never abandoned**"
> protesting against the 1829 Decrees "as infringing those just rights over the Falkland Islands which
> **His Majesty had never relinquished**."
→ Directly serves candidate angle #1 (mutual abandonment). Britain, internally, in 1829, is drawing a
careful legal line between *relinquished* and *abandoned* — and de Bernhardt uses the word
"abandonment" for 1774 in the same breath.

## C15 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ — WELLINGTON'S LETTER, 25 JULY 1829, IN FULL ✅ [P] T1 — PRIMARY, READ DIRECTLY

**Volume VI** (London, 1877), pp. 48–49, letter no. **[1390]**. Public domain.
**File:** `_research/exhibits/wtest-sc_0000999594_00000001411221.txt` (archive.org full text; a second

### C15a — The setup: the Colonial Secretary proposes seizing the moment (p.41)
**Sir George Murray (Secretary of State for War and the Colonies) → Wellington**, Downing Street,
23 July 1829:
> "I should be very glad if you would have the kindness to look over the accompanying papers
> respecting the Falkland Islands, and consider of the propriety of some step being taken towards the
> formation of an establishment there. **It appears to me that the interval between the cessation of
> the power of Old Spain and the consolidation of that of the new governments in South America would
> be the best time for our resuming our former possession of the Falkland Islands.** I have not spoken
> with Lord Melville on the subject; but I believe he is very sensible of the importance, in a naval
> point of view, of the occupation of those islands."

### C15b ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ — WELLINGTON'S REPLY IN FULL (pp.48–49)
**Wellington → Sir George Murray**, Walmer Castle, **25 July 1829**:
> "**I have perused the enclosed papers respecting the Falkland Islands. It is not clear to me that we
> have ever possessed the sovereignty of all these islands.** The Convention certainly goes no further
> than to restore to us Port Egmont, **which we abandoned nearly sixty years ago**. If our right to
> the Falkland Islands had been undisputed at that time, and indisputable, I confess that I should
> doubt the expediency of now taking possession of them. We have possession of nearly every valuable
> post and colony in the world, and I confess that **I am anxious to avoid exciting the attention and
> jealousy of other Powers by extending our possessions and setting the example of the gratification
> of a desire to seize upon new territories. But in this case, in which our right to possess more than
> Port Egmont is disputed, and at least doubtful, it is very desirable to avoid such acts.**
> I am at the same time very sensible of the inconvenience which may be felt by this country, and of
> the injury which will be done to us **if either the French or Americans should settle upon these
> Islands**… That which I would recommend is that the government of Buenos Ayres should be **very
> quietly, but very distinctly, informed that his Majesty has claims upon the Falkland Islands**, and
> that his Majesty will not allow of any settlement upon, or any cession to individuals or foreign
> nations of these Islands by Buenos Ayres, which shall be inconsistent with the King's acknowledged
> right of sovereignty. I think that this is all that can be done at present."

### ⚠ TEXTUAL CORRECTION TO BECK — WE NOW HAVE THE BETTER TEXT
Beck (C14a) renders it: *"It is not at all clear to me that we have ever possessed the sovereignty of
these Islands."*
The 1877 published text reads: *"**It is not clear to me that we have ever possessed the sovereignty
of all these islands.**"*
**Two differences: no "at all", and the word "all".** Both matter. "All these islands" is materially
weaker than "these Islands" — Wellington is doubting sovereignty over **the whole archipelago**, and
explicitly concedes the 1771 Convention restored **Port Egmont only**. Beck's version overstates.
→ **USE THE 1877 TEXT ON SCREEN, NOT BECK'S.** This is exactly the footnote-laundering failure Rule 1
exists to catch: had we quoted Beck's rendering on a quote card, we would have put a subtly
mis-transcribed sentence on screen under a primary-source label.
*(Caveat: OCR of an 1877 scan — "Port Gumont" is plainly OCR corruption of "Port Egmont". Verify
character-exact against the page image before the card is cut. Two independent archive.org copies
agree on the wording.)*

### Why this is the most valuable document in the project
1. **It is the earliest and highest-ranking doubt yet found** — the sitting Prime Minister, 1829,
   four years before the seizure. Earlier than de Bernhardt (1910), Spicer, Fitzmaurice, Troutbeck.
2. **It is not a lawyer's technical caveat — it is a strategic decision.** Wellington doubts the
   title, says so plainly, and *then* recommends asserting the claim anyway — "very quietly, but very
   distinctly" — expressly to block the French and Americans. The mechanism the whole FO chain repeats
   (Fitzmaurice 1936: "for strategic reasons we cannot give them up… adopt a hard line") is already
   fully formed **in 1829, in the Prime Minister's own hand.**
3. **He concedes the 1774 withdrawal as abandonment**: "Port Egmont, **which we abandoned** nearly
   sixty years ago" — the Prime Minister using the word Britain's later diplomacy fought to avoid
   (cf. C10b, Aberdeen's "relinquished for a time, although never abandoned", written *fourteen days
   later*, 8 August 1829).
4. **The Murray letter (C15a) supplies the motive in writing**: settle now because Spain has collapsed
   and the new South American states have not yet consolidated. Colonial opportunism, stated plainly,
   two days before Wellington's reply.
5. **HONESTY GUARD — this cuts both ways.** Wellington also asserts "his Majesty has claims" and "the
   King's acknowledged right of sovereignty" in the same letter. He is not renouncing; he is
   distinguishing a weak-but-real claim from a clean title, and choosing quiet assertion over
   occupation. **Do NOT script this as "Britain admitted it had no right."** He says the claim to
   *all* the islands is doubtful, not that there is no claim at all.

## C17 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ — THE 1946 FOREIGN OFFICE MEMORANDUM, IN EXTENDED VERBATIM ✅ [S→P] T1

### C17a ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ — The FO audits its own case, item by item (Beck pp.66–67)
> "**Discovery** — The evidence for British priority of discovery is **unsatisfactory, but
> irrelevant**.
> **Occupation** — The British case for priority of occupation over the French (1764) is
> **untenable**, since the British occupation was not made effective until 1766…
> **The Secret Understanding of 1770** — There is no evidence to support the belief that a verbal
> pledge to withdraw from the Falklands was given by Lord North in November 1770. **It cannot,
> however, be wholly proven … To accept the fact of a secret pledge is definitely to demolish the
> British case prior to 1833.**
> **The leaden plaque of 1774** — … the British did not return to the Falkland Islands for 60 years,
> **during which their claim may be deemed to have lapsed**.
> **Spanish Withdrawal 1811** — … the Falkland Islands … in the British view, became *terra nullius* …
> **Non-effective occupation by Argentina** — The Falkland Islands were unoccupied between 1811 and
> 1826 … In their decree of the 10th June 1829 the Buenos Aires Government admitted that
> 'circumstances' had hitherto prevented any real exercise of sovereignty …
> **Prescription 1833–1946** — Great Britain has been in formal possession and effective occupation of
> the Falklands since 1833; at that date the Islands were ineffectively occupied by the Buenos Aires
> Government… **In this sense, the British occupation of 1833 was, at the time, an act of
> unjustifiable aggression which has now acquired the backing of the rights of prescription.**"

**This is transformative.** The famous "unjustifiable aggression" line is not an outburst — it is the
**conclusion of a systematic self-audit** in which the Foreign Office marks its own arguments
*unsatisfactory*, *untenable*, and *lapsed*, one after another. And critically it also **audits
Argentina's case and finds it wanting** (non-effective occupation 1811–26; Buenos Aires' own 1829
decree conceding no real exercise of sovereignty).
→ **Perfectly balanced primary evidence. The document damages both cases in the same breath.**

### C17b ⭐⭐⭐⭐ — WHY BRITAIN CANNOT PUBLICLY ARGUE ITS BEST ARGUMENT (Beck p.58)
**William Beckett**, FO legal adviser, October 1946, defining the terms:
> "**Occupation** is a method of acquiring title to territory which is *terra nullius* (no-man's land),
> and **prescription** is a means of acquiring territory which **originally belonged to somebody else**
> and whose title is destroyed by the fact that the acquirer has successfully maintained possession."
Beck then notes the 1983 House of Commons Foreign Affairs Committee found:
> "**no legal argument in support of this prescriptive right has been formally advanced by the United
> Kingdom Government**" — and Beck's explanation: "**perhaps because of the manner in which
> prescription suggests an originally weak, even illegal, title.**"
→ ⭐⭐⭐ **THE MECHANISM OF THE WHOLE STORY.** Britain's strongest legal argument is prescription. But
prescription *by definition* means acquiring land that belonged to someone else. To argue it in public
is to concede the original taking was defective. **So Britain argues length of possession and
self-determination instead — not because they are stronger, but because they are sayable.**
This is HOW > WHY in its purest form and is the best single explanatory beat in the project.

### C17c ⭐⭐⭐ — THE FOREIGN OFFICE EDITED CHATHAM HOUSE (Beck pp.65–66)
> "In October 1936… the **Royal Institute of International Affairs (RIIA)** … decided to consult the
> Foreign Office about the draft of its forthcoming volume on South America. In response, **the
> Foreign Office urged textual changes** of sections likely to be exploited by Argentina as emanating
> from a respected and '**semi-official source**'. **The RIIA agreed to replace two phrases** — these
> stated that '**Great Britain annexed the Falkland Islands from Argentina in 1833**' and '**annexed
> them by force in 1833**' — with a more neutral form of words; thus, *The Republics of South America*,
> published in 1937, indicated that '**in 1833 HMS Clio took the opportunity of occupying the islands
> in the name of His Britannic Majesty and struck the Argentine flag**'."
→ ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THE RIIA IS CHATHAM HOUSE.** In 1936 the Foreign Office got Chatham House to delete the
word "annexed" and the phrase "by force." **In 2026, Chatham House published Marc Weller's "Who owns
the Falkland Islands?" (C12), which states "Britain did not in fact use force."**
Ninety years apart, the same institution, the same proposition. **This is the single most striking
continuity in the entire dossier** — and it is documented on both ends: Beck for 1936, the live
article for 2026.
⚠⚠ **HONESTY GUARD — MANDATORY.** This is NOT evidence that Weller was influenced, briefed, or is
repeating a line. Chatham House in 2026 is not the RIIA of 1936; Weller is an independent scholar who

posed as an irony, never as an accusation.** If this beat cannot be written without implying collusion,

## C24 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ — CONFIRMED AND CLOSED: BRITAIN NEVER PROPOSED ARBITRATION ON THE FALKLANDS

### C27b ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ — DOLZER DIRECTLY REBUTS THE "NO SHOTS FIRED" ARGUMENT (p.106/120)
This is the single most useful cross-reference of the sweep — a direct legal answer to Weller/Chatham

> shot was fired does not change this assessment. The credible threat of the use of force is treated

### C27d — Dolzer on *uti possidetis* and the 1833 threat-of-force framing (supporting)

### 🛑🛑 C38b — THE STRONGEST COMPETING EXPLANATION FOR WHY NEITHER SIDE LITIGATES
This project's spine holds that neither government would risk a court **because each privately
doubted its case**. Greig offers a different explanation, argued at length: **the dispute may not be
justiciable at all.** Two grounds:

**(1) No formulation either side could accept.**
> "it would be difficult to formulate the issue other than in terms of whether the Islands 'belong'
> to A or to B. As the Argentine objective is to secure the return of the Islands, **a decision in
> favour of Britain would be even less acceptable than the decision in favour of Chile in the Beagle
> Channel arbitration.**" And a decision for Argentina without safeguards for the islanders "would
> therefore be unacceptable to Britain."

**(2) Choice of law — there is no agreed body of law to apply.**
> "the choice of law and the scale of favourability would be between two combinations of factors. On
> the one hand, the British claim… conquest or physical occupation alone as a basis of title,
> reinforced by the wishes of the inhabitants; while the Argentine position… the no conquest and
> *uti possidetis* principles… reinforced by the argument that the present population is the means
> whereby Britain has carried out its illegal occupation. **A tribunal would therefore have a range
> of alternatives based upon choosing between two rival concepts of international law.**"

→ ⚠️ **This is a serious, competing, non-conspiratorial explanation of the same behaviour**, and it
converges with three others already filed: **Fawcett** — the dispute is "essentially a political

the end consider the risks of any legal proceeding too great" (C35b).

## C40 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ — THE DISCRIMINATING TEST: BRITAIN LITIGATED AN ALMOST IDENTICAL DISPUTE, IN THE SAME YEARS, AND WON
**Date:** 2026-07-27. Target 3 from the stated collection plan: *does anything discriminate between
the two competing explanations for Britain's non-litigation — private doubt about title (C10–C30) vs
the dispute being non-justiciable (C38b, Greig/Fawcett/Sinclair/Weller)?*
**Method:** if Britain avoided international courts on territorial sovereignty generally, the
non-justiciability reading is favoured. If it litigated comparable disputes freely and refused only
this one, the behaviour is **title-specific**.

### C40a — THE FACTS, VERIFIED [P] T1
**Minquiers and Ecrehos (United Kingdom v France)**, International Court of Justice:
| | |
|---|---|
| Special Agreement notified to the Court | **6 December 1951** |
| Judgment | **17 November 1953** |
| Outcome | **Sovereignty awarded to the United Kingdom** |
| Subject | territorial ownership of **two groups of small islets** between Jersey and the French coast |
| Question put to the Court | which party "had produced **the more convincing proof of title**" |
| Evidence relied on | medieval treaties (1066, 1204) **and** subsequent exercise of State authority |
Source: ICJ case record, `icj-cij.org/case/17`. Corroborated in the corpus by **Greig** (*"the
International Court based its decision in favour of Britain on the finding 'that the British
authorities during the greater part of the nineteenth century have exercised State functions'"*),
**Dolzer** (ICJ Reports 1953 pp.47, 66, 67) and **Calvert**.

### ⭐ C40b — THE OVERLAP IS EXACT
Same government, same years, three territorial disputes, **three different postures**:
| Date | Territory | British posture |
|---|---|---|
| **6 Dec 1951** | Minquiers & Ecrehos (v France) | **Special Agreement to let the ICJ decide sovereignty** |
| **1947, 1951, 1953** (Belstead, C33c) | Falkland Islands **Dependencies** (v Argentina) | **Invited Argentina to the ICJ** |
| throughout | **The Falkland Islands** | **"never proposed it"** (Belstead); "we have never sought a ruling on the Falkland Islands themselves" (Thatcher, C33a) |

→ **Britain was not court-averse on territorial sovereignty in this period. It was Falklands-averse.**
The shape of the Minquiers dispute is close to the Falklands: small islands, competing ancient titles,
resolved on possession and exercise of State authority. Britain submitted that one voluntarily and won.

### 🛑 C40c — BUT THIS CUTS IN A DIRECTION I DID NOT EXPECT, AND IT SHARPENS THE THESIS
The naive reading — *"Britain refused because it knew it would lose"* — is **weakened**, not
strengthened, by the case law:
- **Island of Palmas (1928)**, **Clipperton (1931)**, **Eastern Greenland (1933)**, **Minquiers
  (1953)** all developed the doctrine of *effectivités* in a direction favourable to **long,
  peaceful, continuous possession** — which is Britain's strongest asset.
- **Calvert** (British-sympathetic) explicitly deploys Minquiers *for* Britain: Argentine claims
  "would be held void, as were the French claims in the Minquiers and Ecrehos Case and the American
  claims in the Island of Palmas Arbitration."
- This is exactly **Sinclair's** point at C37b — doubts expressed before 1928 predate the law that
  now favours Britain.

→ **So by 1953 Britain had a better legal position than in 1936, had just won an analogous case, and
still never put the Falklands to a court.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ C40d — THE HONEST FORM OF THE THESIS, WHICH IS STRONGER THAN THE ONE I HAD
Not: *"Britain refused because it knew it would lose."* The 1950s case law does not support that.
Instead: **Britain refused even when the law had moved in its favour and it had just won a comparable
case at the same court in the same years.** Whatever explains the refusal, it is not a simple
prediction of defeat — and it is not general aversion to judicial settlement either, because Minquiers
disproves that.
→ This is more defensible, more interesting, and it survives Sinclair's and Greig's rebuttals rather
than colliding with them. **It also leaves the motive genuinely open**, which is where the evidence
actually stands (C38c).

### ⚠️ C40e — MANDATORY COUNTER-READS. The comparison is suggestive, NOT dispositive.
1. **France is not Argentina.** Minquiers was litigated against a friendly state with a shared legal
   culture, no domestic political stake and every capacity to accept defeat. Greig's objection (C38b)
   is precisely that a Falklands ruling would be **unacceptable to whichever side lost** — and he
   cites the Beagle Channel award, which Argentina rejected, as the proof. Thatcher makes the same
   point (C33b). **This is a real distinction and it may be sufficient on its own.**
2. **Population.** The Minquiers and Ecrehos are uninhabited. The Falklands are not, and Britain
   treats the islanders' wishes as decisive — so a judicial ruling could conflict with the
   self-determination position Britain has built its case on since the 1930s (C14b). A state may
   rationally decline to litigate a question whose answer it would feel bound to override.
3. **Timing.** Britain's documented refusals cluster in **1930 and 1936** (C24b, C18a) — *before*
   Minquiers and before the doctrine matured. The 1950s silence is an **absence of proposal**, not a
   documented refusal. **Absence of evidence here is not evidence of a decision.**

## C41 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ — ISLAND OF PALMAS (HUBER, 1928) READ DIRECTLY: THE DOCTRINE THE WHOLE DISPUTE RUNS ON ✅ [P] T1
**Date:** 2026-07-27. Target 5 from the stated collection plan. **Three lawyers in this corpus treat
this award as the governing authority — Sinclair (C37b), Greig (C38d), Weller (C35) — and this
project had never opened it.**
**Source:** *Island of Palmas case (Netherlands, USA)*, sole arbitrator **Max Huber**, Permanent Court
of Arbitration, award **4 April 1928**. **Reports of International Arbitral Awards, Vol. II,
pp.829–871** — the United Nations' own official publication. **FREE.**
Files: `_research/exhibits/Island-of-Palmas-1928-RIAA-II-829-871.pdf` and `.txt` (full text layer,
44pp, extracted and searchable). Read directly — no intermediary.

---

### C41a — THE INTERTEMPORAL RULE, BOTH LIMBS (RIAA II p.845)
**Limb 1 — creation of rights:**
> "Both Parties are also agreed that **a juridical fact must be appreciated in the light of the law
> contemporary with it, and not of the law in force at the time when a dispute in regard to it arises
> or falls to be settled.**"

**Limb 2 — existence of rights. This is the one nobody quotes, and it is the important one:**
> "As regards the question which of different legal systems prevailing at successive periods is to be
> applied in a particular case (the so-called intertemporal law), **a distinction must be made between
> the creation of rights and the existence of rights. The same principle which subjects the act
> creative of a right to the law in force at the time the right arises, demands that the existence of
> the right, in other words its continued manifestation, shall follow the conditions required by the
> evolution of law.**"

→ **Limb 1** protects Britain from being judged for 1833 by 2026 standards.
→ **Limb 2** requires that a title keep meeting the standards of each later era. **It cuts both
ways**, and every party in this dossier has quoted only the limb that suits them.

### C41b — "AS GOOD AS A TITLE", AND MAINTENANCE IS AS DEMANDING AS ACQUISITION (pp.839–840)
> "practice, as well as doctrine, recognizes… that **the continuous and peaceful display of
> territorial sovereignty (peaceful in relation to other States) is as good as a title**."
> "The growing insistence with which international law, ever since the middle of the 18th century, has
> demanded that the occupation shall be effective **would be inconceivable, if effectiveness were
> required only for the act of acquisition and not equally for the maintenance of the right.**"

And, decisively for a prior-title claim:
> "an inchoate title could not prevail over the continuous and peaceful display of authority by
> another State; for **such display may prevail even over a prior, definitive title put forward by
> another State**." (p.846)
> "**The title of contiguity**, understood as a basis of territorial sovereignty, **has no foundation
> in international law.**" (p.869)

### C41c — DISCOVERY ALONE IS WORTH LITTLE (pp.845–846)
> "an inchoate title of discovery **must be completed within a reasonable period by the effective
> occupation** of the region claimed to be discovered."
→ Bears on **both** claims: Britain's 1690 Strong landing, and Spain's papal/Tordesillas title that
Argentina inherits. It is also precisely what the FO's 1946 memorandum concluded on its own account —
discovery "unsatisfactory, but **irrelevant**" (C17a). **The Foreign Office was applying Palmas.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ C41d — WHAT THIS DOES TO THE FO-DOUBT CHAIN. A GENUINE REFRAMING, AND IT IS FAIRER TO BRITAIN.
This project has read the 1930s rebuild of the British case — from pre-1833 discovery/settlement onto
**prescription and continuous possession** (C14b, C17a) — as *embarrassment management*: the old case
was indefensible, so a sayable one was built.

**Palmas supplies an innocent and better explanation.** In 1928 the leading arbitrator in the field
held that continuous and peaceful display **is as good as a title** and **may defeat even a prior
definitive title**. Britain's strongest asset is exactly that. A competent legal adviser reading the
award in 1928–30 would *have to* re-base the case on prescription. **That is not spin; it is
compliance with new authority.**
→ This is **Sinclair's argument** (C37b) and it is now grounded in the award itself rather than taken
on his word. **Any beat presenting the 1930s rebuild as a cover-up must carry this, or it is unfair
and wrong.**
→ Note the chronology fits precisely: Palmas **April 1928**; Field memorandum **Feb 1928**; Robertson's
"weakness of ours" **Dec 1927**; Fitzmaurice **1930**; the rebuild **early 1930s** (C14b). The doubts
*precede* the award; the rebuild *follows* it.

### 🛑 C41e — BUT THE SAME AWARD SUPPLIES THE STRONGEST ARGUMENT AGAINST BRITAIN
Two words in Huber's formula do enormous work, and both are contestable here:
1. **"PEACEFUL"** — the display must be "peaceful **in relation to other States**". Argentina's
   protests, if legally effective, defeat peacefulness. This is exactly **Dolzer's** argument, which
   uses Britain's *own* pleadings in the Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries case to establish the effective-
   protest standard (C27d). **Palmas is the authority Dolzer is building on.**
2. **"MAINTENANCE"** — effectiveness is required "equally for the maintenance of the right." Britain
   displayed nothing on the islands between **1774 and 1833** — fifty-nine years. Under Huber's own
   rule that is a maintenance failure, which is the doctrinal basis for the abandonment reading now
   reached independently by Dolzer (C27c), Wellington's own words (C15b), and two competitor channels
   (C22c, C32e).
→ And **Limb 2** opens the door the other way too: if a title must keep meeting evolving law, then
the evolution of decolonisation and self-determination norms after 1960 is a live test the British
title must keep passing — which is Weller's framing (C35) and Greig's "changing attitudes" (C38a).

### C41f — THE HONEST SUMMARY
**Palmas does not settle the Falklands. It explains why the dispute is unsettleable on these facts.**
The same award gives Britain its best argument (continuous display beats prior title) and Argentina
its best argument (protest defeats peacefulness; abandonment defeats maintenance). Which prevails
depends on findings of fact — whether Argentine protest was continuous and effective, and what the
1774–1833 gap means — that no tribunal has ever made.
→ **This is the strongest support yet for the non-justiciability reading (C38b), and it comes from
the doctrine rather than from any party's advocacy.**

### 🛑 C43e — MANDATORY GUARDS AGAINST THESIS-FITTING (the agent flagged these; they bind)
1. **Attlee, 23 Feb 1948 (HC vol 447 cc1600–1) is NOT a conflation.** Two days *before* Bevin, he
   said "we proposed that **the matter** should be brought to the International Court" — unscoped
   wording, but the question named the Dependencies and the substance is unambiguous. Same for
   **Mayhew, 23 Mar 1949** ("this question"). **The loose shorthand and the precise formulation were
   born in the same week. Presenting either as a conflation would be thesis-fitting.**
   → This kills any "one dropped word" narrative for a second time. **There is no moment the word was
   dropped** — it was routinely absent from the shorthand and routinely present in the substance.
   What changed in 1968 is that **nobody corrected it.**
2. **The Earl of Perth (HL 1 Dec 1948) is not a government statement.** A backbench peer; his ICJ
   sentence is at **c708, not c707** as Lorton has it; the referent is a three-item list mixing the
   Falklands, Antarctica and British Honduras; and it was neither repeated nor corrected — it died
   on the floor.
3. **Do not say the conflation "began" on 26 Mar 1968.** The defensible claim: *this is the earliest
   ministerial conflation findable in Hansard across two full-text sweeps, and it is the one Beck
   himself points to.*
4. **Do not use Lorton's gloss of Stewart's 11 Dec 1968 statement.** The ICJ is **not mentioned once**
   in it (string count 0), and Stewart's actual words ("The talks do not apply to either of those")
   are ambiguous.
