## 1. The doctrinal heart — the Court read a procedure manual as a property code
**Two categories of land under Spanish/Mexican law:**
- *Tierras realengas / baldías* — the **sovereign's** ungranted public domain → passes to successor state (US) under international law.
- *Tierras concejiles / propios* — the **community's own** lands: *ejido* (multipurpose), *dehesa* (fenced pasture), *montes* (timber/firewood), *propios* (town-rented). Ebright: the community grant **owned** these.

**The Court's sources (1897):**
- **Frederic Hall, *The Laws of Mexico* (1885)** — published ONE YEAR before; Ebright: "the language quoted from Hall was **not supported by any reference to Spanish or Mexican law**."
- **Recopilación de las Leyes de los Reynos de las Indias (1681)** — good on settlement *procedure*, but "had **little to say about the substantive law concerning ownership of property**."
- Fuller's holding: "**Under the laws of the Indies, lands not actually allotted to settlers remained the property of the king**, to be disposed of by him." (*Sandoval*, quoted in Ebright p.48)

**What the Court IGNORED (Ebright's "in error" case):**
- ***Las Siete Partidas*** (Partida 3, tít. 28, ley 9): "**Separately belonging to the common of each city or town are the ejidos, the montes, and the dehesas**… established and granted for the communal benefit." (Ebright p.311)
- **Mexican Colonization Law of 1824, Art. 2:** "the object of this law is **those lands of the nation, not being private property nor belonging to any corporation or pueblo**." (Ebright p.115)
- **Plan of Pitic (1789)** + **Mariano Galván-Rivera (*Tierras y Aguas*):** kings "had to **cede to the settlements of America and to their councils—a certain portion of lands**… *concejiles ó propios*." (Ebright pp.115, 310)
- **U.S. Attorney Matthew Reynolds** = architect of the gov't theory: granting official "designated out-boundaries—**the title to the unallotted portions remaining in the Government**." (Ebright pp.122-123)
- Ebright's summary: the Court misread **procedural** rules (royal management control) as **substantive** ownership, failing to distinguish public (sovereign) from communal (pueblo) domain.

**SCRIPT VALUE:** This is the mechanism — *not* "broken promise," but "a court mistranslated a settlement manual into a deed." Cleaner + more defensible than FLow's "they violated it."

## 2. Why New Mexico 6% vs California 73% (design + powerlessness)
- **California:** 1851 Act = **judicial** 3-person board + appeal to federal district court & SCOTUS. **Statehood 1850** → congressional representation "helped insure… relatively fair." ~12M claimed → **8.8M confirmed (73%)**.
- **New Mexico:** **Territory until 1912** (no leverage). 1854–91 Surveyor General = "**not really an adjudication at all**," lacked "**due process of law**"; only recommended to Congress, whose action was "conclusive… not subject to review." Then CPLC 1891–1904. **CPLC alone = 6%**; SG+CPLC combined = 24% (31% if Peralta-Reavis subtracted).
- **The 1891 Act was deliberately stricter:** required title "**lawfully and regularly derived**"; killed the 1851 "town presumption"; "**excludes by express terms any claim… dependent upon the mere grace or favor**" of the old sovereign. *Sandoval*: the 1891 law meant "to confine… **narrower limits than… the act of 1851**."
- **Texas exception:** "admitted to the Union with **full control over its public lands**" (annexation, not cession) → no federal land court; **state laws and courts** handled titles.

## 3. Where the land went (the spoils, quantified)
- **Lawyers' fees:** customarily ¼–⅓ of acreage; by 1880 lawyers "**may have received as much as 80 percent of land grant property in lieu of fees**."
- **Thomas B. Catron:** interest in **75 grants**, **~2 million acres outright** + attorney for 4M more — "single largest landholder in America."
- **Partition suits** (1876 territorial statute): commons "could not be physically divided" → forced auction. **Las Trampas:** commons sold **~60¢/acre** to merchant **Frank Bond**; attorney McMillan took the largest slice; **villagers averaged ~$25** for their shares.
- **Public domain → Forest Service:** CPLC rejected **32,718,354 of 34,653,340** NM acres. ~**13.6M NM acres** → Forest Service (early 1900s). **Carson & Santa Fe NF = 2.4M acres**, "primarily from community land grants"; Forest Service itself estimates **22%** of those forests were community commons.
- **Homesteaders:** only **~1.2M acres** in NM (1850s–70s) — small vs the Forest Service transfer.
- Of ~20.25M rejected acres (minus Peralta-Reavis), est. **50% was community common lands** rejected under the San Joaquín / San Miguel del Bado doctrine.

# PART II — Deep layer (international law · comparative · resistance), 2026-06-13

## 8. The legal engine: why the treaty promise was unenforceable
- **Acquired-rights doctrine (international law):** private property survives a change of sovereignty; only the *public* domain (tierras baldías) passes to the successor state. The commons = *private* domain (tierras concejiles) → should have stayed with the communities. (O'Connell, *State Succession*; Walker, "Land, Law, and La Raza.")
- **Binding Mexican precedent ignored:** in 1832–33 Mexican official **Antonio Barreiro** + a commission ruled the San Joaquín commons were the settlers' **private property.** Ebright: "Under the acquired rights doctrine, Barreiro's decision… **should have determined** the question… because Mexican law was controlling." Daniel Tyler (studied ALL NM community grants): "the **ejido (common lands) belonged to the community** to which it was appurtenant." Settlers got private tracts *de mancomún* with "corresponding water, pastures, and watering places."
- **The Treaty was NOT self-executing — the keystone:** ***Botiller v. Dominguez*, 130 U.S. 238 (1889).** Rancho Las Virgenes was a conceded *perfect* grant; never filed with the 1851 board. SCOTUS held: if the statute conflicts with the treaty, "**the statute must prevail**"; the Court "has **no power to set itself up as the instrumentality for enforcing** the provisions of a treaty… which the government of the United States… chooses to disregard"; a violation is "**a matter of international concern**" for Mexico to press. → The "washing of hands."
- **Reversed Marshall's *Percheman* doctrine:** Marshall had held perfect titles "**did not need any action of the new government for their protection**" (government bore burden of disproving). Botiller flipped the burden onto the claimant; unfiled claims "**forever barred**" → public domain.
- **It could have been done right (proof it was a choice):** ***U.S. v. Chaves*** (Cubero grant) — Justice **Shiras** confirmed a community grant **with NO grant documents at all**, by actually applying *Percheman*, international law, and the treaty. Ebright: "Under international law United States courts **should have adjudicated land grants in the same manner as Spain and Mexico would have done**." A "beacon of fairness" showing the rest were a choice.
- **Ebright's bottom line:** the two bad outcomes — "improperly confirmed grants are now valuable private property" (speculators) and "improperly rejected grants are now U.S. government land" (Forest Service/BLM). Had Article X's spirit held, "the outcomes… would have been **dramatically different**."

## 9. Comparative — this was a national system, not a NM grievance
- **The Pueblo mirror (same question, opposite answer):**
  - ***U.S. v. Joseph*, 94 U.S. 614 (1876):** Pueblos held NOT "Indians" under federal protection → Pueblo land alienable → non-Indian squatters flood in.
  - ***U.S. v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28 (1913)** [the OTHER Sandoval — DO NOT conflate with 1897]: REVERSED Joseph — Pueblos are dependent communities under federal guardianship; communal title protected.
  - **Pueblo Lands Act 1924 + Pueblo Lands Board:** cleared non-Indian claims, restored/compensated Pueblo land.
  - **THE IRONY:** Hispano commons (1897) — communal title denied, land confiscated; Pueblo commons (1913/1924) — communal title shielded. Same court, same decades, mirror outcomes. → Part 2 / complication beat, not core.
- **The Dawes Act parallel (the systems frame):** **Dawes/General Allotment Act 1887** — same era, same logic: tribal commons sliced into **160-acre individual allotments** ("civilizing power of private property"); **90M+ acres** lost nationally. The Mexican-commons dispossession was **one front of a continent-wide late-19thC assault on communal tenure** (Indian + Hispano) → individualized private title favoring Anglo settlers. THIS is the channel's "you understand the SYSTEM" angle: not regional grievance, a national machine.

## 10. Resistance & survival (the human spine + modern rhyme)
- **Acequias survived where land didn't:** water stayed communal. Ebright: "**communal control of irrigation water by acequia associations has helped the communities… survive**." Still litigated (Taos priority-date adjudications, 1990s→). Privatization now threatens water too.
- **Las Gorras Blancas (1890s):** white-hooded night riders cut fences, tore out railroad track — earliest organized resistance; precursor to Tijerina.
- **La Corporación de Abiquiú (1937, Medardo Abeyta):** legal-strategy shadow government — "because the law robbed them of their collective rights, it must be **through the law that they recover them**." Mailed eviction notices + 10¢/head rental bills to ranchers, wrote its own deeds, performed ownership. Lost every case: *HND v. Suazo* (1940), *Flores v. Brusselbach* (1945), *Martínez v. Mundy* (1956). Correia: "the courts **erased the history of common property in Tierra Amarilla and replaced it with a history of private property**." A "**shadow land-tenure system lurks beside and beneath the official account**" — commons use persisted into the late 20thC → dispossession is "an ongoing struggle," not an event.
- **Tijerina / Alianza Federal de Mercedes — June 5, 1967 courthouse raid:** 19 armed members storm Rio Arriba County Courthouse for DA Alfonso Sanchez; "pitched gun battle," a state police officer + county jailer shot, 2 briefly kidnapped; Tijerina flees. **National Guard tanks "fitted with 40 mm canons and .50-caliber machine guns prowled the dirt roads"**; 500 police/guardsmen; FBI closed off the town. Tijerina → FBI "Rabble Rouser Index" / COINTELPRO. **Acquitted** of raid charges; later jailed for burning a Forest Service sign. Aftermath: state's own **Land Title Study concluded "much of what Tijerina and Alianza had been saying was true."** Forest Service = "an occupying force."
- **Jacona = the survival counter-example:** heirs took up a collection and **bought their own commons in 1909** to save it from outside sale; later self-partitioned to manage taxes.

**META-INSIGHT (the hook):** the misconceptions persist because they are **load-bearing** — each protects something in the present (current private titles, the national forests' legitimacy, the "equality under law / winning the West" national myth). The dispossession *had* to be remembered as fair, lawful, fraud-cleansing, and finished, because the honest memory **indicts present ownership.** Ebright's reviewer: "It takes more than a little courage to challenge **cherished American myths regarding equality under the law and the prevalence of fair play.**" → **Not ignorance — motivated forgetting. The myths are the dispossession's alibi.** Best myth-first cold-open frame.
