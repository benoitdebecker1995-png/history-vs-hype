### (1) TIMELINE
*   **1845, March 1:** U.S. President John Tyler signs legislation authorizing the annexation of the Republic of Texas.
*   **1845, November 10:** President Polk sends envoy John Slidell to Mexico with an offer to purchase New Mexico ($5M) and Alta California ($40M); Mexico refuses to meet him.
*   **1845, December 29:** The United States officially annexes Texas, an act Mexico had warned would be considered a declaration of war.
*   **1846, April 23:** The Mexican Congress issues a declaration of war against the United States.
*   **1846, April 25–26:** The Thornton Affair occurs; Mexican forces attack U.S. troops in disputed territory, providing the casus belli for President Polk.
*   **1846, May 13:** U.S. President Polk signs the formal declaration of war against Mexico.
*   **1846, June 15:** The Oregon Treaty is signed with Great Britain, securing the northern U.S. border and allowing a focused military effort against Mexico.
*   **1847, January 13:** The Treaty of Cahuenga (Capitulation Agreement) is signed, ending the fighting in Alta California.
*   **1847, September:** U.S. forces under General Winfield Scott capture Mexico City, effectively ending major military resistance.
*   **1848, February 2:** The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo is signed at the Basilica of Guadalupe by Nicholas Trist and three Mexican plenipotentiaries.
*   **1848, March 10:** The U.S. Senate ratifies the treaty with significant amendments, including the total removal of Article X.
*   **1848, May 19:** The Mexican Congress ratifies the amended treaty in Querétaro.
*   **1848, May 30:** Ratifications are exchanged and the Protocol of Querétaro is signed to clarify the U.S. Senate's amendments.
*   **1848, July 4:** President Polk formally proclaims the treaty as the law of the land.
*   **1853, December 30:** The Gadsden Purchase (Treaty of Mesilla) is signed, settling border disputes and annulling Article XI of the original treaty.
*   **1927:** The U.S. Supreme Court decides the "Country Club Dispute," resolving a boundary conflict between New Mexico and Texas caused by the shifting Rio Grande.

### (2) KEY FIGURES
*   **Nicholas Trist:** Chief clerk of the U.S. State Department who negotiated the treaty while defying a recall order from President Polk.
*   **James K. Polk:** 11th U.S. President whose expansionist "Manifest Destiny" policy and annexation of Texas triggered the war.
*   **Manuel de la Peña y Peña:** Mexican President and head of the Supreme Court who oversaw the difficult peace negotiations from Querétaro.
*   **Winfield Scott:** U.S. General whose successful invasion of central Mexico and occupation of the capital forced Mexico to the negotiating table.
*   **Luis G. Cuevas, Bernardo Couto, and Miguel Atristain:** The three Mexican plenipotentiaries who represented the collapsed Mexican government during the treaty drafting.
*   **Jefferson Davis:** U.S. Senator who led an unsuccessful effort to amend the treaty to annex even more Mexican states, including Tamaulipas and Chihuahua.
*   **John Nugent:** Journalist for the *New York Herald* who famously leaked the secret treaty text to the public before the Senate could vote on it.
*   **Nathan Clifford & Ambrose Hundley Sevier:** U.S. commissioners sent to Mexico to explain the Senate's amendments and sign the Protocol of Querétaro.
*   **Antonio López de Santa Anna:** Mexican leader whose subsequent return to power led to the Gadsden Purchase, which modified the original treaty's borders.

### (3) KEY CLAIMS
*   **Article VIII (Property & Citizenship):** Guaranteed that Mexicans remaining in the ceded territories could keep their property and choose U.S. or Mexican citizenship within one year; if no choice was made, they became U.S. citizens automatically.
*   **Article IX (Civil Rights):** Guaranteed that Mexicans "shall be maintained and protected in the free enjoyment of their liberty and property"; the U.S. Senate amended this to state they would be admitted to the Union "at the proper time (to be judged of by the Congress)" rather than "as soon as possible."
*   **Article X (The Struck Clause):** Originally guaranteed that the U.S. would honor all land grants awarded by the Spanish and Mexican governments; the U.S. Senate **completely struck** this article, claiming it was redundant but effectively leaving land titles vulnerable to U.S. court challenges.
*   **Article XI (Indian Raids):** Bound the U.S. to prevent and punish raids by "savage tribes" (Comanche and Apache) into Mexican territory; this was later **annulled** by the 1853 Gadsden Purchase because the U.S. found it impossible to enforce.
*   **Article XII (Payment):** The U.S. agreed to pay Mexico $15 million (roughly $560 million today) and assume $3.25 million in debts owed by Mexico to U.S. citizens.
*   **Senate Amendments:** Beyond striking Article X and weakening Article IX, the Senate modified Article VIII to ensure the U.S. was not bound to recognize land grants made after the start of the war.
*   **Language Prevalence:** There is **no clause** in the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo that makes the English version prevail over the Spanish. Both versions were signed as equally authentic; however, the Protocol of Querétaro was later used by Mexico to argue for the Spanish interpretation of land rights, which the U.S. subsequently disavowed.

### (4) DEBATES
*   **Land Grant Validity:** Historians like Richard Griswold del Castillo debate the "unilateral modifications" made by the U.S., arguing that the removal of Article X and the subsequent U.S. legal requirements for "perfect titles" violated the spirit of the treaty.
*   **The "All Mexico" Movement:** A major historiographical debate centers on why the U.S. did not annex the entire country of Mexico despite total military victory, with theories ranging from racial concerns to political divisions over slavery.
*   **Indian Raid Complicity:** There is ongoing debate among historians (such as Brian DeLay) regarding whether the U.S. intentionally failed to stop indigenous raids into Mexico to further weaken the Mexican government's hold on its northern territories.
*   **The Protocol of Querétaro:** Legal scholars debate whether the U.S. commissioners Clifford and Sevier had the authority to sign the Protocol, and whether the U.S. government's later rejection of it constituted a breach of international law.
*   **Racial Classification:** The "Becoming White" thesis explores the debate over how the treaty's citizenship offer forced a legal "whiteness" upon Mexicans that did not match their social or political reality in the 19th-century U.S.

### (7) MODERN RELEVANCE
*   **Community Land Grant Claims:** Controversy persists in New Mexico over "ejidos" (communal lands) that were lost when the U.S. refused to recognize community titles, a topic of a major 2004 GAO report.
*   **Costilla County Land Rights:** Recent (2021) legal hearings in Colorado involve descendants of original Mexican settlers fighting for access to ancestral grazing and timber lands, citing treaty protections.
*   **Water Rights & IBWC:** The International Boundary and Water Commission, established in 1889 as a result of the treaty's border needs, remains the primary body for modern disputes over Rio Grande and Colorado River water allocations.
*   **Chicano Civil Rights:** The treaty is frequently cited by activists and legal scholars as a foundational document for Mexican-American civil rights, particularly regarding language rights and land restitution.
