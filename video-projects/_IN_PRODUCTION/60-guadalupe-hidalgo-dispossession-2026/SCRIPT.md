## COLD OPEN / HOOK

[VISUAL: the bilingual treaty — English and Spanish columns, side by side]

In 1848, a treaty ended the war between the United States and Mexico. It drew the border the two countries still share today, and it handed America half of Mexico — California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico.

[VISUAL: highlight the "inviolably respected" line]

The treaty also made a promise to the roughly hundred thousand Mexicans now living on the American side of that new border. Their land, it said, would be "inviolably respected."

[VISUAL: slow zoom to "ARTICLE X [Stricken out]"]

Three weeks later, the United States Senate deleted the one article meant to guarantee it.

So this isn't a story about the war. It's about that promise — and how, over the next fifty years, America kept it on paper and quietly took it apart in court.

---

## BEAT 1 — THE PROMISE

[VISUAL: Articles VIII, IX, X]

On paper, the treaty was generous. One article let these families keep their land, or sell it and leave. Another made them American citizens. And a third — Article Ten — promised that every piece of land Spain and Mexico had ever officially handed out would stay just as valid as it had always been.

And that mattered, because a lot of this land wasn't owned the way Americans owned land. In New Mexico especially, a village often shared its land. Each family had a house and a small plot to farm — but the pasture they grazed, the forest they cut wood from, the water they all drank, the whole village owned together, in common. Article Ten would have protected all of it.

---

## BEAT 2 — THE DELETION, AND WHY

[VISUAL: "ARTICLE X [Stricken out]"]

The Senate cut Article Ten before it would sign. And that wasn't an accident.

[VISUAL: United States v. Percheman, 33 U.S. 51 (1833) — Chief Justice Marshall's opinion]

Fifteen years earlier, the Supreme Court had answered a question that mattered enormously here. When the United States takes over land from another country, do the people already living there simply keep what they own — or do they have to prove it all over again to their new government? In a case from Florida, the Court had said: they keep it, automatically. An old Spanish land grant was valid the day it was made, and America had no power to make people re-earn land they already owned. If the government wanted to take someone's land away, the government had to prove the claim was bad — not the other way around.

Article Ten would have locked that rule in across the whole Southwest. And that was exactly the rule the Senate didn't want. So it cut the article that would have made it law.

[VISUAL: Protocol of Querétaro text]

Mexico noticed. It refused to finish the deal until America signed a side note — promising, in writing, that deleting Article Ten "did not in any way intend to annul the grants of lands made by Mexico." Reassured, Mexico signed.

[VISUAL: Clayton quote card]

A year later, a new American Secretary of State was asked whether that side note actually bound the United States. His answer was no. The government, he said, had "at no time regarded the Protocol as obligatory." So the very thing that got Mexico to sign, America now said it had never been bound by at all.

---

## BEAT 3 — THE MACHINE

[VISUAL: timeline — 1854 ... 1891 ... 1904]

With Article Ten gone, there was no longer any rule for deciding which of the old grants America had to honor. So the government had to build one. And building it took about fifty years.

It started in 1854, with a single official: a Surveyor General for New Mexico. But he couldn't actually decide anything. All he could do was look at a family's old Spanish papers and send a recommendation to Congress — and Congress would deal with it whenever it felt like it. He had almost no staff and no money to investigate, so the claims just piled up. By 1880, about a thousand had been filed, and Congress had finished a hundred and fifty of them. Everyone else waited, sometimes for decades, not knowing if the land under their feet was even theirs. And while they waited, a ring of lawyers and politicians in Santa Fe used those delays to pry families off their grants — and walked away with millions of acres.

[VISUAL: Court of Private Land Claims]

Then, in 1891, Congress finally set up a real court to finish the job — judges, trials, the whole thing. On the surface, that sounds fairer. But the court was built to be hard on these families. A few years before, some enormous grants had slipped through on fraud, and an embarrassed Congress overcorrected, making the new rules as strict as it possibly could. Now a family had to prove, from paperwork sometimes a hundred years old, that the exact Spanish official who signed their grant had the legal authority to sign it. If a document was missing, the grant was rejected. There was no local jury — which, in New Mexico, would have been mostly Mexican-American. And any appeal went straight to a Supreme Court that, by then, sided with the government almost every time.

## BEAT 4 — THE DOCTRINE

[VISUAL: U.S. v. Sandoval, 167 U.S. 278 (1897) — Chief Justice Fuller's opinion, highlight: "lands not actually allotted to settlers remained the property of the king"]

The case that decided it for everyone came in 1897. A village called San Miguel del Bado had a community grant of more than three hundred thousand acres — most of it the shared pasture and forest the village lived off. The land court confirmed the whole grant. The government appealed. And the Supreme Court reversed it: it ruled that the village's shared land had never belonged to the village at all.

The reasoning went like this. Under Spanish and Mexican rule, the Court said, that shared land had always belonged to the king. So when the land passed from Spain, to Mexico, to the United States, it stayed the government's the whole way down. The villagers could keep their houses and their small farm plots. Everything they had shared in common was gone — three hundred thousand acres, down to about five thousand.

[VISUAL: the books — RELIED ON: Frederic Hall, *The Laws of Mexico* (1885) · Reynolds's own compilation (1895). SET ASIDE: *Las Siete Partidas* 3.28.9 — "the ejidos [common lands], the montes [woodland], and the dehesas [pasture]… belong to the common of each city or town" · Mexican Colonization Law of 1824, Art. 2]

So how could a court decide a village didn't own land it had used for a hundred years? To answer that, the judges had to work out what Spanish law actually said about shared village land. And the books they trusted weren't the ones that actually settled who owned it.

The government's lawyer was a man named Matthew Reynolds, and he was paid to defeat these claims. He told the Court that under Spanish law, a village's shared land had always belonged to the king. But that simply wasn't what Spanish law said. The real Spanish property code was clear: a town's shared pasture and woods belonged to the town. A Mexican law from 1824 said the same thing.

And one detail makes the whole thing hard to defend. The book the judges leaned on as their main authority on Spanish law was one that Reynolds's own office had compiled and published under his name. It left out the part of Spanish law — the weight it gave to long-standing use and possession — that favored the villages. So the lawyer whose job was to defeat these families had also supplied the very rulebook the judges used to rule against them.

[VISUAL: NM — 34.65M acres claimed → 1.93M confirmed ≈ 6% · California ~75%]

And San Miguel del Bado wasn't unusual. Across New Mexico, families had claimed about thirty-five million acres of old Spanish and Mexican land. The court confirmed two million of it. Six percent.

That number only lands when you compare it. California had gone through the very same process, under the very same treaty — and there, landowners kept about three-quarters of what they'd claimed. New Mexicans kept six percent. The difference wasn't that the claims were weaker. It was that New Mexico had no power. California was already a state, with congressmen who could fight for a fair deal. New Mexico was kept a territory for sixty-four years, with no vote in Congress at all — so when Congress wrote these harsher rules, no one was in the room to stop it.

[VISUAL: Peralta-Reavis forged title]

To be fair, not every rejected claim was honest. The most notorious was an outright forgery — a fake Spanish title invented to grab twelve million acres — and the court was right to throw it out. But take the frauds out of the pile, and the pattern is hard to miss. The claims that kept losing were the community ones — the shared land, the part that couldn't be split into neat private plots — rejected over and over, on that same 1897 rule.

[VISUAL: scholar cards — Bradfute "fair, unbiased, conscientious" · Ebright "unfair" · Gómez court treated giveaway "as a national service"]

Historians still disagree on why all this happened. Some think the judges were doing their honest best with a foreign legal system they barely understood. Most now think it went further — that the whole machine was quietly built to move this land into government hands, and that it worked. But whichever it was, the result was the same. The families ended up with six percent. The land was gone.

## CLOSE

[VISUAL: rejected commons → "public domain" → national forest]

And the land never came back. When a village's shared land was rejected, it didn't pass to some new owner. It became federal public land — land the government simply holds. And in the early 1900s, the government took about thirteen million acres of it and turned it into national forest.

[VISUAL: Carson & Santa Fe National Forests on a map, overlaid on the old grant boundaries]

You can go and stand on it today. It's the Carson and Santa Fe National Forests — about two and a half million acres of public land. By the Forest Service's own estimate, nearly a quarter of those two forests was once village common land.

And maybe that's the part worth sitting with. Nobody marched in and seized these mountains. There was no battle for them, and no single villain you could point to — just a promise made in 1848 by the most powerful country on earth, and fifty patient years of court rulings that made sure it never had to be kept. The land changed hands without a shot.
