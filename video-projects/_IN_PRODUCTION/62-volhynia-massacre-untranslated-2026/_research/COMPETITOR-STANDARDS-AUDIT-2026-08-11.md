# Competitor Standards Audit — Volhynia / UPA

**Completed:** 2026-08-11  
**Scope:** Close-match YouTube treatments of Volhynia, OUN-UPA/Bandera, and Polish-Ukrainian historical memory, compared with the current `SCRIPT.md` chronological rebuild and locked structure.  
**Metric rule:** View counts are observations at the date stated, not current or stable performance facts. YouTube page fetches were intermittently blocked during this audit; where a current count or exact publication day could not be independently recovered, the field says so rather than guessing.

## Headline finding

`SCRIPT.md` meets the *serious contested-history* genre standard and has a real distinction: it makes the missing signed extermination order a transparent evidentiary limit, then tests the surviving doctrine, command structure, testimony, attack pattern and registry against it. The closest high-performing direct treatment, Historia Bez Cenzury, reaches a categorical Polish verdict; the English treatments tend either to dramatize perpetrators or demand condemnation. None of the inspected close matches combines the modern diplomatic trigger, a chronological causal chain, and a visible provenance-aware paper trail.

The viewing-risk is not lack of differentiation. It is **context before momentum**: from `SCRIPT.md:28` to `:64`, viewers meet four acronyms/names, post-1918 partition, Dontsov, Kolodzinskyi, 1941 and the police deserters before the Poryck human anchor at `:68`. Strong close matches earn that load either with an immediate witness story (OllieKay) or a specific crisis/war image (World History). The locked chronology should stay, but it needs to make the viewer feel the live question is advancing at every context beat.

### What is convention, and what is creator-specific

**Widespread in the usable close matches**

1. The first minute delivers a tangible human or historical event, then asks the causal question.
2. Context is a chain of causes, not a neutral encyclopaedia preface: Polish policy/war/ideology each explains the next escalation.
3. One named victim or witness bears the emotional weight; the scale then widens.
4. Acknowledge Polish violence or Ukrainian non-participation before making the asymmetry judgement. This is not “both sides”; it is credibility work.
5. The modern-memory question comes only after the audience understands the 1943 mechanism.
6. Conclusions return to the opening moral/political stake and give a reason to act, remember or keep watching.

**One-off choices, not standards to copy**

- HBC’s content warning, graphic quotations and direct national demand belong to its Polish advocacy position and 55-minute runtime.
- OllieKay’s extended moral address and repeated disclaimer language suit an argumentative commentary format; duplicating it would weaken this draft’s calmer referee voice.
- World History’s protagonist-first war montage is efficient but turns Volhynia into a Shukhevych biography. The locked #62 premise is deliberately broader and should remain so.

## Beat-by-beat comparison and pressure points

### 1. Open — `SCRIPT.md:12–22`

The open is better packaged than the closest videos: a contemporary ally rupture is both immediate and inherently puzzling. It also earns the title/thumbnail promise. The risk is that the method explanation (`18`) asks for trust before the viewer has experienced the investigation. HBC makes the audience experience testimony first; World History experiences invasion first; OllieKay experiences a child’s testimony first. #62 should let the decree and official response *be* the first proof of method, then move quickly to “who were the UPA?”

**Verdict:** Keep the event-led opening. Compress only if read-aloud timing shows it exceeds roughly 35–45 seconds before the first historical question.

### 2. Formation and ideology — `28–62`

This is where the draft most needs active presentation, not a different thesis. The audience must carry: UPA, OUN-B, Bandera, Polish/Ukrainian borderland, Dontsov, Kolodzinskyi, 1941, Stetsko, Germany, police deserters. Each item is justified by the lock, but six consecutive explanatory paragraphs can feel like a prerequisite course.

The competitor standard is not “remove context.” HBC spends far more time on national formation. The standard is **give the context a live object and causal job**. Here the post-WWI map must visually establish the disputed mixed land; Kolodzinskyi must be the first unsettling paper; Stetsko’s declaration must immediately turn “independence movement” into “what kind of movement?” The police-desertion figure must be shown as the material bridge from idea to capability.

**Flags for the edit/read-aloud, not rewrites:**

- `28–32`: Four labels plus two competing memories in five lines. Clear on paper, but a viewer who clicked on Zelensky may still not know why *this* UPA is the one involved. The on-screen identity card/map needs to carry more than the voiceover.
- `34–38`: The post-war partition is necessary but may become abstract if the map does not show the mixed borderland before the nationalist fear in `36`.
- `38–42`: Dontsov and Kolodzinskyi are two consecutive intellectual beats. The second has the stronger proof payload; make clear why viewers are meeting the first before the facsimile arrives.
- `50–62`: This is good acceleration but has a risk of “Bandera/Nazi diversion” swallowing the Volhynia promise. The line of forward motion must remain: Germany rejected the desired state; the underground acquired trained armed men; that army did something in 1943.

### 3. Poryck and the widening map — `66–74`

This directly matches the best technique in OllieKay and HBC: one person/place, then reveal the system. It is considerably more restrained than HBC and therefore better suited to the channel. The named registry (`72`) is also a clean evidence visual: it changes “tens of thousands” from a statistic into a demonstrated archival task.

**Verdict:** This is the primary human-anchor beat. Keep one testimony only; do not add graphic accounts to compete with HBC.

### 4. Counterargument, labels and paper trail — `78–120`

This is the distinctive middle act. Competitors commonly settle the label and then use balance to defend it. #62 says exactly what is agreed, what label varies, what source survives, and what that source cannot prove. That creates real investigative tension rather than false suspense.

The risk is density and a duplicated conclusion. `80–92` reaches “organized campaign,” then `100–118` reaches it again after the documentary test. The second is stronger because it earns the judgement; therefore the first must operate as an open question, not feel like the verdict has already arrived. The key retention sentence is `102`: “at a certain point the paper stops.” That is a genuine turn, but the display of Order No. 11 must be legible enough for viewers to understand why an authentic order can still fail to be the order they expected.

**Specific flags:**

- `80–84`: Strong fairness logic, but four claims (reciprocal violence, rescuers, scale, chronology) arrive quickly. Use the visual treatment to maintain the explicit distinction between conflict and an organized anti-civilian campaign.
- `86–92`: The scholar-label comparison is necessary but risks a “three historians say” plateau. The next question—who organized it?—should be the visual/voice landing.
- `104–110`: Excellent source criticism. Do not let caveats read as an academic disclaimer: put the copy/typescript and Soviet-custody caveat visibly beside the paper, then state what it still contributes.
- `112–118`: This is the thesis proof. `118` adds a speculative archive-destruction possibility after the limits have been stated. It is potentially a weak final button because it cannot be resolved in the video; make sure it does not turn an evidence-led verdict into an untestable rescue explanation.

### 5. Memory politics and present relevance — `124–152`

This section answers the opening’s “why now?” better than the benchmarks. It gives a mechanism—post-1991/post-2014 memory shift, legal recognition, selected public story—rather than a simple accusation that Ukraine “glorifies Nazis.” It also correctly sets Russia’s use of the past outside the historical question rather than allowing Moscow to monopolize it.

The serial evidence at `128–144` can feel like a second documentary dossier after the Order No. 11 act. The strict narrative order should be: **why a useful anti-Soviet memory emerged → how the state endorsed it → why it is not unanimous → how selection shows up in one concrete museum/exhumation dispute → why that makes the 2026 honour politically explosive.** This is already the prose order, but the visual sequence must preserve the causal hierarchy.

**Flag:** `146` is the clearest one-sentence answer to the title. Protect it as the landing; `148–150` are necessary modern guardrails but should not dissipate its force.

### 6. Close and conversion — `156–168`

HBC ends by telling a neighbouring state what it must do. OllieKay ends with an extended moral appeal. #62 has a more portable, channel-consistent finish: difficult heroes are a recurring national-memory mechanism, not a uniquely Ukrainian moral failure. Belgium is a useful personal anchor precisely because it does not reopen the Poland/Ukraine argument.

`162` names the channel mission and asks for subscription, while `168` invites source-reading and independent judgment. The pattern works if the hard question at `166` remains the true last emotional thought. The CTA should feel like the viewer joining a method they just saw work, not closing a lecture.

## Does the draft genuinely own a twist?

**Yes, with a qualification.** The *absence of a preserved written extermination order* is not unmentioned elsewhere—HBC itself discusses no written initiating order and relies on wider evidence—but the inspected treatments do not build their central viewing experience around the provenance distinction:

| Treatment | What it does with uncertainty | What #62 does differently |
|---|---|---|
| HBC | Uses missing written order as one route to a categorical genocide conclusion, supported by reports and a Polish-memory appeal. | Shows an actual surviving command order, labels what it does **not** say, and makes the custody/copy problem part of the viewer’s reasoning. |
| World History | Builds a perpetrator chronology and atrocity escalation. | Uses the chronology to arrive at a testable responsibility question rather than a biography verdict. |
| OllieKay | Begins from victim testimony, rejects collective guilt, and argues for moral confrontation. | Moves from one victim/place to a document-led distinction between evidence, inference and modern state memory. |

That is a differentiated History vs Hype twist only if viewers can *see* the distinction. If the order appears as a small, unexplained foreign-language prop, the advantage reverses into a comprehension cost. The on-screen labels promised in the script and the source-genealogy rule are therefore structural, not merely research hygiene.

## Recommendation to the orchestrator

Do not alter the locked chronological spine or copy HBC’s advocacy/gore. Treat the next read-aloud/edit pass as a presentation test with three non-negotiables:

1. The first 90 seconds must repeatedly answer the click question—why a wartime ally rupture is about these particular men—not just correctly introduce terms.
2. The Poryck → map → registry progression must be allowed to breathe as the emotional/systemic centre.
3. The middle act must visually make the viewer understand the difference between “real Order No. 11,” “no signed extermination order,” and “converging evidence of an organized campaign.” That comprehension is the channel’s point of difference and the source of tension.
