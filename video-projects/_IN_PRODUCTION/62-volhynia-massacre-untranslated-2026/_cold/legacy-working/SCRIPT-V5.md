# Why Does Ukraine Still Honour the UPA?

**Status:** V5, final surgical repair after Creator Read-through 4

## OPEN

In May 2026, President Zelensky gave a Ukrainian special-forces unit the honorary name “Heroes of the UPA.” Poland's president responded by withdrawing Zelensky's Order of the White Eagle, Poland's highest honour.

The Polish reaction makes sense once you know what the UPA name means in Poland. It is associated above all with the killing of Polish civilians, especially in Volhynia, where UPA units attacked Polish communities during the Second World War and tens of thousands were killed.

My first suspicion was that maybe Ukraine could honour the UPA because Volhynia was left out, minimized or explained very differently. But then I found something which made the Ukrainian decision more interesting. One current Ukrainian history textbook says that the Volhynian leadership of OUN-B decided to expel the Polish population, and that UPA units attacked Polish settlements in July 1943. Later, the same textbook describes the UPA's fight against the Soviet secret police as a “heroic struggle.”

So if Ukraine itself teaches what happened in Volhynia, why does it still choose to honour the UPA now, especially when doing that creates a dispute with Poland during a war with Russia?

To understand that, I first needed to look at the part Poland objects to, and then at the independence struggle Ukraine says it is honouring.

## HOW THE ANTI-POLISH CAMPAIGN DEVELOPED

The UPA was the Ukrainian Insurgent Army. It was connected to the Bandera wing of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists, or OUN-B. Their objective was an independent Ukrainian state.

After the First World War, most of Ukraine became part of the Soviet Union, while Volhynia and Eastern Galicia became part of Poland. Ukrainian nationalists believed those territories had to belong to a future Ukraine.

Conflict with the Polish state helped radicalize the OUN. Polish rule brought repression, restrictions and promises of autonomy that were never fulfilled. OUN military thinker Mykhailo Kolodzinskyi also argued in 1938 that as long as a substantial Polish population remained, Poland could continue claiming that these lands were Polish. His solution was to remove what he called the “Polish element” and “end Polish claims to the Polish character of these lands.”

**[ON SCREEN: verified Kolodzinskyi passage. Label: `PRE-WAR DOCTRINE, NOT A 1943 ORDER`.]**

The war then destroyed the old order and brought Soviet and German occupations. In spring 1943, around five thousand Ukrainian auxiliary policemen deserted German service. They did not all join the UPA, but those who did brought weapons, training and experience, expanding the movement's ability to act.

So the idea of removing the Polish population was already present inside the OUN before 1943. The police desertions did not cause what happened next, but they gave the movement far more armed and trained men who could help carry it out.

Then, on Sunday, 11 July 1943, UPA units attacked nearly a hundred Polish localities in Volhynia. Some attacks struck churches while people were attending Mass. In Poryck, survivor Jadwiga Krajewska remembered that the first shots came during the Gloria.

Poryck was one place inside a much larger campaign. In Volhynia in 1943, roughly fifty to sixty thousand Polish civilians were killed.

UPA units clearly took part in these attacks. But Poland's objection goes further than that. It asks whether the anti-Polish campaign is tied to the organization being honoured.

There is a real disagreement about how much violence grew from local conflict, peasant participation and the collapse of order, and how much direction came through OUN and UPA structures. Both can be true: local people could participate for their own reasons while the movement organized a wider campaign.

The public explanation given by the OUN in October 1943 condemned what it called mutual mass killing and explained the violence through Polish policy, reciprocal conflict and outside powers.

Okay, so that is how the movement explained the violence publicly. But then look at what one of its own Security Service reports says.

## WHAT THE INTERNAL RECORDS SHOW

This is Document 149, an internal report from the Security Service of the Zahrava military district in eastern Volhynia. It covers mid-September to mid-October 1943 and is signed by the section head using the pseudonym “Tretiy.”

This is what it says about the Poles:

> “The action of destroying the Poles did not bring the expected results.”

**[ON SCREEN: `Акція нищення поляків не принесла очікуваних наслідків` with checked English translation. Show Document 149, *Litopys UPA*, New Series, vol. 2; TsDAVO f. 3838, op. 1, spr. 55, ark. 1–5; copy; signed “Tretiy.”]**

That is a very different description of what was happening. The next sentence says that the “active Polish element” had largely survived. So the author is talking about an anti-Polish action and asking whether it achieved the expected result.

The word “destroying” does not tell us by itself whether the intended result was killing, forced flight, territorial removal or some combination. That wider conclusion comes from the territorial aims we have already seen, the attacks themselves and the other contemporary reports.

Taken together, the records show anti-Polish actions being assigned, carried out and evaluated through OUN and UPA structures. What happened varied by place, and this Security Service report describes serious problems with higher control over local branches. Organized did not mean perfectly controlled, but the campaign was still being handled through movement structures.

That is why Poland's objection cannot be reduced to a few UPA units becoming involved in local violence. The anti-Polish campaign is tied to the organization whose name Ukraine is honouring.

Okay. That explains Poland.

But Ukraine's official reason for honouring the UPA is its role in the struggle for an independent Ukraine. So how substantial was that struggle? Were they really carrying on an organized fight against Soviet rule?

## THE ANTI-SOVIET STRUGGLE

In August 1943, OUN-B and the UPA movement issued a program called “What the Ukrainian Insurgent Army Is Fighting For.” It says the UPA is fighting for an independent united Ukrainian state, and against Russian-Bolshevik and German occupiers.

**[ON SCREEN: “За що бореться Українська Повстанська Армія?”; HDA SBU, file 376, vol. 4, fols. 62–70; published in *Litopys UPA*, vol. 1, pp. 126–130. Highlight the checked Ukrainian wording beside a labelled English translation.]**

That tells us what the movement said it was fighting for. But a political program does not prove that it actually carried out the struggle it claimed.

For that, we can look at what the underground was doing after the Red Army returned in 1944, and at records written by the people trying to defeat it.

The underground was not only hiding in forests. OUN instructions told members to “legalize” themselves inside Soviet institutions, village councils and collective farms, where they could support the underground while appearing to live legally. A June 1945 NKVD guideline warned that “Soviet institutions and enterprises are swamped by a hostile element.”

**[ON SCREEN, REQUIRES EXACT PAGE VERIFICATION: June 1945 NKVD guideline. Show full producer, date and locator before highlighting the checked original wording and translation.]**

Armed groups also attacked local officials and disrupted mobilization. They were trying to stop Soviet rule from becoming secure locally, both inside its institutions and through armed resistance.

That explains the Soviet response. Security records describe clashes, major operations, arrests, infiltration and deportations used to destroy the underground and its support.

The Soviets called the insurgents bandits and had no reason to make them look heroic. Yet their own records show that this was a real organized resistance, not only a claim made by the movement itself.

The form of the resistance changed over time. Large formal UPA formations declined by the end of the 1940s. Smaller groups from the OUN and its Security Service underground continued into the 1950s.

The underground also coerced and killed Ukrainians it suspected of helping the Soviets. So recognizing that the struggle was real does not make these people angels, or excuse everything they did. But the struggle was real.

## BACK TO THE TEXTBOOK

Now we can return to the textbook.

The part about expelling Poles and attacking their settlements is supported by the historical record. So is the part about fighting Soviet rule. The textbook teaches both, but only the anti-Soviet struggle is described as heroic.

One textbook cannot tell us what every Ukrainian thinks. But it shows how acknowledgment and heroic memory can coexist: Volhynia is included, while the struggle against Soviet rule explains what the movement contributed.

The idea of honouring that contribution was already there. A 2010 presidential decree included OUN and UPA members among twentieth-century independence fighters. In 2015, after Russia seized Crimea and began the war in Donbas, Ukraine passed a law recognizing both organizations and making commemoration of that struggle state policy.

What changed was that resistance to Moscow was no longer only history. Russian aggression made the anti-Soviet part of the UPA's story much more relevant to Ukraine's own survival. The 2026 decree itself says the UPA name restores national military traditions while Ukraine defends its territorial integrity and independence.

Russia uses the UPA's real extremist ideology, cooperation with Nazi Germany and crimes against Polish civilians to claim that modern Ukraine is a Nazi state. That does not follow, and Ukraine does not have to deny those facts to reject it. One wartime movement does not define an entire modern country or justify Russia's invasion.

But official recognition still does not tell us whether Ukrainians judge the independence struggle and the movement's entire wartime record in the same way.

On 27 April 2022, the Rating Group asked whether OUN-UPA should be recognized as participants in the struggle for Ukrainian independence. Eighty-one percent supported that recognition, and ten percent opposed it.

Five months later, KIIS asked: “How do you, in general, assess the activities of OUN-UPA during the Second World War?” Forty-three percent answered positively, eight percent negatively, and thirty-seven percent found it difficult to judge.

KIIS itself pointed to the difference. Recognizing that OUN-UPA fought for Ukrainian independence is not the same as giving one positive or negative judgment on everything it did during the war.

## POLAND AND THE PROBLEM OF COMMEMORATION

This makes the Ukrainian decision easier for me to understand. Ukraine is fighting Russia again, so examples of people resisting Moscow for an independent Ukraine become useful. A country at war needs stories and people it can look up to. There is nothing inherently strange to me about that.

But Poland's objection also makes more sense to me now. It is not only asking Ukraine to remember the dead. The two countries have acknowledged the victims together, and their presidents jointly mourned them in 2023. Poland is asking whether the UPA name itself should receive state honour when the anti-Polish campaign is tied to that same organization.

This isn't a uniquely Ukrainian problem. In 2024, one Polish ministry stopped official commemorations of the anti-communist fighter Józef Kuraś, known as “Ogień,” after objections concerning crimes against civilians. The Polish Institute of National Remembrance argued that actions which deserve criticism should not erase his contribution to the struggle for an independent Poland.

I am not saying these cases or the crimes are equivalent. They are not. The comparison is only about the problem of commemoration: what does a state do when somebody made a real contribution to an independence struggle, but also has serious crimes attached to their record?

## WHERE I END UP

So two things are quite clear. The UPA and the later nationalist underground really did fight Soviet rule for an independent Ukraine. The same movement was also tied to an organized campaign against Polish civilians.

I now understand why the first part matters so much today. The UPA gives Ukraine a historical example of people resisting Moscow for an independent state.

What worries me is not that Ukraine remembers or even admires that contribution. Historical figures do not have to be perfect before we recognize something they genuinely did.

The problem for me begins if making the UPA heroic requires cleaning up the rest of its history: the extremist ideology, the cooperation with Nazi Germany and the anti-Polish campaign in Volhynia. Those facts belong to the same movement. Acknowledging them does not mean accepting Russia's propaganda about modern Ukraine.

I am still not completely sure what that means for giving the UPA name itself an official honour. But my concern is much clearer now. You can remember people as an example without turning them into uncomplicated heroes. Commemoration becomes a problem when it starts erasing the history that makes the hero uncomfortable.
