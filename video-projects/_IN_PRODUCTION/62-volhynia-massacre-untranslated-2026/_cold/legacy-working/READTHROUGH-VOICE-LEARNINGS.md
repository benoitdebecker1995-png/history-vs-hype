# Read-through voice learnings

## Scope

These are only new or sharpened lessons strongly supported by the 17 August 2026 read-through. They do not replace the existing Voice and Brain Model.

## Voice-profile learnings

### 1. Benoit marks provisional interpretations explicitly

“The basic disagreement is clear” triggered an immediate objection. His own repair—“seems obvious on the surface”—does two jobs: it prevents narrator omniscience and signals that the video will complicate the first impression.

**Sharpened rule:** When an interpretation is preliminary, Benoit naturally marks it as preliminary with phrases such as “on the surface,” “initially I thought,” or “at first this seemed.” Do not turn a provisional thought into a clean declarative thesis.

### 2. Concrete noun repetition is preferable when the noun carries the argument

He repeatedly stopped at “the massacre,” “the story,” “their version,” “the honour,” “execution,” “what is happening,” and “present.” These were not vocabulary problems. The argument depended on identifying exactly which massacre, claim, honour, or action was meant.

**New rule:** Repeat the concrete noun instead of using an elegant pronoun or compressed reference when that noun carries a causal or moral distinction. Say “the Ukrainian reason for honouring the UPA,” not “the honour”; “the anti-Polish action,” not “the action,” after any interruption or topic shift.

### 3. Benoit rejects editorial stage directions in narration

“Germany belongs in this background” sounded impossible to him. It announces the writer's organization instead of making a historical statement.

**New rule:** Do not narrate why a topic belongs in the script. State the causal fact that makes the topic necessary.

### 4. Plain contrast works; balanced abstraction does not

He accepted direct formulations such as Poland's objection and the polling comparison, but stopped at “factual erasure,” “the precise beginning,” and “connect that action to the organization.”

**Sharpened rule:** A contrast should name both concrete sides. “The textbook teaches the attacks but calls the anti-Soviet struggle heroic” is preferable to “factual erasure does not explain what is happening.”

### 5. Short landings work only after the referents are secure

“So did they?” works because the preceding sentence names the claim: the UPA fought for an independent Ukraine. “And that explains Poland” was less secure because “that” summarized a confused document sequence.

**Sharpened rule:** Short spoken landings are native to Benoit, but only after the previous paragraph has established a single unmistakable referent.

## Brain / reasoning learnings

### 6. Evidence must answer a viewer question already in the room

Document 149 failed in V1 not because it was weak or too technical, but because the script had not established what the viewer needed it to answer. Volodymyr–Horokhiv and Huta Stepańska failed even more strongly because they only increased corroboration.

**New rule:** Before every primary source, write the viewer's current question in plain language. If the source does not directly answer that question, keep it under the hood. The preferred order is: question → producer/function → source words → immediate inference → necessary qualification.

### 7. Actor introduction is functional, not biographical

Bandera, Stetsko, Shukhevych, Tretii, Zahrawa, and Security Service all triggered “who?” questions. Benoit did not ask for biographies. He asked what each name was doing in the argument.

**New rule:** A named actor earns narration only after a one-clause functional introduction. If that clause does not change what the viewer understands, remove the name.

### 8. Rebuttals require an introduced opponent or interpretation

“Not spontaneous,” “not an equal war,” “not invented to hide Volhynia,” and “not an outside accusation” all failed because the opposing claim was absent from the viewer's version of the debate.

**New rule:** Never answer a research debate under the surface of the script. Introduce who advances the interpretation and why it matters, or remove the rebuttal.

### 9. Causal dependencies matter more than chronological completeness

The background failed at the points where V1 merely moved from borders to doctrine to police to Germany to July. Benoit's stops consistently asked why the next event followed.

**New rule:** Historical background should be built as dependencies: failed state → disputed land → territorial objective → wartime opportunity → armed capacity → anti-Polish action. A correct fact that does not create the conditions for the next fact should be demoted.

### 10. “Why does this matter?” is a hard gate

When Benoit asks why a fact or document is present, this is not a request for another transition phrase. It means the fact has no narrative job.

**New rule:** A detail earns narration only if it advances the current causal chain, answers the current evidentiary question, or changes the final interpretation. Otherwise keep it in research, notes, or visuals.

### 11. Source provenance has two layers

Benoit needs to hear what a source is, who produced it, and why it matters. He does not need fond/inventory/file/folio spoken aloud.

**New rule:** Narration carries functional provenance. The screen carries technical provenance. Speak archival locators only when the locator itself is disputed or essential to the inference.

### 12. Technical accuracy must be scaled to viewer need

The formal UPA versus later OUN/SB underground distinction is historically necessary, but V1 introduced it with Shukhevych and unexplained organizational labels.

**New rule:** Preserve necessary distinctions in the smallest form that prevents a false claim. Do not add names or institutional taxonomy merely to demonstrate precision.

### 13. Belief updates must correspond to a real prior uncertainty

The read-through supplied one real update: Benoit tentatively suspected the ugly history might no longer be part of the heroic story; the textbook complicated that. It rejected the manufactured update that the independence struggle was “invented to hide Volhynia.”

**New rule:** Before writing “this changed how I saw it,” identify the actual prior thought in creator evidence. If none exists, state the conclusion without a conversion arc.

### 14. The macro-spine can remain locked while viewer-facing subquestions change

The read-through did not reject the order of modern dispute → two historical halves → textbook return → memory → Poland/Russia → conclusion. It rejected the inherited command-order subquestions inside Volhynia.

**New rule:** Distinguish macro thought order from local investigative framing. Preserve an approved spine while replacing a subquestion that no longer serves the video's main problem.

## Tested hypotheses

- **Referential clarity:** strongly confirmed across at least eleven interruptions.
- **Evidence after question:** strongly confirmed by Zahrawa, both corroborating reports, and Soviet records.
- **Actors before names:** strongly confirmed by Bandera/Stetsko, Zahrawa/Tretii/SB, and Shukhevych.
- **No unheard rebuttals:** strongly confirmed by spontaneous-war, equal-sides, invented-independence and outside-accusation lines.
- **Causal bridges over transition phrases:** strongly confirmed by the border/war sequence, Germany jump, July jump, and Soviet-return gap.
- **“Why does this matter?” gate:** strongly confirmed.
- **Sources interpretable before display:** strongly confirmed.
- **Screen carries technical provenance:** strongly confirmed by “blah, blah, blah” response to the archival locator.
- **Abstraction failure is referential, not merely lexical:** strongly confirmed. The rejected phrases suppress the exact noun or comparison on which the reasoning depends.
