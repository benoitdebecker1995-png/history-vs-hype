## COLD OPEN

In August nineteen forty-three, a Ukrainian commander wrote a report to his superiors. It reads: "I liquidated all Poles from young to old. I burned all the buildings and took the possessions and livestock for the needs of the battalion."

Eighty-three years later, Ukraine's president named an elite special-forces unit after the army that commander served in — the UPA — and called them heroes.

Three weeks later, Poland took its highest award back from Ukraine's president.

For Poland, these men were the main perpetrators of the Volhynia massacre. So that decree sounded like Germany naming a brigade after the SS.

The killings themselves are not seriously disputed. The disagreement is what kind of campaign this was.

Poland calls it genocide. Ukraine's memory institute calls it a Second Polish-Ukrainian War. Most English-language historians call it ethnic cleansing. Grzegorz Motyka, the leading Polish historian of the subject, calls it genocidal ethnic cleansing.

And those aren't just different words for the same thing. A two-sided war means two sides fighting each other. Ethnic cleansing means organized violence to remove people from a territory. Genocide means the intention went further than removal. It means destroying the group.

So I wanted to see what the documents actually support.

Ukraine says these men are being honoured because they fought for an independent Ukraine against Moscow. Poland says you cannot separate that from what the same army did to Polish civilians.

And the question is: how far up the chain did it go?

## THE TWO STORIES

For Ukraine, the UPA was an army fighting for independence after almost every other hope of independence had disappeared.

Bandera led the nationalist faction that later built the UPA. In nineteen forty-one, that faction declared Ukrainian independence. Germany ordered it withdrawn. The faction refused, so Bandera was arrested and later held in Sachsenhausen.

The underground he left behind did attack the Germans too, raiding prisons and ambushing convoys. But these were small operations. They were not going to change the war against Germany.

After Germany's defeat, the UPA kept fighting the Soviets into the early nineteen fifties.

And during the massacres themselves, Bandera was still confined outside Ukraine. He had limited contact with the movement, but the surviving record does not show what he knew or whether he influenced this policy.

So that part of the Ukrainian story is real. These men did fight Moscow. And when Russia took Crimea in twenty fourteen, Ukraine needed figures who had done that before.

Soviet names came off streets. Soviet monuments came down. A twenty fifteen law recognized both the nationalist movement and the UPA as fighters for Ukrainian independence, and made it illegal to publicly disrespect them. The decree from twenty twenty-six is an extension of that.

So that's why honoring these men is useful now.

But Poland remembers the same army very differently. Its case is that this was neither local chaos nor an equal war. The UPA set out to remove Poles from Volhynia. Villages were attacked on the same days. Commanders reported what they had done. And fifty to sixty thousand Poles were killed there in nineteen forty-three.

Poland's position is not that Ukrainians never died. Its parliament acknowledges crimes against Ukrainian civilians in the same resolution that calls Volhynia genocide.

What Poland rejects is the idea that those reprisals turn the UPA's earlier campaign into a spontaneous war between equal sides.

## HOW THEY GOT THERE

To understand why this happened, you have to go back to the First World War.

Ukrainians proclaimed a state of their own. This state lasted for about three years. The east of Ukraine fell to the Soviets. In the west, Volhynia and Galicia went to the new Poland.

For Ukrainian nationalists, that left a problem. Land full of someone else's people is land someone else can claim.

Dmytro Dontsov became the intellectual father of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists — the OUN. He made the nation the highest moral value. If an action helped the nation get its state, then the action was moral.

And one of the movement's military thinkers, Mykhailo Kolodzinskyi, wrote that a future uprising should "sweep away literally to the last man the Polish element from the Western Ukrainian Lands."

That does not prove who ordered the killings in nineteen forty-three. But it does show that removing Poles was part of the movement's thinking before the massacre.

Then, in nineteen forty-one, Germany invaded the Soviet Union and the OUN made its move.

On June thirtieth, Yaroslav Stetsko proclaimed an independent Ukrainian state. The text survives, signed by Stetsko. It says the new state would "collaborate closely with National Socialist Greater Germany, which under the leadership of Adolf Hitler is creating a new order in Europe and the world."

This wasn't necessarily because they were devoted Nazis. It was a practical alliance. After terror, deportations, and famine under Soviet rule, Moscow was the biggest obstacle to an independent Ukraine. The OUN thought Germany might allow a Ukrainian client state, like Slovakia or Croatia.

Germany had a different plan. It wanted Ukraine as territory to exploit, not as a country allowed to govern itself. Bandera and Stetsko refused to withdraw the declaration, and both were arrested.

And later, the movement changed this document. OUN émigrés reprinted it without the passage promising collaboration with Hitler's Germany. An edited version was later displayed in a Bandera museum outside Lviv.

That does not prove a Volhynia order was destroyed. It shows that the movement knew what part of its own record was difficult to keep.

## THE ARMY AND THE ATTACKS

German repression pushed the OUN-B underground, where it began building an army.

By nineteen forty-two, thousands of Ukrainians who would later join the UPA were serving as German auxiliary policemen. They helped the occupation keep order and round up Jews for murder.

Some joined for wages and food. Others wanted to keep their families out of forced-labour roundups. And some wanted the power the uniform gave them over their neighbours.

The nationalist leadership had also ordered its own members into the police to gain training and collect weapons.

Why they needed that army became clear in nineteen forty-three. Germany was losing. The nationalist leadership expected the map of Eastern Europe to be redrawn again, and believed a restored Poland would try to reclaim the region.

Around five thousand policemen deserted German service that spring and joined the nationalist underground with their weapons and training.

A policeman named Redesha later explained: "We burned Polish settlements so there would be no trace of their existence, and so that the Poles would never again try to lay claim to Ukraine."

Now the idea had men to carry it out.

On Sunday, July eleventh, nineteen forty-three, the UPA hit around a hundred Polish villages at the same time. Many villagers were at mass. In Poryck, attackers surrounded the church and fired machine guns through the doors.

Around two hundred people died in that village that morning. Around four thousand died across Volhynia that day.

By the end of nineteen forty-three, fifty to sixty thousand Poles were dead in Volhynia. A Polish husband-and-wife team, the Siemaszkos, spent decades building a registry. Their volumes contain around thirty-six thousand names, one by one.

## WAS IT A TWO-SIDED WAR?

The Ukrainian case is right about one thing: Poles also killed Ukrainians.

Polish attacks came back the other way in nineteen forty-four. And in nineteen forty-seven, communist Poland deported around a hundred and forty thousand Ukrainians in Operation Vistula.

But the timing and the scale were not equal. Timothy Snyder, working from Motyka's research, puts the wider conflict at roughly seventy thousand dead Poles to twenty thousand dead Ukrainians.

And it wasn't Poles against Ukrainians as whole peoples either. The UPA's own security service shot Ukrainians it considered unreliable, including men who refused to kill Poles.

So the part about Polish reprisals is real. But most serious historians don't accept the spontaneous part.

Jared McBride calls this ethnic cleansing. Ivan Katchanovski rejects the genocide label, but still calls it one-sided ethnic cleansing organized by the OUN-B, the UPA, and its security service. Motyka goes further and calls it genocidal ethnic cleansing.

The disagreement between those historians is narrower than the political one. They agree this was organized violence to remove the Polish population from Volhynia. They disagree about whether the aim was removal or destruction, and about how close the surviving documents take us to the men at the top.

## THE MISSING PAPER

The report we started with is an example of what we do have. A commander had just destroyed two Polish villages, and he was reporting a finished job up to his superiors.

That is not a mob of angry people. It is an officer working inside a chain of command.

And if we look at UPA internal directives from nineteen forty-four, one ends with a standing greeting: "Weapons ready. Death to Poles."

But if we follow these reports back to the top, at a certain point the paper stops.

The Volhynian UPA commander was Dmytro Klyachkivsky, known as Klym Savur. Administrative and military orders bearing his signature survive.

Some Polish historians cite a directive they describe as Klym Savur's signed order to destroy the Polish population. But the original comes from the same archive complex nobody can inspect today.

So the real problem is not that nobody has ever claimed a signed order existed. It's that nobody can produce an authenticated kill order bearing his signature that all sides can inspect.

The closest evidence we have are two pieces of paper, both from a UPA commander named Yurii Stelmashchuk.

The first is a confession from nineteen forty-five. After he was captured, Stelmashchuk told Soviet interrogators that Klyachkivsky had given him "the secret directive about the complete physical destruction of the Polish population."

That confession was recorded under duress and in the interrogators' wording. So it cannot be treated like a voluntary statement.

Viatrovych says Soviet investigators fabricated or manipulated it to discredit the Ukrainian independence movement. But a copy sits in Ukraine's own archive, filed there in nineteen sixty-three. Another published copy carries a Soviet general's handwritten notes dated April nineteen forty-five.

That makes it difficult to say the document itself was invented decades later. It does not prove the confession was voluntary, or that every word came from Stelmashchuk.

The second piece is older, and stronger: a letter from June nineteen forty-three, written while the killing was underway. Stelmashchuk doubted that the order had come from Klyachkivsky, so he wrote to the OUN leadership to check. In that letter, he repeated the same secret directive and said it had been given by word of mouth.

We do not know whether he received an answer, but he carried the order out.

Some Polish historians describe this as Klym Savur's signed directive. Motyka is more precise. Stelmashchuk signed the letter, but the letter reports an oral order. It is not the order itself.

And there's one more problem. Nobody has been able to inspect the original file the letter is cited from. It was restricted again or lost around the collapse of the Soviet Union. So the public evidence is a transcription reproduced by historians.

That is strong evidence of a reported oral order. It is not Klyachkivsky's signature.

## CLOSE

So where does that leave us?

The record shows an organized campaign under Klyachkivsky. It does not show an authenticated order from him that would settle the argument about his exact intent.

And that's why this argument survives.

Ukraine has offered Poland some things. The two countries signed a reconciliation declaration in nineteen ninety-seven, and in twenty twenty-three their presidents mourned the victims together. But Poland still wants to dig up the graves and name the dead.

And the unresolved history hands Russia a weapon. Moscow takes this very real crime and uses it to claim that Ukraine itself is a Nazi state. But nothing in these documents proves that about modern Ukraine.

Poland still arms Ukraine because its own security depends on an independent Ukraine. So it condemns the UPA while helping modern Ukraine survive.

This is not unique to Ukraine. In my own Belgium, we still have statues and squares honoring Leopold the Second, after everything that happened in the Congo.

There's nothing wrong with looking to the past for heroes when you're in a fight now. What's wrong is burying what they did.

We should try to look at these people in their historical context, without justifying everything that they did.
