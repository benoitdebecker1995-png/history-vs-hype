# Ukraine Teaches the Massacre. So Why Call the UPA Heroes?

## OPEN

When I first saw that Ukraine had given a military unit the honorary name “Heroes of the UPA,” I thought I understood the basic problem. The UPA is remembered in Ukraine for fighting for independence, while the massacre of Polish civilians is probably left somewhere outside that story.

But then I checked a Ukrainian history textbook published in 2023 and listed in the Ministry of Education's textbook library.

It tells students that the regional leadership of the OUN-B decided to expel the Polish population from Volhynia, and that UPA units attacked Polish settlements. Then, in the same book, the UPA's later fight against the Soviet security forces is described as heroic.

That does not mean the presentation is neutral. The section begins with Polish violence, uses the language of a Polish-Ukrainian confrontation, and asks students to consider an argument against calling the campaign genocide. So the massacre is present, but it sits inside a larger story of conflict and liberation. I think that distinction is more useful than simply asking whether the event is mentioned.

So my first explanation was too simple. At least in this textbook, Volhynia has not disappeared. The more difficult question is how the organization connected to that campaign can still become a heroic national symbol.

To answer that, I think we need to test both parts of the story. How organized was the campaign against Polish civilians? And was the later independence struggle substantial enough to explain why Ukrainians remember it?

## WHY POLISH CIVILIANS BECAME A TARGET

The OUN, the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists, was founded in 1929 with the goal of creating an independent Ukrainian state. It was not representative of every Ukrainian, but it was trying to solve a real political problem. After the First World War, millions of Ukrainians lived inside Poland, including in Volhynia and Eastern Galicia, while the larger part of Ukraine was controlled by the Soviet Union.

Polish rule included elections and legal Ukrainian parties, but also restrictions, broken promises of autonomy and periods of repression. None of that made the killing of civilians inevitable. Most Ukrainians did not join the OUN. But the OUN increasingly treated the Polish population in those territories as more than neighbours. They became an obstacle to a future Ukrainian state, because their presence strengthened Poland's claim to the land.

By the late 1930s, the OUN military thinker Mykhailo Kolodzinskyi had put that logic into writing. During a future uprising, he wanted the Polish population removed from the territory the nationalists claimed.

**[ON SCREEN: map → Kolodzinskyi original page and checked translation. Label: `REMOVAL DOCTRINE — NOT AN OPERATIONAL ORDER`.]**

That gives us an idea, but it does not yet explain how an armed campaign became possible.

In 1940, the OUN split, and the younger faction followed Stepan Bandera. OUN-B then tried to use Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union to create an independent state. Its 1941 proclamation promised cooperation with Hitler's Germany, so that connection is not an invention. But Germany rejected Ukrainian independence, detained Bandera and his deputy Yaroslav Stetsko, and repressed the organization. The underground continued without them.

Then, in spring 1943, around five thousand Ukrainian auxiliary policemen deserted German service. Some joined the UPA willingly, some were recruited by force, and some had very few alternatives. They brought weapons, training and experience into a movement that was rapidly becoming an army.

Again, that establishes capacity. It does not by itself establish a policy against civilians. For that, we have to look at the attacks.

## THE CAMPAIGN

On 11 July 1943, UPA units attacked more than a hundred Polish localities in Volhynia. One of them was Poryck, where people had gathered for Sunday Mass. Historian Grzegorz Motyka reconstructs how the church was surrounded and attacked. On the same page, survivor Jadwiga Krajewska remembered that the first shots came during the Gloria.

**[ON SCREEN: map → Poryck locator → Motyka page with reconstruction and testimony.]**

Poryck matters because it gives us one place and one group of people, but it was part of a much larger operation. Around four thousand Poles were killed during the July action. Across Volhynia in 1943, historians estimate that roughly fifty to sixty thousand Polish civilians were killed.

The Siemaszkos' village-by-village registry documents more than thirty-six thousand victims by place and date, many of them by name. The registry has its own methodology and limitations, but it makes it very difficult to reduce this to a vague story about communities that suddenly turned on each other.

Polish underground and partisan forces also killed Ukrainian civilians, especially as the conflict spread. Some Ukrainians protected Polish neighbours and were killed for doing so. Those facts belong in the history. But they do not make the campaigns equivalent. The mass anti-Polish campaign came first, pursued a territorial objective and killed far more civilians.

The timing and scale show coordination. They still do not tell us exactly where the original decision was made, so this is where the documents become important.

## WHAT THE DOCUMENTS CAN ESTABLISH

What survives is not one neat master order. We have contemporary operational reports in which UPA units describe attacks and report through a military structure. We also have regional OUN material regulating when special units could conduct liquidation actions. That evidence is contemporary, and it does not depend on later Soviet interrogations.

This also gives us a useful comparison. In October 1943, the OUN's public statement described a Polish-Ukrainian conflict and denied organizational responsibility for mass killing. Internal records do not read in the same way. They show commanders discussing anti-Polish actions through an organization with rules, reporting and regional authority. A public denial cannot settle the question when the movement's own internal material points somewhere else.

It supports organization. But the word “liquidation” has to be read in its actual sentence and context; it cannot automatically be turned into an extermination order every time it appears.

Then there is the testimony of Yurii Stelmashchuk, a UPA commander under the regional commander Dmytro Klyachkivsky. In Soviet custody in 1945, Stelmashchuk said that Klyachkivsky had relayed a secret directive for the physical destruction of the Polish population.

That is serious evidence, but it is not a clean confession. The surviving edition identifies the document as a typed copy, and Stelmashchuk was being interrogated by Soviet security before his execution. It can support a link to the regional command. It cannot give us a voluntary statement or authenticate the exact words of an original order.

**[ON SCREEN: whole Stelmashchuk page → highlight; labels: `COPY, TYPESCRIPT` and `SOVIET CUSTODY`.]**

I could not find an authenticated signed extermination order in the project sources. I therefore would not claim that we know the exact wording or can trace the original decision with certainty to the central OUN leadership. I also found no basis for saying that Bandera personally ordered Volhynia while he was detained by the Germans.

But that limit does not turn the campaign into spontaneous village violence. The removal doctrine, attack pattern, military reporting, command structure and testimony point to an organized campaign. What remains disputed is the exact genesis of the order and the label historians should apply—not whether tens of thousands of Polish civilians were deliberately driven out and killed.

## THE OTHER PART OF THE UPA STORY

That explains Poland's anger, but not why the UPA became useful to Ukraine. For that, I wanted to test the heroic part of the story as well.

The UPA did fight Soviet rule, and we do not need to rely only on UPA memoirs to establish it. Soviet party and security records describe armed clashes, attacks on communications, resistance to Red Army mobilization and the deployment of NKVD forces against the insurgency. Collections from the late 1940s still contain regular reports on operations against UPA formations and the OUN underground.

**[ON SCREEN: Soviet collection identity → one 1944 report → one 1948 report. Identify producer and date.]**

That insurgency also involved coercion and violence against Ukrainian civilians, especially through the movement's security apparatus, so “independence fighter” cannot become a moral blank cheque. But the armed struggle against returning Soviet rule was real and continued after Germany had been defeated.

This is the part of the history that Soviet accounts reduced to fascist banditry. After Ukraine became independent in 1991, the UPA could instead be remembered as one of the few organized movements that had fought for a Ukrainian state.

At first that memory was strongest in western Ukraine and remained contested elsewhere. After Russia annexed Crimea and the war in Donbas began in 2014, resistance to Moscow became much more relevant nationally. In 2015, Ukraine recognized members of the OUN and UPA in law as fighters for independence. After the full-scale invasion in 2022, public support rose further, although polling changes substantially depending on whether people are asked about independence fighters or about the movement's conduct during the war.

That difference in wording matters. Someone can recognize the UPA as a movement for independence without approving everything it did during the war. The polls therefore help explain how commemoration becomes possible, but they do not prove that Ukrainians agree about Volhynia or even understand the question in the same way.

Then, in 2026, Zelensky gave a military unit the honorary name “Heroes of the UPA.” Poland's president revoked Zelensky's Order of the White Eagle because of that decision, and Zelensky returned the medal.

**[ON SCREEN: `1991 → 2014 → 2015 LAW → 2022 → 2026 DECREE`, then Polish statement.]**

So this was not a sudden invention by Zelensky. It was the latest step in a much longer rehabilitation, accelerated by a new war against Moscow.

## WHAT THE TEXTBOOK DOES NOT RESOLVE

This brings me back to the textbook, because it changed what I thought the disagreement was.

The easy accusation would be that Ukraine can honour the UPA only by hiding Volhynia. This particular book does not do that. It tells students that the OUN-B leadership in Volhynia decided to expel the Polish population, and it also presents the fight against Soviet rule as heroic.

But putting both facts in a textbook does not settle what should be commemorated. The Polish objection is stronger than “Ukraine refuses to admit that civilians died.” It is that the same organization conducted the independence struggle and the anti-Polish campaign, so an official honour for the organization also honours a name inseparable from those crimes.

I understand better now why Ukrainians can see the UPA as part of a real independence tradition. The anti-Soviet insurgency was not invented, and Russia's use of OUN and UPA history does not make modern Ukraine a Nazi state or justify an invasion.

But Russia did not invent Volhynia either. The campaign was organized, and the uncertainty around the original command does not erase organizational responsibility.

So I ended up changing one part of my view. I would no longer say that Ukraine simply removes the massacre from its history. At least one official textbook proves that claim is too broad. What Ukraine's state memory does is separate the part of the UPA it wants to commemorate from the part Poland says cannot be separated from it.

And that, I think, is the real disagreement. Understanding why Ukrainians honour the independence struggle explains the decision. It does not by itself answer whether the organization should carry the honour.
