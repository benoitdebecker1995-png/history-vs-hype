## COLD OPEN (0:00–1:15)

In August 1943, a Ukrainian commander wrote a report to his superiors. It reads: "I liquidated all Poles from young to old. I burned all the buildings and took the possessions and livestock for the needs of the battalion."

Eighty-three years later, on May 26th, 2026, Ukraine's president signed a decree naming an elite special-forces unit after the army that man served — the UPA — and called them heroes. Three weeks later, Poland took its highest award back from Ukraine's president. Because for Poland, these men are the main perpetrators of the Volhynia massacre — the killing that report describes. To Poland, that decree sounded like Germany naming a brigade after the SS.

Poland's claim is that this massacre was a genocide — in the strict sense, the intent to destroy a people. And for historians, that argument turns on one thing: whether it was ordered from the top. Because if an order to commit this massacre came down, it would have left three things behind — attacks going off everywhere at once, reports coming back up the chain, and the order itself. So that's why I looked into the documents these men left behind, the orders they signed and the reports they filed. Both countries tell a different story about the same men, and each story has a grain of truth. And that's what I'm trying to do in this video: look at which of those grains the documents actually support.


## CH 1 — TWO THINGS AT ONCE (1:15–3:00)

The army in that decree is the UPA — the Ukrainian Insurgent Army. And the historical consensus is that two things about these men are true at the same time.

They fought for an independent Ukraine against everyone who occupied it. The Germans arrested their leader in 1941 and held him for three years, and his brothers died in Auschwitz. And when the Soviets invaded Ukraine again, the UPA kept fighting the Soviets in the forests into the 1950s, until the Soviets finally defeated them. And in 1943, that same army set out to clear Volhynia — the region in the northwest of today's Ukraine — of the Poles living there. By the end of that year, somewhere between fifty and sixty thousand Poles were dead. A lot of that is documented by the men who did it, in their own reports.

Both versions are supported by evidence. It isn't two stories where one of them has to be false.

Which leaves the question I actually wanted answered. Ukraine is fighting for its existence, and Poland is the ally on its land border. So why put that alliance in danger, in the middle of a war, over what these men did in 1943 — which Ukraine doesn't even contest?

The answer starts in 2014, when Russia took Crimea. A country at war needs heroes who fought the same enemy, and these men fought Moscow for years. That feeling came before the law. After 2014, Ukraine started stripping the Soviet past out of public life — Soviet names off the streets, Soviet monuments down. The law that wrote these men in as fighters for Ukrainian independence was part of that, in 2015, and it made it illegal to publicly disrespect them. The decree from 2026 is an extension of that law.

And the law put a name on something already there. In 2012, threats cancelled most of a lecture tour by the historian who wrote the leading biography of Bandera. If you write about these killings, you can get labelled a traitor, or a Russian agent.

So that's why honoring these men is useful now. And to understand how we got to the massacre that these men committed, we have to go back a hundred years — to a country that lived for about three years, and then disappeared.


## CH 2 — THE LOST STATE (3:00–4:00)

After the First World War, Ukrainians did what half of Europe was doing. They proclaimed a state of their own. Its defeat left behind an idea. The east of Ukraine fell to the Soviets. In the west, Volhynia and Galicia went to the new Poland. The powers that won the war sat around a table and assigned the land, and Ukraine wasn't at that table. There were enough Poles living in western Ukraine for Poland to claim it.

The idea started with Dmytro Dontsov, the intellectual father of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists — the OUN. He made the nation the highest moral value there is. If an action helped the nation get its state, the action was moral. And one of the movement's military thinkers, Kolodzinskyi, wrote down what that would mean in practice. When the day of independence came, the western lands would have to be cleared of the Polish population. Because land full of someone else's people is land someone else can claim.


## CH 3 — THE FALSE DAWN (4:00–5:00)

Then, in the summer of 1941, the movement got its chance. Germany invaded the Soviet Union, and as its armies rolled into Lviv, the Ukrainian nationalists made their move. On June 30th, Yaroslav Stetsko proclaimed an independent Ukrainian state.

The text still survives, signed by Stetsko himself. In it, the new state pledged to "collaborate closely with National Socialist Greater Germany, which under the leadership of Adolf Hitler is creating a new order in Europe and the world."

This wasn't necessarily because they were devoted Nazis. It was a practical alliance. In 1941, Moscow remained the greatest obstacle to an independent Ukraine after two decades of terror, deportations, and famine.

But Berlin had no interest in an independent Ukraine. The nationalists had thought that Germany would allow it to exist as a client state, like Slovakia or Croatia. Instead, Berlin ordered them to withdraw the proclamation. They refused, and that refusal sent the movement's leader, Stepan Bandera, to Sachsenhausen.


## CH 4 — THE ARMY (5:00–7:10)

So the Germans had locked up the movement's leader, but the movement didn't vanish. It went underground and began building an army. In time it turned on the Germans too, raiding prisons and ambushing convoys.

By 1942, many of the men who would later fight in its ranks were serving in German uniform. Partly, that was the plan.

Thousands of Ukrainians served as German auxiliary policemen, helping the occupiers keep order and round up Jews for murder. Some joined for the wage and the food. Some joined to keep their families out of forced labor roundups. And some joined for the power it gave them over their neighbours.

This kind of collaboration existed across occupied Europe. Poland had its Blue Police too — Poles in German service. But in Ukraine there was an extra layer: nationalist leaders had ordered their own members into the police to gain training and collect weapons.

Why they needed that army became clear in 1943. After Stalingrad, Germany was clearly losing the war. The nationalist leadership expected the map of Eastern Europe to be redrawn once again, and believed its final enemy would be a resurrected Poland.

So, in the spring of 1943, around five thousand policemen deserted the Germans and joined the nationalists, bringing with them their weapons and training. Not all were hardened killers. But some already were, killing Poles before they even reached the assembly points. A policeman named Redesha later explained: "We burned Polish settlements so there would be no trace of their existence, and so that the Poles would never again try to lay claim to Ukraine. It was explained to us that by doing this, it would make it easier to bring about the coming Ukrainian revolution."

Now the idea had men to carry it out.


## CH 5 — BLOODY SUNDAY (7:10–7:50)

On Sunday, July 11th, 1943, the UPA hit around a hundred Polish villages at the same time. The villagers were at mass. In Poryck, the attackers surrounded the church and fired machine guns through the doors. Around two hundred people died in that village that morning. Around four thousand died that single day. By the end of that year, across Volhynia, the toll had run to somewhere between fifty and sixty thousand Poles. A Polish husband-and-wife team, the Siemaszkos, spent decades building a registry of the dead. In their volumes they list about thirty-six thousand names, one by one.


## CH 6 — TWO-SIDED? (7:50–9:10)

In today's Ukraine, this chapter is remembered as a two-sided war — Poles and Ukrainians killing each other in the chaos of occupation. Something local, spontaneous, and tragic for everybody involved.

The part about Poles killing Ukrainians is true. The violence the UPA had started in 1943 came back the other way. Polish underground units hit Ukrainian villages in 1944, and in 1947 communist Poland deported around a hundred and forty thousand Ukrainians from their homes in Operation Vistula. Poland acknowledges this violence — its own parliament names it in the same resolution that calls Volhynia a genocide. But even with those killings counted, the numbers stay lopsided. Timothy Snyder, working from the Polish historian Grzegorz Motyka's research, puts the whole conflict at roughly seventy thousand dead Poles to twenty thousand dead Ukrainians. The numbers aren't the main point of contention. What Ukraine's official historians dispute is the framing.

And like so often in history, it wasn't Poles against Ukrainians. The UPA's own security service shot Ukrainians it considered unreliable — thousands of them, including men who refused to kill Poles.

Most serious historians don't accept the spontaneous part. The Siemaszko registry names the dead village by village — whole waves of them killed in attacks on the same days. You cannot call that spontaneous. Then the question becomes: if it was not spontaneous, who organized it? Is there any actual proof of an order from the top?


## CH 7 — THE MISSING PAPER (9:10–12:10)

The men on the ground wrote down what they did. The report we started the video with is an example of this. The commander who wrote it had just destroyed two Polish villages, and he was reporting a finished job up to his superiors. That isn't a mob of angry people — that's an officer reporting up a chain of command. And if we look at UPA's internal directives from 1944, one ends with a standing greeting: "Weapons ready. Death to Poles." The anti-Polish sentiment was alive and well in the UPA.

But if we follow these orders and reports back to the top, at a certain point the paper stops. The regional commander of Volhynia, Dmytro Klyachkivsky, signed everything — orders on defending villages, on tribunals, on land reform — all under his code-name, "Klym Savur." What no one can actually produce is an order to kill Poles with his signature on it.

The closest thing we have are two pieces of paper, and both come from the same man: a UPA commander named Stelmashchuk.

The first is a confession, from 1945. After he was captured, Stelmashchuk told Soviet interrogators that Klyachkivsky had personally given him "the secret directive about the complete physical destruction of the Polish population." That's a confession under duress, in the interrogators' own wording. And Ukraine's answer to the 1945 confession comes from the man who ran both the security service's archive and the state's memory institute. He says the KGB invented it. Except a copy of that 1945 confession sits in Ukraine's own archive, filed there in 1963. And another copy of it was published in Russia, carrying a Soviet general's handwritten notes dated two months after the interrogation. So the document he says the KGB invented was already in the file, annotated, decades before.

The second piece of evidence is older, and it's stronger. In June 1943, while the killing was underway, Stelmashchuk doubted that order had really come from the top. So he wrote to the leadership to check. In that 1943 letter he repeats the same secret directive — the one he says was given by word of mouth. We don't know if he ever got a reply, but he carried the order out anyway. Poland's leading historians still quote the 1943 letter, and some of them call it Klym Savur's own signed order. Motyka never calls it signed. For him it's a letter reporting an order, not the order itself. And Ukraine's objection to this one is different: the same man says the 1943 letter isn't in the file it's cited from at all. That one nobody can settle. The file was classified again, by the best account, or burned when the Soviet Union collapsed, so what survives is a transcription. Still — nothing with his signature on it.

And this is a movement that cleaned up its own record where it could. By 1944 the Soviets were slowly beating the Germans. And many UPA units made a secret deal with the Germans for the weapons they were leaving behind — while telling the world they were anti-Nazi fighters. And after the war, the ones who made it west carried that 1941 proclamation with them. In the movement's own reprintings, the passage pledging collaboration with Hitler's Germany is simply gone. They erased it. And after Ukraine finally became independent in 1991, that edited version made it home. In a Bandera museum outside of Lviv, the proclamation went on display with the Hitler passage missing.

None of that tells you what happened to any one order. It tells you what kind of record we're reading.


## CH 8 — THE WORD (12:10–12:55)

And because that piece of paper is missing, Ukraine and Poland still can't agree what to call this massacre — more than eighty years later. Even Motyka accepts that no signed order ever turned up. What convinced him to call it a genocide was the coordination — everything going off everywhere at once. Poland looks at the behaviour. Ukraine points to the fact that there is no signed order. That's the main point of contention. But it could be that this order was destroyed in the archive.

And it's not that Ukraine has offered Poland nothing. The two countries signed a reconciliation declaration in 1997, and in 2023 the two presidents mourned the victims side by side. But Poland wants Ukraine to say the word — genocide — and Ukraine won't. For years it even blocked the exhumation of the graves of the massacre. The digs only resumed in 2025.


## CH 9 — WHO THE DEADLOCK SERVES (12:55–13:30)

And Ukraine's refusal hands Russia a weapon. Moscow takes this very real crime and uses it to claim that Ukraine itself is a Nazi state. And that argument is how Russia justifies the war against it. That is what "denazification" means in the Kremlin's rhetoric.

All the while, Russia sits on the Soviet central files, held for fifty years while the Soviet Union denounced these men as fascist butchers. Yet it has never produced the one piece of paper that would prove the order to cleanse Volhynia came from the top.

And Poland, despite all of this, keeps arming Ukraine anyway. Because Poland decided long ago that its own security depends on an independent Ukraine.

So Ukraine remembers the part that glorifies these men, and buries the part that doesn't. The same state that calls Russia's atrocities at Bucha by their name is commemorating men who did similar things to the Poles, eighty years ago.

This is not unique to Ukraine. France still celebrates Napoleon — the man who brought slavery back after the Revolution had abolished it.
