# Why Does Ukraine Still Honour the UPA?

**Status:** V4 — final targeted refinement; one exhibit blocker remains

## OPEN

In May 2026, President Zelensky gave a Ukrainian special-forces unit the honorary name “Heroes of the UPA.” Poland's president responded by withdrawing Zelensky's Order of the White Eagle, Poland's highest honour.

The Polish reaction makes sense once you know what the UPA name means in Poland. It is associated above all with the killing of Polish civilians, especially in Volhynia, where UPA units attacked Polish communities during the Second World War and tens of thousands were killed.

But then I found something which made the Ukrainian decision more interesting. One current Ukrainian history textbook says that the Volhynian leadership of OUN-B decided to expel the Polish population, and that UPA units attacked Polish settlements in July 1943. Later, the same textbook describes the UPA's fight against the Soviet secret police as a “heroic struggle.”

So Ukraine is not necessarily honouring the UPA because this other part of the history has disappeared. At least in this textbook, both parts are there. The question is why the anti-Soviet struggle gets to define the public honour, especially when doing that creates a dispute with Poland during a war with Russia.

To understand that, I first needed to look at the part Poland objects to, and then at the independence struggle Ukraine says it is honouring.

## HOW THE ANTI-POLISH CAMPAIGN DEVELOPED

The UPA was the Ukrainian Insurgent Army. It was connected to the Bandera wing of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists, or OUN-B. Their objective was an independent Ukrainian state.

After the First World War, most of Ukraine became part of the Soviet Union, while Volhynia and Eastern Galicia became part of Poland. Ukrainian nationalists believed those territories had to belong to a future Ukraine.

Conflict with the Polish state helped radicalize the OUN. Polish rule brought repression, restrictions and promises of autonomy that were never fulfilled. Inside the movement, the Polish population itself became connected to the future border: if large Polish communities remained after another war, Poland could use their presence to claim this land again. Before the Second World War, OUN military thinker Mykhailo Kolodzinskyi had already written about removing what he called the “Polish element.”

**[ON SCREEN: Kolodzinskyi passage. Label: `PRE-WAR DOCTRINE — NOT A 1943 ORDER`.]**

The war then destroyed the old order and brought Soviet and German occupations. In spring 1943, around five thousand Ukrainian auxiliary policemen deserted German service. They did not all join the UPA, but those who did brought weapons, training and experience, expanding the movement's ability to act.

So by the middle of 1943, ideas about removing the Polish population already existed inside the nationalist movement, and it now had a much larger armed force with which to act on them.

Then, on Sunday, 11 July 1943, UPA units attacked nearly a hundred Polish localities in Volhynia. Some attacks struck churches while people were attending Mass. In Poryck, survivor Jadwiga Krajewska remembered that the first shots came during the Gloria.

Poryck was one place inside a much larger campaign. In Volhynia in 1943, roughly fifty to sixty thousand Polish civilians were killed.

UPA units clearly took part in these attacks. But Poland's objection goes further than that. It asks whether the anti-Polish campaign is tied to the organization being honoured.

There is a real disagreement about how much violence grew from local conflict, peasant participation and the collapse of order, and how much direction came through OUN and UPA structures. Both can be true: local people could participate for their own reasons while the movement organized a wider campaign.

The public explanation given by the OUN in October 1943 condemned what it called mutual mass killing and explained the violence through Polish policy, reciprocal conflict and outside powers.

Okay, so that is how the movement explained the violence publicly. But then look at what one of its own Security Service reports says.

## WHAT THE INTERNAL RECORDS SHOW

This is Document 149, an internal report from the Security Service of Zahrava, one of UPA-North's military regions. It covers mid-September to mid-October 1943 and is signed by the section head using the pseudonym “Tretiy.”

This is what it says about the Poles:

> “The action of destroying the Poles did not bring the expected results.”

**[ON SCREEN: `Акція нищення поляків не принесла очікуваних наслідків` with checked English translation. Show Document 149, *Litopys UPA*, New Series, vol. 2; TsDAVO f. 3838, op. 1, spr. 55, ark. 1–5; copy; signed “Tretiy.”]**

That is a very different description of what was happening. The next sentence says that the “active Polish element” had largely survived. So the author is talking about an anti-Polish action and asking whether it achieved the expected result.

The word “destroying” does not tell us by itself whether the intended result was killing, forced flight, territorial removal or some combination. That wider conclusion comes from the territorial aims we have already seen, the attacks themselves and the other contemporary reports.

Taken together, the records show anti-Polish actions being assigned, carried out and evaluated through OUN and UPA structures. What happened varied by place, and this Security Service report describes serious problems with higher control over local branches. Organized did not mean perfectly controlled, but the campaign was still being handled through movement structures.

That is why Poland's objection cannot be reduced to a few UPA units becoming involved in local violence. The anti-Polish campaign is tied to the organization whose name Ukraine is honouring.

Okay. That explains Poland.

But Ukraine's official reason for honouring the UPA is that it belonged to the struggle for an independent Ukraine. So did it?

## THE ANTI-SOVIET STRUGGLE

One UPA leaflet from 1942–43 is called “What Does the Ukrainian Insurgent Army Fight For?” It says the UPA is fighting for an independent united Ukrainian state, and against both Russian-Bolshevik and German occupiers.

**[ON SCREEN, ONLY AFTER EXHIBIT VERIFICATION: leaflet “За що бореться Українська Повстанська Армія?”, TsDAHOU f. 1, op. 70, spr. 999, ark. 129–130. Highlight the independently checked Ukrainian wording and label the English translation.]**

That establishes what the movement said it was fighting for. But a political program does not prove that it actually carried out the struggle it claimed.

For that, we can look at the records written by the people trying to defeat it.

When the Red Army returned in 1944, Soviet security records describe clashes with UPA units, attacks on communications, resistance to mobilization, and major operations to destroy the underground. They record arrests, infiltration and deportations used against the movement and communities supporting it.

**[ON SCREEN: February 1944 Soviet appeal to UPA members; January 1945 NKVD results report; June 1945 Khrushchev memorandum to Stalin. Show producer, date, archive locator and only verified highlighted wording.]**

The Soviets called the insurgents bandits and had no reason to make them look heroic. Yet their reports show armed resistance and a response involving troops, intelligence networks and mass repression.

The form of the resistance changed over time. Large formal UPA formations declined by the end of the 1940s. Smaller groups from the OUN and its Security Service underground continued into the 1950s.

For years after Soviet rule returned, an organized Ukrainian underground continued armed resistance in the name of independence. It also coerced and killed Ukrainians it suspected of helping the Soviets. Taking the struggle seriously does not make it clean, but it was real.

## BACK TO THE TEXTBOOK

Now we can return to the textbook.

The part about expelling Poles and attacking their settlements is supported by the historical record. So is the part about fighting Soviet rule. The textbook teaches both, but only the anti-Soviet struggle is described as heroic.

One textbook cannot tell us what every Ukrainian thinks. But it shows how acknowledgment and heroic memory can coexist: Volhynia is included, while the struggle against Soviet rule explains what the movement contributed.

That memory already existed, especially in western Ukraine. A 2010 presidential decree included OUN and UPA members among twentieth-century independence fighters. In 2015, after Russia seized Crimea and began the war in Donbas, Ukraine passed a law recognizing both organizations and making commemoration of that struggle state policy.

Russian aggression made the anti-Soviet part increasingly relevant across the country. Resistance to Moscow was now connected to Ukraine's survival. The 2026 decree itself says the UPA name restores national military traditions while Ukraine defends its territorial integrity and independence.

But official recognition still does not tell us whether Ukrainians judge the independence struggle and the movement's entire wartime record in the same way.

On 27 April 2022, the Rating Group asked whether OUN-UPA should be recognized as participants in the struggle for Ukrainian independence. Eighty-one percent supported that recognition, and ten percent opposed it.

Five months later, KIIS asked: “How do you, in general, assess the activities of OUN-UPA during the Second World War?” Forty-three percent answered positively, eight percent negatively, and thirty-seven percent found it difficult to judge.

These were different surveys, with different samples, so I would not use them as a trend. But the questions reveal an important difference. Saying that OUN-UPA took part in the independence struggle is much easier than giving one judgment on everything it did during the war.

## POLAND, PUBLIC HONOUR AND RUSSIA

This makes the Ukrainian decision easier for me to understand. During another war against Moscow, the state honours a movement that really did fight Moscow for an independent Ukraine.

But Poland's objection also makes more sense to me now. It is not only asking Ukraine to remember the dead. The two countries have acknowledged the victims together, and their presidents jointly mourned them in 2023. Poland is asking whether the UPA name itself should receive state honour when the anti-Polish campaign is tied to that same organization.

This is not a problem only Ukraine faces. In 2024, one Polish ministry stopped official commemorations of the anti-communist fighter Józef Kuraś, known as “Ogień,” after objections concerning crimes against civilians. The Polish Institute of National Remembrance argued that actions which deserve criticism should not erase his contribution to the struggle for an independent Poland.

I am not saying these cases or the crimes are equivalent. They are not. The comparison is only about the problem of commemoration: what does a state do when a real contribution and serious crimes belong to the same historical name?

Russia takes the OUN's real extremist ideology, its real cooperation with Nazi Germany in 1941, and the real crimes against Polish civilians, and uses them to claim that modern Ukraine is a Nazi state.

That conclusion does not follow. The history of one wartime nationalist movement does not define an entire modern country, and it does not justify Russia's invasion. You can admit the worst parts of this history without accepting the propaganda built from them.

So historically, I think the two main points are quite clear. The UPA and the later nationalist underground really did fight Soviet rule in the name of an independent Ukraine. And the anti-Polish campaign cannot be separated from the OUN and UPA structures being honoured.

I understand the Ukrainian position better now because I had underestimated how real and sustained the anti-Soviet struggle was, and why it became more important after Russia attacked Ukraine again. I also understand Poland's objection better because the internal records do not let us treat Volhynia as something outside the organization.

Where I am still not completely settled is what follows from that. I can imagine honouring a real contribution while openly condemning the crimes committed by the same movement. But I am not sure whether that is enough when the honour is attached to the UPA name itself, rather than only to the specific people or acts being remembered.

And I think that is the difficult question left by the textbook. Both parts can be taught. Both can be historically real. But when a state turns one of them into a public honour, what happens to the other part of the same name?
