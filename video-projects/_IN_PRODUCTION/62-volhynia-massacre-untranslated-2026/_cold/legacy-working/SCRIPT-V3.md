# Why Does Ukraine Still Honour the UPA?

**Status:** V3 — surgically repaired after the latest creator read-through

## OPEN

In May 2026, President Zelensky gave a Ukrainian special-forces unit the honorary name “Heroes of the UPA.” Poland's president responded by withdrawing Zelensky's Order of the White Eagle, Poland's highest honour.

The Polish reaction makes sense once you know what the UPA name means in Poland. It is associated above all with the killing of Polish civilians, especially in Volhynia, where UPA units attacked Polish communities during the Second World War and tens of thousands were killed.

So the harder question for me was why Ukraine considered this honour valuable enough to create a dispute with Poland in the middle of a war with Russia.

Ukraine's answer is that the UPA fought for an independent Ukrainian state. And initially I thought maybe the ugly part of the history simply no longer played much role in the story Ukrainians were being asked to remember.

But then I found a current Ukrainian history textbook which makes that explanation difficult. It says that the Volhynian leadership of OUN-B decided to expel the Polish population, and that UPA units attacked Polish settlements in July 1943. Later, the same textbook describes the UPA's fight against the Soviet secret police as a “heroic struggle.”

So this textbook is teaching the anti-Polish campaign and the anti-Soviet struggle. But it does not tell me how much historical weight each part should carry, or how a movement with both in its record becomes a public honour. To understand that, I first needed to look at the part Poland objects to, and then at the independence struggle Ukraine is honouring.

## HOW THE ANTI-POLISH CAMPAIGN DEVELOPED

The UPA was the Ukrainian Insurgent Army. It was connected to the Bandera wing of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists, or OUN-B. Their objective was an independent Ukrainian state.

Attempts to create that state after the First World War had failed. Most of Ukraine became part of the Soviet Union, while Volhynia and Eastern Galicia became part of Poland. This left millions of Ukrainians inside the Polish state, including nationalists who believed those territories had to belong to a future Ukraine.

Conflict with the Polish state helped radicalize the OUN. Polish rule brought repression, restrictions and promises of autonomy that were never fulfilled. But inside the movement, this was not only a conflict with the Polish government. The Polish population itself became connected to the future border. If large Polish communities remained when Europe's borders were redrawn after another war, Poland could use their presence to claim this land again. Before the Second World War, one OUN military thinker, Mykhailo Kolodzinskyi, had already written about removing what he called the “Polish element.”

**[ON SCREEN: Kolodzinskyi passage. Label: `PRE-WAR DOCTRINE — NOT A 1943 ORDER`.]**

Then the Second World War destroyed the prewar political order in the region and brought successive Soviet and German occupations. In spring 1943, around five thousand Ukrainian auxiliary policemen deserted German service. OUN-B moved to absorb them before rival armed groups could. They did not all follow the same path into the UPA, but those who joined brought weapons, training and experience. Their arrival substantially expanded the growing movement's military capacity.

So by the middle of 1943, ideas about removing the Polish population already existed inside the nationalist movement, and it now had a much larger armed force with which to act on them.

Then, on Sunday, 11 July 1943, UPA units attacked nearly a hundred Polish localities in Volhynia. Some attacks struck churches while people were attending Mass. In Poryck, survivor Jadwiga Krajewska remembered that the first shots came during the Gloria.

Poryck was one place inside a much larger campaign. In Volhynia in 1943, roughly fifty to sixty thousand Polish civilians were killed.

UPA units clearly took part in these attacks. But Poland's objection to honouring the UPA name goes further than participation. It treats the violence as a campaign that the organization itself was directing and implementing.

There is a real disagreement about how much of the violence came from local Polish-Ukrainian conflict, peasant participation and the collapse of order, and how much direction came through OUN and UPA structures. Both can be true at the same time. Local people could participate for their own reasons while the movement organized a wider campaign. The movement's own records are useful because they show what was being handled through its structures rather than only what happened on the ground.

## WHAT THE INTERNAL RECORDS SHOW

Several internal and operational reports survive. One assigns UPA units to particular settlements and reports the results. Another evaluates how the anti-Polish action went from place to place, including places where it was not carried out.

One of the clearest is Document 149.

This is an internal report from the Security Service of Zahrava, one of UPA-North's military regions. The Security Service handled intelligence, investigations and punishments inside the movement. The report covers its work from mid-September to mid-October 1943 and is signed by the Security Service section head using the pseudonym “Tretiy.”

This is what it says about the Poles:

> “The action of destroying the Poles did not bring the expected results.”

**[ON SCREEN: `Акція нищення поляків не принесла очікуваних наслідків` with checked English translation. Show Document 149, *Litopys UPA*, New Series, vol. 2; TsDAVO f. 3838, op. 1, spr. 55, ark. 1–5; copy; signed “Tretiy.”]**

The next sentence says that the “active Polish element” had largely survived. So the author is describing an anti-Polish action and judging whether it achieved the expected result.

The word “destroying” does not tell us by itself whether the intended result was killing, forced flight, territorial removal or some combination. That wider conclusion comes from the territorial aims we have already seen, the attacks themselves and the other contemporary reports.

Taken together, those records show anti-Polish actions being assigned, carried out and evaluated through OUN and UPA structures. They also show that implementation varied from place to place and that higher control over local Security Service decisions was weak. So organized did not mean perfectly centralized or identical everywhere. But organizational direction was not a secondary detail either. The anti-Polish campaign was part of what these structures were doing.

That is why this matters to the opening. Poland is not objecting only because some UPA units became involved in local violence. The movement's own records tie the anti-Polish campaign to the organization whose name Ukraine is honouring.

But Ukraine honours the movement for a different part of its history: the fight for an independent Ukraine against Soviet rule. So the next thing I wanted to check was whether that was a substantial historical struggle or mainly a heroic image created later.

## THE ANTI-SOVIET STRUGGLE

In 1944, the Red Army returned to western Ukraine, and Soviet rule returned with it. The nationalist underground continued fighting.

We do not have to rely on the movement's own heroic accounts to establish this. Soviet security records describe armed clashes with UPA units, attacks on communications, resistance to military mobilization, and major operations designed to destroy the underground. They record arrests, infiltration and deportations used to break the movement and the communities supporting it.

**[ON SCREEN: Soviet operational records, with producer, date and relevant passages.]**

These records were written by the people trying to defeat the movement. They called the insurgents bandits and had no reason to make them look heroic. But their reports still show an armed resistance and a Soviet response involving troops, intelligence networks and mass repression.

The form of the resistance changed over time. Large formal UPA formations declined by the end of the 1940s. Smaller groups from the OUN and its Security Service underground continued into the 1950s.

For years after Soviet rule returned, an organized Ukrainian underground continued armed resistance in the name of independence. This was not only a later symbol. It was a real historical struggle, although the movement also coerced and killed Ukrainians it suspected of helping the Soviets. Taking the struggle seriously does not make it clean or automatically heroic.

## BACK TO THE TEXTBOOK

Now we can return to the textbook.

The part about expelling Poles and attacking their settlements is supported by the historical record. So is the part about fighting Soviet rule. This textbook teaches both, but only the anti-Soviet struggle is described as heroic.

One textbook cannot tell us what every Ukrainian thinks. But it shows how acknowledgment and heroic memory can coexist: Volhynia is included, while the anti-Soviet struggle receives the heroic description.

After independence, positive memory of the UPA was strongest in western Ukraine. Under President Yushchenko, the state tried to move that regional memory toward wider national recognition. Then Russia seized Crimea and began the war in Donbas in 2014. Resistance to Moscow was no longer only a story from western Ukraine. It had become directly relevant to the country's survival.

In 2015, Ukraine officially recognized OUN and UPA members as fighters for independence. Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022 made that connection even stronger. And the 2026 decision to give the UPA name to soldiers now fighting Russia sits within that development.

But official commemoration does not tell us whether people judge the independence struggle and the movement's entire wartime record in the same way.

On 27 April 2022, the Rating Group asked whether OUN-UPA should be recognized as participants in the struggle for Ukrainian state independence. Eighty-one percent supported that recognition, and ten percent opposed it.

In 2025, KIIS asked something broader: “How do you, in general, assess the activities of OUN-UPA during the Second World War?” Thirty-seven percent answered positively, eight percent negatively, and half found it difficult to say.

Half of the people answering that second question could not give either a positive or a negative judgment. These surveys were conducted in different years and asked different questions, so they do not show support rising or falling. What they do show is that recognizing the independence struggle and judging the movement's entire wartime record are not the same question.

## POLAND, PUBLIC HONOUR AND RUSSIA

This helps me understand the Ukrainian state's choice. During another war against Moscow, state commemoration gives more weight to a movement which also fought Moscow for an independent Ukraine.

But Poland's objection is not only about whether Ukraine acknowledges the dead. The two countries have acknowledged the victims together, and their presidents jointly mourned them in 2023. Poland's objection is whether the UPA name itself should receive state honour when the same organization was involved in the anti-Polish campaign.

That objection raises a problem Poland has also faced in its own commemorations. In 2024, one Polish ministry stopped official commemorations of the anti-communist fighter Józef Kuraś, known as “Ogień,” after objections concerning crimes against civilians. The Polish Institute of National Remembrance objected that actions which deserve criticism should not erase his contribution to the struggle for an independent Poland.

I am not saying these cases or the crimes are equivalent. They are not. What is similar is the argument: when a state honours someone for a real independence struggle, what should it do with the other parts of the same history?

Russia takes the OUN's real extremist ideology, its real cooperation with Nazi Germany in 1941, and the real crimes against Polish civilians, and uses them to claim that modern Ukraine is a Nazi state.

That conclusion does not follow. Germany rejected the movement's attempt to declare an independent Ukraine and later repressed its members. More importantly, the history of one wartime nationalist movement does not define an entire modern country, and it does not justify Russia's invasion. You can admit the worst parts of this history without accepting the propaganda built from them.

Where I ended up is that the part Ukraine honours is historically real. For years, the UPA and the later nationalist underground fought Soviet rule in the name of an independent Ukraine. During another war with Russia, I understand why that history has value in Ukrainian public memory.

But Poland is also right that the anti-Polish campaign cannot simply be detached from the organization being honoured. The movement's own records show that campaign being organized, discussed and evaluated through OUN and UPA structures.

I do not think honouring the independence struggle requires denying everything else in the record. But the honour has to be precise. If the Ukrainian state honours the UPA for fighting Soviet rule, it should be equally clear that the organized campaign against Polish civilians belongs to the same history and deserves condemnation. Remembering the victims separately is not quite the same as carrying that distinction inside the honour itself.
