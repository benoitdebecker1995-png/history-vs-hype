# Why Does Ukraine Still Honour the UPA?

**Status:** V2 — rebuilt from the creator read-through
**Exhibit rule:** Archival locators and source metadata appear on screen. They are not spoken unless the viewer needs them to follow the argument.

## OPEN

In May 2026, President Zelensky gave a Ukrainian military unit the honorary name “Heroes of the UPA.” Poland's president responded by withdrawing Zelensky's Order of the White Eagle, Poland's highest honour.

When I saw this, my first question was why Ukraine considered this worth doing in the middle of a war with Russia, when the Polish reaction was completely predictable.

On the surface, the disagreement seems obvious. Ukraine remembers the UPA as people who fought for an independent Ukraine. Poland remembers the killing of Polish civilians in Volhynia during the Second World War.

And initially I thought the answer might be fairly simple. Maybe the ugly part of this history was no longer really part of the story Ukrainians were being asked to remember.

But then I found a current Ukrainian history textbook which makes that explanation difficult. It says that the Volhynian leadership of OUN-B decided to expel the Polish population, and that UPA units attacked Polish settlements in July 1943. Later, the same textbook describes the UPA's fight against the Soviet secret police as a “heroic struggle.”

So it is teaching both. The question is how those two parts fit together, and why one of them ends up defining what the UPA means in Ukraine today. To answer that, I think we have to check both stories properly.

## HOW AN INDEPENDENCE MOVEMENT ARRIVED HERE

The UPA was the Ukrainian Insurgent Army. It was connected to the Bandera wing of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists, usually called OUN-B. Their objective was an independent Ukrainian state.

After the First World War, attempts to create that state failed. Most of Ukraine became part of the Soviet Union, while Volhynia and Eastern Galicia became part of Poland. This left millions of Ukrainians inside the Polish state, including nationalists who believed those territories had to belong to a future Ukraine.

Polish rule included elections and legal Ukrainian parties, but also repression, restrictions and promises of autonomy that were never fulfilled. Most Ukrainians did not join the OUN, and none of this made mass killing inevitable. But inside the nationalist movement, the Polish population became connected to a territorial problem. If large Polish communities remained after the war, Poland could use their presence to claim the land again.

Before the war, one OUN military thinker, Mykhailo Kolodzinskyi, had already written about removing what he called the “Polish element.”

**[ON SCREEN: Kolodzinskyi passage. Label: `PRE-WAR DOCTRINE — NOT A 1943 ORDER`.]**

Then the Second World War destroyed the states and armies that had controlled the region. In spring 1943, around five thousand Ukrainian auxiliary policemen deserted German service. Some joined the UPA willingly, some were forced, and some followed their units. They brought weapons, training and experience with them.

So by the middle of 1943, the movement had a reason to change who would control the territory when the war ended, and it now had an armed force capable of doing it.

On Sunday, 11 July, UPA units attacked around a hundred Polish localities in Volhynia. Some attacks were timed for Sunday Mass. In Poryck, survivor Jadwiga Krajewska remembered that the first shots came during the Gloria.

Poryck was one place in a much larger campaign. In Volhynia in 1943, roughly fifty to sixty thousand Polish civilians were killed.

But there is still an important question here. Is this anti-Polish campaign actually tied to the organization being honoured today?

## WHAT THE MOVEMENT SAID TO ITSELF

This is where one internal document matters.

Zahrawa was one of the movement's regional structures. Its Security Service was the underground's internal security arm. In late 1943, a report about the situation inside that structure was signed with the pseudonym “Tretii.”

And this is the line I want to show you:

> “The action of destroying the Poles did not bring the expected results.”

**[ON SCREEN: `Акція нищення поляків не принесла очікуваних наслідків` — checked translation beneath it. Show: Document 149, *Litopys UPA*, New Series, vol. 2; TsDAVO f. 3838, op. 1, spr. 55, ark. 1–5; copy; signed “Tretii.”]**

That wording matters because it tells us how the movement discussed the violence internally. Its own Security Service called it an action, described its purpose as destroying the Poles, and judged whether it had achieved the expected result.

The same report also complains about serious weaknesses in the higher Security Service's control over local structures. So organized action did not mean perfect control over every person in every village.

We still do not have a signed master order that settles exactly where the first decision came from. Historians disagree about whether it began with the central OUN leadership or the regional command in Volhynia. But for the question we are asking here, the internal report is enough to establish something important: the anti-Polish action belongs to the history of the movement itself.

Okay. That explains Poland.

Ukraine's stated reason for honouring the UPA is that it fought for an independent Ukraine against Soviet rule.

So did they?

## THE ANTI-SOVIET TEST

In 1944, the Red Army returned to western Ukraine, and Soviet rule returned with it. The nationalist underground kept fighting.

We do not have to take its own heroic accounts at face value. Soviet security records describe armed clashes with UPA units, attacks on communications, resistance to military mobilization, and major operations designed to destroy the underground. They record arrests, infiltration and deportations used to break the movement and the communities supporting it.

**[ON SCREEN: Soviet operational records, with producer, date and relevant passages.]**

Those records were written by the people trying to defeat the movement. They called the insurgents bandits; they had no reason to make them look heroic. But their reports still show that this was a real armed resistance which forced the Soviet state to commit troops, intelligence networks and mass repression to eliminate it.

The scale and form of that resistance changed. Large, formal UPA formations declined by the end of the 1940s. After that, smaller groups from the OUN and its Security Service underground continued into the 1950s. So it would be misleading to imagine intact UPA armies fighting all the way through that decade.

This movement really did continue fighting Soviet rule in the name of an independent Ukraine. It also coerced and killed Ukrainians it suspected of helping the Soviets. Taking the independence struggle seriously does not require turning it into a clean one.

## BACK TO THE TEXTBOOK

Now the textbook makes more sense.

The part about expelling Poles and attacking their settlements is supported by the historical record. So is the part about fighting Soviet rule. The textbook includes both, but they do different jobs. Volhynia is something students are told happened. The anti-Soviet struggle is what makes the UPA heroic.

And I think that is closer to the real disagreement. It is not necessarily that Volhynia has been erased. It is which part of UPA history gets to answer the question: who were these people, and what should they represent now?

That anti-Soviet memory already existed when Ukraine became independent in 1991, especially in western Ukraine. Under President Yushchenko, the state tried to give it wider national recognition. But after Russia seized Crimea and began the war in Donbas in 2014, resistance to Moscow was no longer mainly a western Ukrainian memory. It had become immediately relevant to the whole country's survival.

In 2015, Ukraine officially recognized OUN and UPA members as fighters for independence. Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022 made that identification even stronger. And the 2026 decision to give the UPA name to soldiers now fighting Russia follows from that same development.

The polling is interesting because the answer changes depending on exactly what people are asked.

On 27 April 2022, the Rating Group asked whether OUN-UPA should be recognized as participants in the struggle for Ukrainian state independence. Eighty-one percent supported that recognition, and ten percent opposed it.

In 2025, KIIS asked a different question: “How do you, in general, assess the activities of OUN-UPA during the Second World War?” Thirty-seven percent answered positively and eight percent negatively. Fifty percent found it difficult to say, and another five percent said they did not know.

These are not two points on a trend line. The first question names the independence struggle. The second asks people to judge their wartime activity as a whole. Ukrainians respond much more confidently to the first than to the second.

## WHY THE ARGUMENT DOES NOT GO AWAY

This helps me understand the Ukrainian position. When the country is again fighting Moscow for its independence, people reach for an earlier movement that also fought Moscow for Ukrainian independence. That part is not imaginary.

But Poland's objection also becomes clearer. The UPA that fought Soviet rule and the UPA whose own Security Service discussed “the action of destroying the Poles” are not two separate organizations. So when the organization itself is publicly honoured, Poland does not think the Volhynia part can simply be left outside the honour.

Ukraine and Poland have acknowledged the victims together. Their presidents jointly mourned them in 2023. But remembrance of the victims does not by itself answer that objection.

And then there is Russia, which takes the OUN's real extremist ideology, its real cooperation with Nazi Germany in 1941, and the real crimes committed against Polish civilians, and uses them to claim that modern Ukraine is a Nazi state.

That conclusion does not follow. Germany rejected the movement's attempt to declare an independent Ukraine and later repressed its members. More importantly, the history of one wartime nationalist movement does not define an entire modern country, and it does not justify Russia's invasion. You can admit the ugliest parts of this history without accepting the propaganda built from them.

Where I ended up is that I understand the Ukrainian position better, because the evidence forced me to take the anti-Soviet struggle seriously. It happened, and I understand why it matters much more to Ukrainians during another war with Russia.

But I also understand Poland's objection better. The anti-Polish campaign was not just violence that happened somewhere around the movement. Its own internal report discussed that action and whether it had worked.

And I think that's the whole fight. Can you glorify people for one thing they stood for, when they also did other terrible things?
