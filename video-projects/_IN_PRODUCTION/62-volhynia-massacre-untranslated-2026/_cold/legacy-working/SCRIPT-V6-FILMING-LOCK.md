# Why Does Ukraine Still Honour the UPA?

## OPEN

In May 2026, President Zelensky gave a Ukrainian special-forces unit the honorary name “Heroes of the UPA.”

Poland's president later withdrew Zelensky's Order of the White Eagle, Poland's highest honour.

And the Polish reaction makes sense once you know what the UPA means in Poland.

It is tied above all to Volhynia, where UPA units attacked Polish communities during the Second World War and tens of thousands of Polish civilians were killed.

When I first saw this, I thought maybe the answer was actually pretty simple.

Maybe Ukraine could honour the UPA because Volhynia had basically been pushed out of the story, minimized, or explained away.

Then I found a current Ukrainian history textbook which makes that difficult.

It says that the Volhynian leadership of OUN-B decided to expel the Polish population, and that UPA units attacked Polish settlements in July 1943.

Later, the same textbook describes the UPA's fight against the Soviet secret police as a “heroic struggle.”

**[ON SCREEN: exact textbook passages, title, edition and page.]**

So that made the question much more interesting for me.

If a current Ukrainian textbook can say this openly, then we can't explain the honour simply by saying Volhynia has been forgotten.

So why honour the UPA anyway?

And especially, why do it now, when Ukraine is fighting Russia and it obviously creates a problem with Poland?

To answer that, I think we have to test both parts of this properly.

First, what exactly was the UPA's role in Volhynia?

And then, how real was the independence struggle Ukraine says it is honouring?

---

## WHAT HAPPENED IN VOLHYNIA

UPA stands for the Ukrainian Insurgent Army.

It was tied to the Bandera wing of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists, or OUN-B. Their goal was an independent Ukrainian state.

After the First World War, attempts to create that state failed.

Most of Ukraine became part of the Soviet Union. Volhynia and Eastern Galicia became part of Poland, leaving millions of Ukrainians inside the Polish state.

Under Poland, Ukrainians could vote and had legal political parties.

But there was also repression, restrictions and promises of autonomy that were never fulfilled.

That helps explain why part of the nationalist movement radicalized.

It does not make what happened later inevitable, and most Ukrainians were not members of the OUN.

But inside the OUN, the Polish population also became connected to a territorial problem.

If a large Polish population was still living on this land when Europe's borders were redrawn after another war, Poland could use that population to claim the land again.

One of the OUN's military thinkers, Mykhailo Kolodzinskyi, put that idea into writing before the war.

He talked about removing what he called the “Polish element” and ending Polish claims to the Polish character of these lands.

**[ON SCREEN: verified Kolodzinskyi passage. Label clearly: `PRE-WAR DOCTRINE. NOT A 1943 ORDER.`]**

Then the war changed what the movement was capable of doing.

In spring 1943, around five thousand Ukrainian auxiliary policemen deserted German service.

They did not all end up in the UPA. Some joined willingly, some were forced, and some simply followed their units.

But those who joined brought weapons, training and experience with them.

There is one important thing about that police service as well.

Some of those auxiliary police formations had been used by the Germans in the persecution and murder of Jews.

That does **not** mean every policeman who later joined the UPA had personally participated in a killing. We cannot say that.

But it does mean that some of the trained men entering the insurgency came out of units that had already been used for mass violence.

So by 1943, the idea of removing the Polish population was already there.

The police desertions now added trained, armed men to the growing UPA.

Then, on Sunday, 11 July 1943, UPA units attacked nearly a hundred Polish localities in Volhynia.

Some attacks struck churches while people were attending Mass.

In Poryck, survivor Jadwiga Krajewska remembered that the first shots came during the Gloria.

Poryck was only one place inside a much larger campaign.

In Volhynia in 1943, roughly fifty to sixty thousand Polish civilians were killed.

Now, at this point I don't want to just say “UPA units were involved” and move on.

Can we actually see that in their own records?

A few days later, around Huta Stepańska, UPA commander Ivan Lytvynchuk filed an operational report describing attacks on twenty-six Polish settlements.

**[ON SCREEN: Ivan Lytvynchuk “Dubovyi” operational report, 17–19 July 1943, Huta Stepańska area. *Litopys UPA*, vol. 2, pp. 171–176. Highlight only the exact checked passage.]**

So yes, we can actually see UPA operations against Polish settlements in their own reporting.

Local Ukrainians also took part in some attacks.

And that matters, because “local violence” and “UPA violence” are not necessarily two different explanations.

Both could be happening at the same time.

And obviously that does not mean every local Ukrainian took part.

There are also accounts of Ukrainians warning Polish neighbours, hiding them and helping them escape. Some Ukrainians were themselves killed for giving that help.

So the question is not really whether local people participated.

They did.

The harder question is whether this was mainly local Polish-Ukrainian violence exploding in the chaos of occupation, with the UPA getting involved, or whether OUN and UPA structures were themselves organizing a wider anti-Polish campaign.

And one way to test that is to compare what the movement said publicly with what it said internally.

---

## WHAT THE INTERNAL RECORDS SHOW

In October 1943, the OUN publicly described the violence as part of a wider Polish-Ukrainian conflict.

It blamed Polish interwar policy, the Polish underground, Polish auxiliary police, Soviet partisans and the German occupation.

It talked about massacres on both sides and said the OUN opposed the conflict.

Okay.

That is how the movement explained what was happening publicly.

Now look at this.

This is an internal Security Service report from the Zahrava military district in eastern Volhynia.

It covers roughly mid-September to mid-October 1943.

And this is what it says about the Poles:

> “The action of destroying the Poles did not bring the expected results.”

**[ON SCREEN: `Акція нищення поляків не принесла очікуваних наслідків.` Show checked translation, document number, date range, archive provenance and signature “Tretiy.”]**

The next sentence says that the “active Polish element” had largely survived.

So internally they're calling this an action, and they're asking whether it worked.

That line is important.

But I also don't want to make it say more than it does.

What exactly was the expected result?

Killing everybody?

Forcing people to flee?

Removing the Polish population from the territory?

Some combination of those?

This sentence by itself does not tell us.

So you have to put it beside the territorial ideas we just looked at, the attacks themselves, and the other operational reports.

Taken together, that evidence shows anti-Polish actions being carried out, reported on and evaluated through OUN and UPA structures.

That still does not give us a neat signed central order saying, “do this everywhere.”

We don't have that.

And this same report complains about weak higher-level control over local Security Service structures.

So the internal record itself is messier than a perfectly top-down picture.

But whatever was happening locally, this was not separate from the organization.

Its own people are reporting on the anti-Polish action and judging the result.

And that gets much closer to why Poland objects to honouring the UPA name itself.

Okay.

So that's the Polish side of the argument.

Now I wanted to test the Ukrainian side just as hard.

Ukraine says it is honouring the UPA because it fought for an independent Ukraine.

So did it?

And was that more than just some small guerrilla war?

---

## THE ANTI-SOVIET STRUGGLE

In 1943, the UPA published a leaflet called **“What the Ukrainian Insurgent Army Is Fighting For.”**

It says the UPA is fighting for an independent united Ukrainian state and against Russian-Bolshevik and German occupiers.

**[ON SCREEN: “What the Ukrainian Insurgent Army (UPA) Is Fighting For,” 1943. HDA SBU f.13, file 376, vol.60, fol.1r–v. *Enemy Archives*, Doc. 5, pp.102–104.]**

Fine.

That tells us what the movement said about itself.

But did they actually keep fighting Soviet rule once the Red Army came back?

This is where Soviet records become much more useful.

In June 1945, the Ukrainian NKVD sent instructions to its regional offices about OUN members getting inside Soviet institutions.

The document says they were working inside local administrations, land agencies, post and telephone services, military offices and other Soviet institutions.

They were gathering information about officials, security arrangements, prisoners and troop movements.

And that information could be used to plan attacks.

The NKVD document actually warns that:

> “Soviet institutions and enterprises are swamped by a hostile element.”

**[ON SCREEN: June 1945 NKVD guidelines, signed Tymofii Strokach. *Enemy Archives*, pp. 790–791. HDA SBU f.13, file 372, vol.59, fols.38–39. Use published English translation unless original Russian folios are secured.]**

That is much more useful to me than a heroic memoir.

These are the people trying to destroy the movement.

And their own records are saying OUN members are getting inside Soviet institutions, collecting intelligence and helping armed groups operate.

Soviet records also describe major operations, arrests, infiltration and deportations aimed at destroying the underground and the people supporting it.

So this really was an organized resistance.

Not only something the movement claimed about itself.

By the end of the 1940s, the large UPA formations had mostly disappeared.

But smaller OUN and Security Service groups continued into the 1950s.

And the underground also coerced and killed Ukrainians it suspected of helping the Soviets.

So no, recognizing that this struggle was real does not make these people angels or excuse everything else they did.

But the struggle was real.

## BACK TO THE TEXTBOOK

Now go back to the textbook.

The part about expelling Poles and attacking Polish settlements is supported by the historical record.

So is the part about fighting Soviet rule.

Both basic claims check out.

But only one gets described as heroic.

One textbook obviously cannot tell us what every Ukrainian thinks.

But it does show how these two things can exist together.

Volhynia can be there in the history.

And at the same time, the anti-Soviet struggle can become the part of the UPA's history that gets commemorated.

That idea was already developing before the current war.

In 2010, a Ukrainian presidential decree included OUN and UPA members among twentieth-century fighters for Ukrainian independence.

Then in 2015, after Russia seized Crimea and the war in Donbas began, Ukraine passed a law recognizing OUN and UPA as organizations which fought for Ukrainian independence and made commemoration of that struggle part of state policy.

And I think the change after 2014 is pretty obvious.

Resistance to Moscow was no longer only history.

After Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022, that became even more relevant.

The 2026 decree which gave the current military unit the name “Heroes of the UPA” actually says this directly.

It says the name is meant to restore the historical traditions of Ukraine's national army while Ukraine is defending its territorial integrity and independence.

So we don't really have to guess why the Ukrainian state thinks the UPA name is useful.

It tells us.

There is also a difference between saying:

> “The UPA fought for Ukrainian independence.”

and saying:

> “Everything the UPA did was good.”

Those are not the same claim.

In April 2022, the Rating Group asked whether OUN-UPA should be recognized as participants in the struggle for Ukrainian independence.

Eighty-one percent supported that.

Five months later, the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology asked people to assess OUN-UPA's activities during the Second World War more generally.

Forty-three percent answered positively, eight percent negatively, and thirty-seven percent found it difficult to judge.

These are different surveys with different questions, so I'm not using them to claim some trend between April and September.

But these are also different questions.

One asks whether OUN-UPA took part in the struggle for Ukrainian independence.

The other asks people to give an overall judgment on their activities during the Second World War.

Because these were separate surveys with different samples, I wouldn't use the percentages to explain each other.

But the distinction between the questions is important. Recognizing an independence struggle is not the same thing as giving one judgment on everything the movement did during the war.

And this is also where the Russian argument gets slippery.

OUN-B really did have an extremist ideology.

It really did cooperate with Nazi Germany.

And UPA really was tied to the anti-Polish campaign in Volhynia.

Those things are real.

What does **not** follow from that is that modern Ukraine is therefore a Nazi state, or that Russia's invasion becomes justified.

A wartime nationalist movement does not define an entire modern country.

You do not have to clean up the history to reject the propaganda.

## POLAND AND THE PROBLEM OF COMMEMORATION

So this makes the Ukrainian decision much easier for me to understand.

If you're fighting Russia now, I can understand why people who fought Soviet rule for an independent Ukraine become useful historical figures.

I don't think there is anything automatically strange about remembering or admiring that part of what they did.

But Poland's objection also makes much more sense to me now.

Poland is not only asking Ukraine to remember that Polish civilians were killed.

The two countries have already acknowledged the victims together, and their presidents jointly mourned them in 2023.

The harder Polish objection is to the name **UPA** itself receiving state honour.

Because the anti-Polish campaign was tied to that same organization.

And this isn't uniquely Ukrainian.

Poland has its own version of this problem.

In 2024, one Polish ministry stopped official commemorations of the anti-communist fighter Józef Kuraś, known as “Ogień,” after objections concerning crimes against civilians.

The Polish Institute of National Remembrance responded that the actions which deserve criticism should not erase his contribution to the struggle for an independent Poland.

I'm not saying these cases are equivalent.

They're not.

The part I think is comparable is just the problem.

What do you do with somebody who really did fight for independence, but also has serious crimes attached to the same record?

And I think that's pretty close to where I land on the UPA.

---

## WHERE I END UP

So two things are quite clear.

The UPA and the later nationalist underground really did fight Soviet rule for an independent Ukraine.

The same movement was also tied to an organized campaign against Polish civilians.

I now understand why the first part matters so much today.

The UPA gives Ukraine a historical example of people resisting Moscow for an independent state.

And I don't think remembering or even admiring that contribution is itself the problem.

The problem for me begins if making the UPA heroic requires cleaning up the rest of its history.

The extremist ideology.

The cooperation with Nazi Germany.

And the anti-Polish campaign in Volhynia.

Those facts belong to the same movement.

Acknowledging them does not mean accepting Russia's propaganda about modern Ukraine.
