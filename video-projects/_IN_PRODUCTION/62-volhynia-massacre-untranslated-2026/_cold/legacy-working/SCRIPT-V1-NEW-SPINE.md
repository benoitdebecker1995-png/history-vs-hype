# Why Does Ukraine Still Honour the UPA?

## OPEN

In May 2026, President Zelensky gave a Ukrainian military unit the honorary name “Heroes of the UPA.” Poland's president responded by taking away the Order of the White Eagle, Poland's highest honour. Zelensky sent the medal back.

When I first saw this story, I wanted to understand why Ukraine would do this now, while it is fighting Russia, and why Poland considered it serious enough to confront an ally.

The basic disagreement is clear. Ukraine remembers the UPA as people who fought for an independent Ukraine. Poland remembers the same organization because of the killing of Polish civilians during the Second World War.

And I think both stories have a grain of truth. So what I wanted to know was what version the evidence actually supports, and why both countries end up emphasizing such different parts of the same history.

Then I found something that made the question much more interesting.

A current Ukrainian history textbook says that the Volhynian leadership of OUN-B decided to expel the Polish population, and that UPA units attacked Polish settlements in July 1943. But the same textbook later says that the UPA continued its “heroic struggle” against the Soviet secret police into the 1950s.

So the massacre is there, and the heroic story is there as well. Before deciding what that means, I think we need to test both parts properly.

## WHAT HAPPENED IN VOLHYNIA

UPA stands for the Ukrainian Insurgent Army. It was tied to the Bandera wing of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists, or OUN-B, which wanted to create an independent Ukrainian state.

After the First World War, an independent Ukrainian state briefly existed and then disappeared. Most of Ukraine became part of the Soviet Union. Volhynia and Eastern Galicia became part of Poland, which left millions of Ukrainians inside the Polish state.

Polish rule included elections and legal Ukrainian parties, but also repression, restrictions and broken promises of autonomy. Most Ukrainians did not join the OUN, and none of this made the later killing inevitable. But for the nationalists, the Polish population became connected to a territorial problem. If Poles still lived on this land when the war ended, Poland could claim it again.

One of the OUN's military thinkers, Mykhailo Kolodzinskyi, put the idea of removing what he called the “Polish element” into writing before the war.

**[ON SCREEN: Kolodzinskyi passage. Label: `DOCTRINE — NOT THE 1943 ORDER`.]**

By 1943, the nationalist underground also had an army. Around five thousand Ukrainian auxiliary policemen deserted German service that spring. Some joined the UPA willingly, some were forced, and some simply followed their units. But they brought weapons and training with them.

Germany belongs in this background, but the simple labels do not work very well. OUN-B did try to use Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union to declare an independent Ukraine in 1941, and its proclamation promised cooperation with Hitler. That connection was real. But Germany rejected Ukrainian independence and arrested Bandera and Yaroslav Stetsko. The movement later fought some German units, while some UPA commanders also made tactical arrangements with the Germans when the Soviet army returned. Their own objective was an independent Ukraine.

On Sunday, 11 July 1943, UPA units attacked around a hundred Polish localities in Volhynia. Some attacks struck churches while people were attending Mass. At Poryck, survivor Jadwiga Krajewska remembered that the first shots came during the Gloria.

Poryck was one place inside a much wider campaign. Across Volhynia, roughly fifty to sixty thousand Polish civilians were killed in 1943. Polish forces also killed Ukrainian civilians, especially in 1944, and communist Poland later deported around 141,000 Ukrainians during Operation Vistula. That violence was real.

But it does not make Volhynia a spontaneous war between equal sides. The large anti-Polish campaign started earlier, it was meant to change who lived on the land, and it killed far more civilians in Volhynia.

So then the question becomes: how do we know it was organized?

## WHAT THEY SAID IN PUBLIC — AND INSIDE THE MOVEMENT

Publicly, the OUN presented the violence as part of a Polish-Ukrainian conflict. It blamed Polish actions and outside powers and denied organizing the mass killing.

Okay, so that is how the movement explained the violence publicly.

But then look at what one of its own Security Service reports says.

Document 149 in *Litopys UPA*, New Series, volume 2, is a copy of a report from the Zahrawa Security Service structure. It is signed “Tretii,” and its archival reference is TsDAVO of Ukraine, fond 3838, inventory 1, file 55, folios 1 to 5.

It says:

> “The action of destroying the Poles did not bring the expected results.”

**[ON SCREEN: original Ukrainian — `Акція нищення поляків не принесла очікуваних наслідків` — then checked English translation. Keep the signature and archival provenance visible.]**

That is a very different description of what was happening. This is not an outside accusation, written decades later. It is an internal report discussing an anti-Polish action and judging its results.

The same report also describes serious problems with higher Security Service control over local structures. That is an important qualification. An organized campaign does not mean that every local unit followed one perfectly controlled plan, or that we can reconstruct every order from the top.

Another internal report, from the Volodymyr–Horokhiv district in July 1943, makes the uneven execution even clearer. It grades how the action went in different places: well in some districts, badly in others, and not carried out at all in several places. A separate operational report from 27 July records an identified unit reporting on the Huta Stepańska action.

So the reports do not give us the original meeting or an authenticated signed master order. Historians still disagree about whether the first decision came from the central OUN leadership or from Dmytro Kliachkivsky's regional command in Volhynia.

But you cannot call this spontaneous. These are people inside the organization discussing an action, measuring its results and reporting operations. The precise beginning is less certain. The organized campaign is not.

And that explains Poland.

## SO DID THEY FIGHT THE SOVIETS?

But Ukraine obviously is not honouring these people because of Volhynia. It honours them because they fought for an independent Ukraine.

So did they?

Yes. And we do not have to rely only on UPA memoirs or modern Ukrainian claims to establish that.

Soviet security records describe armed clashes with UPA units, attacks on communications, resistance to mobilization and large operations to destroy the nationalist underground. Soviet authorities also deported tens of thousands of people in an attempt to break its support.

**[ON SCREEN: Soviet-produced operational report. Show producer, date and the passage describing the underground as an active security problem.]**

And these records matter because the Soviet security services had no reason to help build a Ukrainian heroic story. They called the insurgents bandits and treated them as enemies of the state. But their own reports still describe an armed movement that required troops, intelligence networks, arrests, infiltrations and mass deportations to defeat. So even if we put the UPA's own version aside, the Soviet response tells us that the resistance was substantial.

This was a real armed struggle. The formal UPA military structure weakened badly by the end of the 1940s, and Roman Shukhevych was killed in 1950. After that, it was mainly the OUN and Security Service underground—not intact UPA field units—that continued in smaller numbers into the 1950s.

That struggle was not clean either. The nationalist Security Service killed suspected collaborators and coerced Ukrainian civilians. But the basic Ukrainian claim is still true: this movement continued fighting Soviet rule for an independent Ukraine after the Germans were gone.

That changed how I understood the honour. The independence struggle was not invented later to hide Volhynia. It was real.

## WHY THAT PART BECAME THE MAIN STORY

Now we can go back to the textbook.

It does not simply remove Volhynia. It acknowledges the expulsion decision and the attacks, but places them inside a broader Polish-Ukrainian confrontation. That account begins with Polish violence and asks students to consider an argument against calling the campaign genocide. The anti-Soviet struggle, meanwhile, is the part described as heroic.

So I don't think factual erasure fully explains what is happening. The more important question is which part gets to define the UPA. Volhynia is present, but the fight for independence remains the answer to the larger question: who were these people?

That memory already existed after Ukraine became independent in 1991, especially in western Ukraine. Under President Yushchenko, it moved further into national state policy. Then Russia seized Crimea and began the war in Donbas in 2014. Suddenly, the story of Ukrainians fighting Moscow was much more relevant across the country. In 2015, a Ukrainian law officially recognized OUN and UPA members as fighters for independence. The full-scale invasion in 2022 made the connection even more immediate.

So the state did not have to persuade people that Volhynia never happened. It could commemorate the part of the UPA story that now looked most similar to Ukraine's own situation: an armed movement fighting Moscow for an independent state. That is also why the 2026 decree is not an isolated decision. It extends a way of remembering the UPA that had been moving from a regional memory into national law and military commemoration for years.

The polling shows why the wording matters. In April 2022, 81 percent supported recognizing OUN-UPA as participants in the struggle for Ukrainian independence, while 10 percent opposed it.

But in 2025, KIIS asked a broader question: how do you assess OUN-UPA activity during the Second World War? Only 8 percent answered negatively, but just 37 percent answered positively. Half said it was difficult to say either way, and another 5 percent did not know.

Those are not numbers you can put on one graph and call a simple rise or fall in support. One question foregrounds the independence struggle. The other asks people to judge everything they did during the war, and that produces much more uncertainty.

## WHY THE ARGUMENT DOES NOT GO AWAY

Ukraine has acknowledged Polish victims. The two countries signed a reconciliation declaration in 1997, and in 2023 their presidents mourned the victims together. But acknowledgment does not settle Poland's objection.

Poland is saying that the people who fought Moscow and the organization that attacked Polish civilians are part of the same history. If the state honours the organization itself, Poland does not think you can select only the independence part of the name.

That is the main point of contention.

Russia then takes the real ideology, the real cooperation with Germany and the real crime in Volhynia, and turns them into a claim that modern Ukraine is a Nazi state. That conclusion does not follow, and none of this history justifies Russia's invasion.

Where I ended up is that I understand the Ukrainian position much better. The anti-Soviet struggle was real, and especially during another war against Russia, I can see why Ukrainians honour it.

But I also understand Poland's objection better, because the anti-Polish campaign was not something that merely happened around the UPA. The movement's own documents connect that action to the organization being honoured.

So the difficult question is still there. Can an organization be publicly honoured for a real struggle for independence while separating that honour from another organized campaign carried out by the same organization?
