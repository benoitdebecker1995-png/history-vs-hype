# Current evidence synthesis

## The current answer in one paragraph

The evidence supports a harder conclusion than either simple national story. OUN-B/UPA forces carried out an organized campaign against Polish communities in Volhynia; the current record strongly supports regional command responsibility but does not supply an authenticated signed master order or establish Bandera's personal operational role. The UPA also conducted a real, sustained anti-Soviet insurgency. Ukrainian state memory can therefore draw on a genuine independence struggle while acknowledging Volhynia without allowing it to define the organization. Poland's objection is precisely that this separation is morally and historically unacceptable when the state honours the organization itself.

## Evidence map

### 1. What happened in Volhynia

**Supported factual core**

- The violence was not adequately explained as a collection of spontaneous local clashes.
- OUN-B/UPA structures pursued the removal of Polish communities from contested territory.
- The large anti-Polish campaign in Volhynia preceded the major 1944 Polish retaliatory actions and was asymmetric in chronology, territorial purpose, geography, and civilian scale.
- Polish underground and state violence against Ukrainian civilians was real. Sahryń and Operation Vistula belong in the account, but they do not make the 1943 Volhynia campaign symmetrical.

**What the evidence does not establish**

- A complete reconstruction of the first decision-making meeting.
- An authenticated signed extermination order.
- Bandera's personal operational direction of the 1943 campaign.
- One universally accepted legal or scholarly label. “Ethnic cleansing,” “genocidal ethnic cleansing,” and “genocide” remain distinct positions; uncertainty over classification does not make the organized campaign uncertain.

### 2. Organization and command

**Strongest current chain**

1. OUN nationalist doctrine supplied a territorial motive and included ideas of removing the Polish population.
2. By 1943 the underground possessed organized units and expanded military capacity.
3. The timing and geographical pattern of attacks show coordination.
4. A 27 July 1943 operational report concerning the Huta Stepańska/Lytvynchuk area reportedly records the liquidation of a Polish settlement by an identified unit.
5. Security Service material from the Zahrawa regional structure reportedly describes Lytvynchuk as an initiator or organizer of killings and burnings.
6. Later high-level material, including July 1944 UHVR/Shukhevych discussions, supports knowledge and retrospective acceptance at senior levels, but does not prove who authored the first 1943 decision.

**Current ceiling**

- Dmytro Kliachkivsky's regional responsibility is strongly supported.
- The dispute over central authorization versus regional initiative remains open.
- Stelmashchuk is supporting evidence only: Soviet custody, coercive context, a surviving copy rather than an accessible original, and retrospective testimony limit its weight.
- Order No. 1/43 is logistical/preservation material, not evidence of an extermination policy. It must not be presented as such.

**Exhibit status**

The operational report and Zahrawa material are promising narration evidence, but no exhibit should be approved until the original page, archive identity, exact original-language words, checked translation, date, and provenance are locked. A defensible summary is not yet a showable document card.

### 3. Public account versus internal record

**Public position**

OUN publications and later defenses framed the killings as a Polish-Ukrainian conflict, blamed Polish conduct and outside powers, or characterized the violence as spontaneous.

**Internal evidence**

Operational and Security Service records describe units, commanders, attacks, killings, and burnings within an organizational structure. This does not solve every question about the originating order, but it is difficult to reconcile with a purely spontaneous-village-conflict explanation.

**Inference**

The clean claim is not “we found the master order.” It is: the available internal record and attack pattern support an organized campaign, while the exact origin and wording of the initiating decision remain unresolved.

### 4. The anti-Soviet insurgency

**Supported factual core**

- The UPA/OUN underground conducted real armed resistance against Soviet power.
- Soviet-produced records document clashes, attacks, resistance to mobilization, counterinsurgency operations, and deportations designed to break the underground and its support networks.
- Organized resistance weakened after 1950, while remnants survived into the 1950s.
- This history is not merely a post-1991 nationalist invention.

**Necessary complication**

- The movement and its Security Service also coerced and killed Ukrainian civilians and suspected collaborators.
- Real anti-Soviet resistance does not sanitize Volhynia; Volhynia does not make the anti-Soviet struggle imaginary.
- Insecure battlefield numbers, including disputed Hurby figures, should not carry narration.

### 5. Germany and the OUN/UPA

**Supported synthesis**

- OUN-B cooperation with Nazi Germany in 1941 was real, including the independence proclamation's cooperation language.
- German repression of OUN-B leadership was also real.
- UPA forces undertook some anti-German actions, generally limited relative to their longer anti-Soviet struggle.
- Some commanders later made tactical contacts or arrangements with German forces as the Soviets returned.

**Inference**

Neither “German puppet” nor “major anti-Nazi resistance movement” adequately describes the full record. The movement pursued its own nationalist goal and changed tactics according to the threat and opportunity it perceived.

### 6. The 2023 textbook finding

**What is presently supportable about the exact Vlasov/Kulchytsky/Panarin textbook**

Independent reporting on the 2023 grade-ten textbook describes approximately three pages under the heading “The essence of the Ukrainian-Polish confrontation during the Second World War.” The treatment reportedly:

- begins with attacks by the Polish underground on Ukrainians;
- says the Volhynia regional OUN-B leadership decided to expel the Polish population;
- describes UPA attacks on Polish settlements on 11–13 July 1943;
- explains territorial fears and vengeance as motives;
- presents a Patryliak argument against the genocide classification and asks students to support or refute it;
- includes casualty estimates while showing that Ukrainian textbooks are not uniform in detail or framing.

This supports the broad proposition that Volhynia can be acknowledged inside an interpretive structure that does not make it the defining truth about the UPA.

**Critical locator boundary**

The exact phrase “heroic struggle” has not been securely verified in the exact 2023 Vlasov/Kulchytsky/Panarin school textbook. That wording is verifiable in other official Ukrainian educational or memory materials, including Ukrainian Institute of National Memory material and a Ukrainian university teaching guide. These sources support the broader coexistence of acknowledgment and heroic framing, but they must not be described on screen or in narration as the same textbook page.

**Safe claim now**

> A current Ukrainian textbook can acknowledge the expulsion decision and the attacks while framing them inside a wider Polish-Ukrainian confrontation; separate official educational and memory material explicitly presents the UPA struggle as heroic.

**Unsafe claim now**

> This exact textbook calls the UPA's struggle heroic on the same page where it acknowledges Volhynia.

That second sentence remains unsupported until the original textbook pages are inspected and locked.

### 7. How UPA memory became nationally useful

**Chronology supported by the current research**

- After 1991, positive UPA memory was strongest in western Ukraine and remained contested nationally.
- Under Yushchenko, the state moved the UPA further into national commemorative policy.
- After Russia's seizure of Crimea and the war in Donbas in 2014, resistance to Moscow became more politically and emotionally useful.
- The 2015 law recognized OUN/UPA members as fighters for Ukrainian independence.
- Decommunization removed Soviet symbols and opened public space for replacement figures and traditions.
- The full-scale invasion after 2022 accelerated the relevance of an armed anti-Moscow genealogy.
- UINP material explicitly links UPA memory to Ukrainian military and state-building traditions and, in commemorative material, to present defenders.

**Mechanism**

The state did not need to prove that every UPA action was heroic. It could define the honoured category as resistance for independence, place Volhynia in a separate conflict or tragedy frame, and connect the usable anti-Soviet strand to present defense against Russia.

This is not necessarily total erasure. It is selection, hierarchy, and compartmentalization.

### 8. Polling and public opinion

**Verified 2017 result**

- 49% supported recognizing OUN-UPA participants as fighters for Ukrainian independence.
- 29% opposed.
- 23% found it difficult to answer.
- Regional support varied sharply: approximately 80% west, 51% center, 19% east, and 30% south.

**Interpretive limit**

This measures acceptance of the “fighters for independence” status. It does not measure approval of every wartime action, knowledge of Volhynia, or willingness to honour the organization as a whole. Later surveys using a broader evaluation question are not directly interchangeable. A single upward line combining differently worded questions would be misleading.

### 9. Poland's actual objection

Polish official objections are not satisfied merely by demonstrating that Ukrainian materials mention Polish victims. The deeper claim is organizational: the same OUN/UPA tradition being honoured is tied to the campaign against Polish civilians. Poland rejects the idea that a state can officially select only the independence struggle carried by the name while treating the massacre as a separate historical compartment.

That is why acknowledgment, joint mourning, reconciliation statements, and renewed exhumations can coexist with a continuing dispute over honour.

### 10. Russia's use of the history

Russia exploits real facts—the OUN's ideology, 1941 cooperation, wartime crimes, and Bandera symbolism—to construct a false totalizing conclusion about modern Ukraine and to justify “denazification.” The inference does not follow. A rigorous video should concede the history without adopting the propaganda conclusion.

## Strongest counterarguments the video must preserve

1. **Ukrainian counterargument:** The anti-Soviet insurgency was real, independence was the movement's governing political aim, and current commemoration addresses that struggle rather than Volhynia.
2. **Polish counterargument:** Honouring an organization is not the same as honouring an abstract aspiration; the organization's campaign against civilians cannot be detached from its public name.
3. **Historical-method counterargument:** An organized campaign can be established without a signed master order, but gaps in the command record must not be filled by certainty.
4. **Memory counterargument:** Inclusion of Volhynia in education disproves a claim of total erasure, but inclusion alone does not show equal narrative weight or institutional accountability.

## Current unresolved evidence gaps

- Original pages and checked translations for the July 1943 operational report and Zahrawa material.
- Exact original pages of the 2023 Vlasov/Kulchytsky/Panarin textbook, including the full sequence, captions, exercises, and neighboring UPA treatment.
- Whether any exact “heroic struggle” formulation occurs elsewhere in that same textbook; it must not be inferred from other official materials.
- The best single Soviet-produced document for a visible, source-led anti-Soviet scene.
- Exact pages for the most useful memory-scholarship claims, with each scholar's interpretation kept distinct.
- A clean primary locator for the relevant Polish official objection and the 2026 Ukrainian decree before filming.

## Current thesis with calibrated confidence

**High confidence:** Ukraine's honour is built around a real anti-Soviet independence struggle; Poland's objection is built around a real organized campaign against Polish civilians by the same movement.

**Moderate-to-high confidence:** Ukrainian official memory can acknowledge Volhynia while containing its significance through conflict framing, classification disputes, and selection of the anti-Moscow strand as the honoured legacy.

**Unresolved:** The exact balance between deliberate state narrative design, wartime mnemonic necessity, regional inheritance, institutional activism, and broader public belief. The video should present these as mechanisms with evidence, not pretend one monocausal explanation has been proven.
