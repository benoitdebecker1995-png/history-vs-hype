# Voice audit — Script V1, new spine

**Script audited:** `SCRIPT-V1-NEW-SPINE.md`  
**Primary voice evidence:** literal T8 Volhynia ad-lib, relevant retrieved raw corpus examples, and creator-approved T4–T6 narration  
**Method:** compare actual script sentences with literal Benoit language and thought movement; abstract voice rules are secondary  
**Spoken length:** approximately 1,812 words, excluding headings and production notes

## Verdict

The draft is substantially closer to Benoit's literal voice than the failed rebuild. Its strongest feature is not word choice but movement: Benoit asks why the dispute matters, tests the anti-Polish evidence, then says “that explains Poland” and genuinely tests the Ukrainian case. The conclusion changes because the anti-Soviet evidence earns that change.

The draft does not copy the raw transcript's filler or factual uncertainty. It retains the raw causal scaffolding, ordinary connectors, source-first reasoning, and moderately long sentences where the logic requires them.

No structural voice failure was found. A few sentences are more compressed or formal than Benoit's spontaneous speech, but they carry necessary source precision and do not reorganize his thought.

## Voice risks retained for read-aloud

### 1. Necessary archival density

The Document 149 identification—volume, signature, fond, inventory, file and folios—is denser than Benoit's normal speech. It earns its place because the creator explicitly wants the document shown and its provenance established. Delivery should allow the exhibit to carry part of this information; the spoken sentence can be shortened after the read-aloud if the locator becomes a stumble.

### 2. “Substantial”

The sentence “the Soviet response tells us that the resistance was substantial” is accurate and clear, but Benoit is more likely to say “this was a real armed movement” or “this was not something small.” It is not a present failure; test it aloud.

### 3. Formal UPA/underground distinction

The distinction between weakened formal UPA structures and the later OUN/SB underground is necessarily technical. The sentence is still syntactically plain. Do not simplify it into the inaccurate claim that intact UPA units broadly fought into the mid-1950s.

### 4. The modern-memory explanation

“It extends a way of remembering the UPA” is slightly more writerly than the surrounding language. The causal explanation around it is clear, and the sentence avoids academic terms such as “mnemonic security” or “usable genealogy.” Let the read-aloud decide whether “It continues something that had already been happening for years” sounds more natural.

## Anti-pattern check

- **Artificial punch fragments:** No recurring fragment rhythm. Short lines appear only at earned turns: “So did they?” and “And that explains Poland.”
- **Decorative symmetry:** The two historical halves are compared because the evidence requires it, not to manufacture balance.
- **Fake quotation or source-pointing:** The script names Document 149, reads its exact translated sentence, and explains the document's weight. It does not say “as you can see on screen.”
- **Repeated proof formulas:** The draft does not make every beat say “this proves X but not Y.” The main evidentiary limit is stated once around Document 149 and once in the concise command-origin ceiling.
- **Institutional voice:** No “the evidence suggests a complex interplay,” “narrative construction,” “collective-memory regime,” or similar language.
- **Thought-order drift:** No new belief update, Germany investigation, command-order chapter, or genocide-definition chapter was introduced.

## Audit conclusion

The script is ready for a creator read-aloud as V1. The read-aloud should focus on four seams only: the textbook transition into background, the full archival locator for Document 149, “the resistance was substantial,” and the modern-memory sentence beginning “That is also why the 2026 decree…”. These are local delivery questions, not reasons to reopen the approved spine.
