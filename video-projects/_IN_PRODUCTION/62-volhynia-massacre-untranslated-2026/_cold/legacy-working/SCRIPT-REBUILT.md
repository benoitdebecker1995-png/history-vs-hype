# Why Does Ukraine Still Honour the UPA?

## OPEN

In May 2026, Zelensky gave a Ukrainian military unit the honorary name “Heroes of the UPA.” Poland's president responded by taking away the Order of the White Eagle, Poland's highest honour. Zelensky sent the medal back.

When I first saw this story, I wanted to understand why Ukraine did this, and why this was such a big deal for Poland. Ukraine is fighting a war. Poland is one of the countries helping it. So why create this argument now?

The basic explanation is not very difficult. Ukraine remembers the UPA as people who fought for an independent Ukraine. Poland remembers the same organization because of the killing of Polish civilians during the Second World War.

And I think both stories have a grain of truth. So what I wanted to know was what happens when you put them together.

## WHAT UKRAINE SAYS

I first wanted to check whether Volhynia is simply missing from the Ukrainian version.

At least in some official educational material, it isn't. A Ukrainian history textbook from 2023 says that the regional OUN-B leadership decided to expel the Polish population from Volhynia, and that UPA units attacked Polish settlements in July 1943.

But the chapter begins with Polish violence and places Volhynia inside a longer Polish-Ukrainian conflict. It even asks students to consider an argument against calling the campaign genocide.

So simple denial doesn't fully explain this. The killing can be present, but it is not necessarily the part of the UPA story that defines the organization.

To understand why, we need to look at what the UPA actually did, on both sides of this story.

## HOW POLAND BECAME PART OF THE PROBLEM

The UPA grew out of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists, or OUN, which wanted to create an independent Ukrainian state.

After the First World War, that state did not survive. Most of Ukraine became part of the Soviet Union. Volhynia and Eastern Galicia became part of Poland, which left millions of Ukrainians inside the Polish state.

There were Ukrainian parties and elections, but there was also repression, restrictions and broken promises of autonomy. Most Ukrainians did not join the OUN, and none of this made the later killing inevitable. But for the nationalists, the Polish population created a territorial problem. If these lands were full of Poles, Poland could claim them again after the war.

One of the OUN's military thinkers, Mykhailo Kolodzinskyi, wrote that a future uprising should remove what he called the Polish element from western Ukrainian lands.

**[ON SCREEN: original page, checked translation, label `IDEOLOGY — NOT THE 1943 ORDER`.]**

Okay, so the removal idea existed. But an idea is not an army.

In 1941, OUN-B tried to use Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union to declare an independent Ukraine. The proclamation promised cooperation with Hitler's Germany. That part is real.

But Germany did not want an independent Ukraine. Bandera and Yaroslav Stetsko refused to withdraw the proclamation, and the Germans detained them. Bandera was still in German custody during the Volhynia massacres, so I could not find evidence that ties him personally to the operational orders in 1943. The underground he helped lead continued without him.

The UPA later attacked German positions, raided prisons and fought for weapons and territory. But these operations were limited, and by 1944 some UPA commanders were also making tactical arrangements with the Germans because the Soviet army was returning. So I don't think either simple label works. They pursued their own Ukrainian political goal, sometimes against Germany and sometimes through cooperation with it. Their much longer fight was against the Soviet state.

Then, in spring 1943, around five thousand Ukrainian auxiliary policemen deserted German service. Some joined the UPA willingly. Some were forced. Some had nowhere else to go. But they brought weapons and training, and suddenly the nationalist underground had the core of an army.

## WHAT HAPPENED IN VOLHYNIA

On 11 July 1943, UPA units attacked more than a hundred Polish localities in Volhynia.

One of them was Poryck. People were attending Sunday Mass when the church was surrounded and attacked. Survivor Jadwiga Krajewska remembered that the first shots came during the Gloria.

**[ON SCREEN: Poryck locator, Motyka page, then named-victim registry.]**

Poryck was one place. Across Volhynia, roughly fifty to sixty thousand Polish civilians were killed in 1943. The Siemaszko registry records the victims village by village, many of them by name.

Polish forces also killed Ukrainian civilians. Sahryń in 1944 is one example, and later the communist Polish state forcibly removed about 141,000 Ukrainians during Operation Vistula. I don't think we should hide that.

But it still doesn't make Volhynia a spontaneous war between equal sides. The large anti-Polish campaign started earlier, it was meant to change who lived on the land, and it killed far more civilians in Volhynia.

So then the question becomes: how do we know it was organized?

## WHAT THEY SAID IN PUBLIC—AND INSIDE THE MOVEMENT

Publicly, the OUN described the killing as part of a Polish-Ukrainian conflict. It blamed Polish actions and outside powers, and it denied organizing the mass violence.

Okay, so that is what the movement wanted people to hear.

But then look at the internal record. A UPA operational report from July 1943 describes an identified unit attacking a Polish settlement. Security Service material from the same regional structure describes one commander as an organizer of killings and burnings.

**[ON SCREEN: one whole operational page, short checked quotation, provenance and date. Then Zahrawa report only if the original page is locked.]**

That is a very different description from spontaneous villagers losing control. These are units acting, commanders being discussed and reports moving through an organization.

The exact beginning is harder. Historians disagree about whether the first decision came from the central OUN leadership or from Dmytro Kliachkivsky's regional command in Volhynia. We also don't have an authenticated signed master order that settles it.

Stelmashchuk later told Soviet interrogators that Kliachkivsky had passed on a secret oral directive to destroy the Polish population. But he was in Soviet custody, the surviving record is a copy, and the original file cannot now be checked. So I would use that as supporting evidence, not as the one document that decides everything.

What we do have is enough to call the campaign organized. It is not enough to reconstruct the original conversation word for word, or to say Bandera personally ordered it.

And that explains why Poland does not see the UPA as freedom fighters who happen to have one difficult chapter. For Poland, the organization is tied directly to the destruction of Polish communities.

## DID THEY ACTUALLY FIGHT THE SOVIETS?

But Ukraine is not honouring the UPA because of Volhynia. So I wanted to test the Ukrainian story in the same way.

Did they actually fight Soviet rule in the way Ukraine says they did?

Yes. And we don't have to rely only on UPA memoirs for that.

Soviet records describe armed clashes, attacks on communications, resistance to mobilization and large security operations against the UPA and OUN underground. The Soviets deported tens of thousands of people in an attempt to break the movement's support. Organized resistance declined after 1950, but parts of the underground survived into the 1950s.

**[ON SCREEN: Soviet-produced 1944 report and later security report, with producer/date visible.]**

So that part isn't invented either. These people did continue fighting for an independent Ukraine after the Germans were gone.

That doesn't make everything they did heroic. The OUN Security Service killed suspected collaborators and coerced Ukrainian civilians as well. But the independence struggle itself was real.

## HOW THAT BECAME THE HEROIC STORY

When Ukraine became independent in 1991, memory of the UPA was still mainly regional. It was much stronger in western Ukraine and heavily contested elsewhere.

Under President Yushchenko, it moved further into national state policy. Then, after Russia annexed Crimea and the war in Donbas began in 2014, the part of the UPA story about resisting Moscow became much more useful.

In 2015, Ukraine passed a law recognizing OUN and UPA members as fighters for independence. After the full-scale Russian invasion in 2022, public support increased again. But the polling depends very heavily on the question. Asking whether they were independence fighters is not the same as asking whether people approve of everything OUN-UPA did during the war.

For example, one national survey in 2017 found that 49 percent supported recognizing OUN-UPA as independence fighters, with very large regional differences. Later surveys asking people to evaluate what OUN-UPA did during the Second World War use a different question and produce a different kind of answer. So I would not put those percentages on one graph and call it a simple rise in approval. They tell us that the independence label became more acceptable. They do not tell us that everybody agrees about Volhynia.

The 2026 decree is the latest step in that process. It did not suddenly invent UPA memory. It took a real anti-Soviet struggle, already recognized by the state, and connected it to soldiers fighting Russia now.

## WHY ACKNOWLEDGMENT DOES NOT SATISFY POLAND

And this takes me back to the textbook.

The simple version would be that Ukraine honours the UPA because it doesn't know or teach what happened to the Poles. I don't think the evidence gets us all the way there. At least some Ukrainian educational material does acknowledge the expulsion decision and the attacks.

But Poland's objection is not solved by putting Volhynia in a textbook. Poland is saying that the people who fought Moscow and the organization that attacked Polish civilians are part of the same history. If the state honours the organization itself, Poland does not think you can choose only the independence part of the name.

And it is not that Ukraine has never acknowledged the victims. The two countries signed a reconciliation declaration in 1997, and in 2023 Zelensky and the Polish president mourned them together. Those gestures were real. But they did not settle the question of what Ukraine should call the crime, or whether the UPA itself should be honoured.

Russia then takes this very real crime and uses it to call modern Ukraine a Nazi state. That conclusion does not follow. The OUN's ideology, its cooperation with Germany and Volhynia are real history. They still do not make present-day Ukraine Nazi, and they do not justify invading it.

## WHERE I ENDED UP

I understand much better now why Ukrainians honour the UPA. The anti-Soviet insurgency was real, and especially during another war against Russia, I can see why that history matters.

I don't think honouring a struggle for independence is automatically wrong. My problem starts when keeping people heroic requires you to soften or remove what they did when it no longer fits the story.

But I also would not say now that Ukraine simply erases Volhynia. That is too broad. The more difficult problem is that the massacre can be acknowledged and still not become the part that defines the UPA in Ukrainian memory.

Poland refuses that separation. And that, I think, is the whole fight: can you honour an organization for one thing it fought for, when the same organization also did something you would never want included in the honour?
