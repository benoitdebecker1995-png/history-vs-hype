## SCRIPT

### COLD OPEN

In August 1943, a Ukrainian commander wrote a report to his superiors. It reads: "I liquidated all Poles from young to old. I burned all the buildings and took the possessions and livestock for the needs of the battalion."

> [ON-SCREEN: the Klymchak after-action report BANGS on "It reads" — McBride p. 648. Caption: "a UPA after-action report, preserved in a Soviet case file, reproduced by historians."]

Eighty-three years later, on May 26th, 2026, Ukraine's president signed a decree naming an elite special-forces unit after the army that man served — the UPA — and called them heroes. Three weeks later, Poland took its highest award back from Ukraine's president. It did that because, for Poland, these men are the main perpetrators of the Volhynia massacre — the killing that report describes. To Poland, that decree sounded like Germany naming a brigade after the SS.

> [ON-SCREEN: the 26 May 2026 decree · White Eagle revoked by Nawrocki, 19 Jun 2026 · three former presidents returning their awards in solidarity.]

The killings themselves are not seriously disputed. The disagreement is what kind of campaign this was.

Poland calls it genocide — a decision by the UPA leadership to destroy the Polish population of the region. Ukraine's memory institute and Volodymyr Viatrovych call it a Second Polish-Ukrainian War, two underground movements fighting each other over the same land.

Most English-language historians reject the two-sided-war description and call it ethnic cleansing. Grzegorz Motyka, the leading Polish historian of this subject, calls it genocidal ethnic cleansing.

These are not three words for the same thing. A two-sided war means two forces fighting each other. Ethnic cleansing means organizing violence to remove a population from a territory. Genocide means the intention went further than removal. It means destroying the group.

That difference matters now. Ukraine says it is honouring the UPA for fighting for independence, not for killing Poles. Poland says you cannot separate that fight from what the same army did to Polish civilians in 1943.

The attacks and the reports can show whether the massacre was organized. The harder question is whether the decision can be traced to the UPA leadership. That is the one thing nobody can produce — an order from the top that everyone can inspect.

### CH 1 — UKRAINE'S CASE

Ukraine's case starts with what the UPA was fighting for. Ukraine remembers the Ukrainian Insurgent Army as a resistance movement that kept fighting Soviet rule after almost every other hope of independence had disappeared.

Bandera led the nationalist faction that later built the UPA. In 1941, that faction declared Ukrainian independence. Germany ordered the declaration withdrawn. The faction refused, so Bandera was arrested and later held in Sachsenhausen. The underground he left behind turned on the Germans too, raiding prisons and ambushing convoys.

After Germany's defeat, the UPA kept fighting Soviet rule. Its organized resistance survived into the early 1950s.

The same Ukrainian memory institutions describe Volhynia as a two-sided war. They point to Polish attacks on Ukrainian civilians, the deportation of around a hundred and forty thousand Ukrainians in Operation Vistula, and the absence of an authenticated kill order bearing the Volhynian commander's signature.

So if Ukraine does not deny that Poles were killed, why risk the alliance by honouring the army that killed them?

The answer starts after Russia seized Crimea in 2014. Ukraine needed historical figures who had fought Moscow, and the UPA had done that for years. Soviet names came off streets and Soviet monuments came down. A 2015 law then recognized both the nationalist movement and the UPA as fighters for Ukrainian independence and made it illegal to publicly disrespect them. The 2026 decree extends that policy.

> [ON-SCREEN: "UKRAINE'S CASE" · UPA anti-Soviet activity · Bandera arrest and Sachsenhausen · Operation Vistula · Law #2538-1 · Crimea 2014. ⚠ **MANDATORY under the raids line:** Piotrowski's hedge — UPA anti-German actions were "demonstrative and propagandistic… not to weaken German military potential" (blind-spot audit ③, `01-VERIFIED-RESEARCH.md:400`). The VO states this as *Ukraine's case*, not as our finding; the card is what keeps it from reading as our finding.]

So that's why honoring these men is useful now.

### CH 2 — POLAND'S CASE

Poland starts with what happened to the civilians.

Its case is that the killing was not local chaos and it was not an equal war. The UPA set out to remove the Polish population from Volhynia. Villages were attacked on the same days, commanders reported what they had done, and somewhere between fifty and sixty thousand Poles were killed there in 1943.

Poland's position is not that Ukrainians never died. Its own parliament acknowledges the crimes committed against Ukrainian civilians in the same resolution that calls Volhynia a genocide. What Poland rejects is the claim that those reprisals turn the UPA's earlier campaign into a spontaneous war between equal sides.

Poland accepts that nobody can produce an authenticated order everyone can inspect. Its answer is that the attacks and the reports show the pattern anyway. That is why Poland uses the word genocide, and why naming a military unit after the UPA caused this reaction.

> [ON-SCREEN: "POLAND'S CASE" · 2016 Sejm resolution · Volhynia remembrance.]

The movement was already writing about removing Poles before 1943.

### CH 3 — THE MOTIVE

After the First World War, Ukrainians proclaimed a state of their own. This state lasted for about three years. The east of Ukraine fell to the Soviets. In the west, Volhynia and Galicia went to the new Poland. Ukrainians who wanted their own state were left without one.

Dmytro Dontsov became the intellectual father of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists — the OUN. He made the nation the highest moral value. If an action helped the nation get its state, then the action was moral.

One of the movement's military thinkers, Mykhailo Kolodzinskyi, wrote that a future uprising should "sweep away literally to the last man the Polish element from the Western Ukrainian Lands." His reason was territorial: land full of someone else's people is land someone else can claim.

> [ON-SCREEN: post-WWI map · Dontsov · Kolodzinskyi's *Military Doctrine*, *Ukraina Moderna* 20 (2013), p. 266, with Himka's English rendering at p. 154. Card must read "the Polish element."]

This document does not prove who gave an order in 1943. It does show that removing the Polish population was part of the movement's thinking before the massacre began.

### CH 4 — THE FALSE DAWN

In the summer of 1941, Germany invaded the Soviet Union and the OUN made its move. On June 30th, Yaroslav Stetsko proclaimed an independent Ukrainian state.

The text survives, signed by Stetsko. It says the new state would "collaborate closely with National Socialist Greater Germany, which under the leadership of Adolf Hitler is creating a new order in Europe and the world."

> [ON-SCREEN: the Act of Restoration, 30 June 1941 — Stetsko signature and Hitler passage highlighted.]

This wasn't necessarily because they were devoted Nazis. In 1941, after two decades of terror, deportations, and famine, Moscow was the biggest obstacle to an independent Ukraine. Germany had allowed client states in Slovakia and Croatia, and the OUN thought Ukraine might be next.

Hitler's government had a different plan. It treated Ukraine as conquered territory to be exploited and eventually settled, not as a country allowed to govern itself. German officials ordered the proclamation withdrawn. Bandera and Stetsko refused, and both were formally arrested in September 1941. Bandera was later sent to Sachsenhausen.

> [ON-SCREEN: Bandera arrest 15 Sep 1941 → Sachsenhausen (RL pp. 245–46, 286–87) — ⚠ **MANDATORY caption**, because the VO no longer says it: "held in a special wing for political prisoners, privileges ordinary inmates didn't get — but a prisoner all the same." · brothers in Auschwitz (RL pp. 381–397) · German colony/Lebensraum plan (Plokhy pp. 287, 297) — ⚠ still the ONLY place the video says *why* Berlin refused; give it a beat and a highlight.]

I went back to the exact wording because the movement later changed it. After the war, OUN émigrés reprinted the proclamation without the passage promising collaboration with Hitler's Germany. An edited version was later displayed in a Bandera museum outside Lviv. That does not prove a Volhynia order was destroyed. It shows that the movement changed the document later, and cut the passage about Hitler out of it.

> [ON-SCREEN: original and sanitized proclamation side by side — RL pp. 509 and 545.]

German repression pushed the OUN-B underground, and from there it started building an army.

### CH 5 — THE ARMY

By 1942, thousands of Ukrainians who would later join the UPA were serving as German auxiliary policemen. They helped the occupation keep order and round up Jews for murder. Some joined for wages and food. Others wanted to protect their families from forced labour, while some wanted the power the uniform gave them over their neighbours.

The OUN leadership had also ordered its own members into the police so they could get trained and collect weapons.

> [ON-SCREEN: Himka pp. 275/279 · 29 Mar 1942 OUN instruction ("at least one OUN-B member in every police unit") · Blue Police context, Piotrowski pp. 108–109 — ⚠ never overstate the symmetry. ⚠ The VO's "round up Jews for murder" is softer than Himka pp. 275/279, which has the auxiliary police participating in the killing — his call, logged; the card must not soften it further.]

Why they needed that army became clear in 1943. Germany was losing. The OUN leadership wanted those policemen before anyone else took them, and many were leaving German service anyway. The nationalist leadership expected the map of Eastern Europe to be redrawn again, and believed a restored Poland would try to reclaim the region.

So around five thousand Ukrainian policemen deserted German service that spring and joined the nationalist underground with their weapons and training. Not all of them were hardened killers. But some were already killing Poles on the way to join the underground.

A policeman named Redesha later explained: "We burned Polish settlements so there would be no trace of their existence, and so that the Poles would never again try to lay claim to Ukraine. It was explained to us that by doing this, it would make it easier to bring about the coming Ukrainian revolution."

> [ON-SCREEN: Snyder pp. 166–167 — ⚠ **MANDATORY: Snyder's verbatim + his name on screen**, because "a restored Poland" is paraphrased in VO and no quoted judgment may sit in an unnamed mouth · desertions 19 Mar–14 Apr 1943, ~5,000 of 12,000 (RL p. 547) · Redesha, McBride pp. 648/653, char-exact. ⚠ **MANDATORY and must be legible** — caption: "testimony to Soviet interrogators, HDA SBU f. 13, spr. 1020." The VO dropped "under interrogation" on his call, so this caption is the video's only duress marker on this quote, and CH7 asks the viewer to discount Stelmashchuk's confession on exactly that ground. · McBride pp. 651–52: not all 5,000 were ideologues — never paint the whole army with one quote.]

Now the idea had men to carry it out.

### CH 6 — THE ATTACKS

On Sunday, July 11th, 1943, the UPA hit around a hundred Polish villages at the same time. Many villagers were at mass. In Poryck, the attackers surrounded the church and fired machine guns through the doors. Around two hundred people died in that village that morning, and around four thousand died across Volhynia that day.

By the end of 1943, somewhere between fifty and sixty thousand Poles were dead in Volhynia. A Polish husband-and-wife team, the Siemaszkos, spent decades building a registry. Their volumes contain about thirty-six thousand names, one by one.

> [ON-SCREEN: McBride p. 640 · Poryck, Siemaszko p. 896 · registry pages and names.]

The Ukrainian case is right that Poles also killed Ukrainians. Polish attacks came back the other way in 1944, and Operation Vistula followed in 1947. But the timing and the scale are not equal. Timothy Snyder, working from Grzegorz Motyka's research, puts the wider conflict at roughly seventy thousand dead Poles to twenty thousand dead Ukrainians.

And it wasn't Poles against Ukrainians as whole peoples. The UPA's own security service shot Ukrainians it considered unreliable — thousands of them, including men who refused to kill Poles.

> [ON-SCREEN: SB purge — Piotrowski p. 254, citing Zynovii Knysh: "the Banderowcy killed about four thousand Ukrainian political opponents alone." ⚠ Single-citation-chain figure — the VO says "thousands," never the exact number (Rule 9); order of magnitude corroborated by Snyder p. 166 and RL/Motyka (889 executed of 938 investigated, Volhynia, Jan–Apr 1945). Concrete case, same page: Slavko Hulub, "hung and perforated with bayonets by the UPA for refusing to participate in the killing of Poles," sign on his chest: "Khto ne z namy, toi proty nas."]

That coordination is why most historians reject the idea that this was simply local violence inside a wider war. The remaining question is how high the decision went.

### CH 7 — THE PAPER TRAIL

The report we started with is a commander reporting a finished job up to his superiors. He had just destroyed two Polish villages. That is not a mob of angry people. It is an officer working inside a chain of command.

One of the UPA's internal directives from 1944 ends with a standing greeting: "Weapons ready. Death to Poles."

> [ON-SCREEN: Klymchak report, 30 Aug 1943, McBride p. 648 · *Nadzvychaine zariadzhennia*, 6 Apr 1944, KAW M/II/30/2.]

But if we follow these orders and reports back to the top, at a certain point the paper stops.

Bandera himself was still confined in Berlin and Sachsenhausen during the massacres. He had limited contact with the movement, but the surviving record does not show what he knew or whether he influenced the policy. The UPA commander in Volhynia was Dmytro Klyachkivsky, known as Klym Savur. Administrative and military orders bearing his signature survive. What we don't have is an order we can inspect, bearing his signature and ordering the killing of the Polish population.

> [ON-SCREEN: Rossoliński-Liebe p. 180 — Bandera confined outside Ukraine during the massacres · Order No. 11, 4 Sep 1943, shown whole. Never crop the word "liquidate" away from its German/Bolshevik object.]

The closest thing we have are two pieces of paper, and both come from the same man: a UPA commander named Yurii Stelmashchuk.

The first piece is a confession from 1945. After he was captured, Stelmashchuk told Soviet interrogators that Klyachkivsky had given him "the secret directive about the complete physical destruction of the Polish population."

That confession was recorded under duress and in the interrogators' wording, so it cannot be treated like a voluntary statement. Volodymyr Viatrovych, who later ran both Ukraine's security-service archive and its memory institute, argues that Soviet investigators fabricated or manipulated it to discredit the Ukrainian independence movement. He also points out that the protocol was missing from the criminal file where he expected to find it.

But a copy of that confession sits in Ukraine's own archive, filed there in 1963. Another published copy carries a Soviet general's handwritten notes dated April 1945. That makes it difficult to argue that the document itself was invented decades later. It does not prove that the confession was voluntary, or that every word came from Stelmashchuk.

The second piece of paper is older, and stronger — a letter from June 1943, written while the killing was underway. Stelmashchuk doubted that the order had come from Klyachkivsky, so he wrote to the OUN leadership to check. In that letter he repeated the same secret directive and said it had been given by word of mouth.

We do not know whether he received an answer, but he carried the order out. Some Polish historians describe this as Klym Savur's signed directive. Motyka is more precise. Stelmashchuk signed the letter, but the letter reports an oral order. It is not the order itself.

And there's one more problem. Nobody has been able to inspect the original file the letter is cited from. It was restricted again or lost around the collapse of the Soviet Union, so the public evidence is a transcription reproduced by historians. That is strong evidence of a reported oral order, but it is not Klyachkivsky's signature.

> [ON-SCREEN: Stelmashchuk protocol, 28 Feb 1945 · Gorshkov's 24 Apr 1945 notes · letter of 24 Jun 1943 as published · custody diagram converging on HDA SBU spr. 11315.]

### CH 8 — WHAT THE HISTORIANS CONCLUDE

The three labels do not fit the evidence equally well. The Polish reprisals and Operation Vistula were real, but they do not make this an equal war. The timing and the scale were different. The UPA hit villages on the same days, and its commanders reported the results up the chain. That is why the two-sided-war framing has the weakest scholarly support.

Jared McBride reconstructs the killings from local records and perpetrator biographies. He calls it ethnic cleansing because the OUN and UPA set out to remove the Polish population from a territory and organized mass violence to do it.

Ivan Katchanovski is a political scientist who has worked with the Ukrainian security-service archives. He rejects the genocide label, but still concludes that this was one-sided ethnic cleansing organized by the OUN-B, the UPA, and its security service.

Motyka goes further and calls it genocidal ethnic cleansing. The coordination and the scale convince him that the intent crossed the line into genocide, even without an authenticated order bearing Klyachkivsky's signature.

> [ON-SCREEN: historian positions — McBride, *Slavic Review* 75.3, pp. 631–633 · Snyder · Rossoliński-Liebe · Himka · Katchanovski 2020 conclusion · Motyka, *Od rzezi wołyńskiej do akcji “Wisła”*.]

So the scholarly disagreement is narrower than the political one. These historians agree that the OUN-B and UPA organized mass violence to remove the Polish population from Volhynia. They disagree about the toll, whether the aim was to remove the Poles or destroy them, and how close the surviving documents take us to the men at the top. The record establishes an organized campaign under Klyachkivsky, but not an authenticated order from him that would end the argument about his exact intent.

### CH 9 — WHY THE ARGUMENT SURVIVES

The disagreement did not end with the historians. It stopped Poland from exhuming and identifying the victims. Ukraine blocked Polish exhumations in 2017. The ban was lifted in 2025, and the first dig in a decade had only just resumed when the 2026 decree reopened the dispute. It's not that Ukraine has offered Poland nothing. The two countries signed a reconciliation declaration in 1997, and in 2023 the two presidents mourned the victims together. But those ceremonies never settled Poland's demand to dig up the graves and name the dead.

The dispute also shapes what can be said inside Ukraine. In 2012, threats cancelled most of a lecture tour by the historian who wrote the leading biography of Bandera. Write about these killings, and you can still get labelled a traitor, or a Russian agent.

And the unresolved history hands Russia a weapon. Moscow takes this very real crime and uses it to claim that Ukraine itself is a Nazi state. That is one of the claims Russia uses to justify its invasion. But nothing in these documents proves it about modern Ukraine.

The Soviet Union held the central files for fifty years and called these men fascist butchers. Russia still controls those files. Yet it has never produced the one piece of paper that would prove the order to cleanse Volhynia came from the top.

Poland continues to arm Ukraine because its own security depends on an independent Ukraine. So it condemns the UPA while helping modern Ukraine survive.

### CLOSE

So Ukraine remembers the part that glorifies these men, and buries the part that doesn't. The same state that calls Russia's atrocities at Bucha what they are is commemorating men who did similar things to the Poles, eighty years ago.

This is not unique to Ukraine. France still celebrates Napoleon — the man who brought slavery back after the Revolution had abolished it. In my own Belgium, there are still statues and squares honoring Leopold the Second — after everything that happened in the Congo.

In my opinion, there's nothing wrong with looking to the past for heroes when you're in a fight now. What's wrong is burying what they did. We should try to look at these people in their historical context, without justifying everything that they did.

And that's the whole fight. Can you glorify people for one thing they stood for, when they also did other terrible things?
