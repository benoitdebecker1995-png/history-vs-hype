## WHO THE UPA WERE

UPA stands for the Ukrainian Insurgent Army. It became the armed force of the Bandera faction of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists, or OUN-B. The political goal came first.

The OUN was founded in 1929 to create an independent Ukrainian state. To understand why some members later saw the Polish population as an obstacle, we have to go back to the borders created after the First World War.

Volhynia became part of Poland under the 1921 Treaty of Riga, which ended the Polish-Soviet War. Eastern Galicia followed a different route. Poland had defeated the West Ukrainian republic there, and the Allied powers recognised Polish rule in 1923.

When those borders were settled, millions of Ukrainians lived inside Poland. Polish rule allowed elections and legal parties, but it also brought broken promises of autonomy, restrictions and repression. Most Ukrainians did not join the OUN, and none of this made the killing inevitable. But it helped the OUN argue that Ukraine could lose these lands because Poles lived there.

By the late 1930s, one of the OUN's military thinkers, Mykhailo Kolodzinskyi, had put that fear into writing. In a future uprising, he wanted the Polish population removed from western Ukrainian territory so Poland could not use it to claim the land after another war.

This gives us a motive, but not an explanation for the massacre. An idea is not an order, and the movement still needed an army, weapons and trained men to attack communities across Volhynia.

## FROM POLITICAL MOVEMENT TO ARMED FORCE

The OUN split in 1940, partly over control and partly between generations. The older leadership in exile backed Andrii Melnyk. Younger underground activists followed Stepan Bandera, whose faction became OUN-B.

The OUN-B's connection with Nazi Germany was real. After Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, Bandera's deputy Yaroslav Stetsko proclaimed an independent Ukraine in Lviv and promised close cooperation with Hitler's Germany. The OUN-B saw Germany as a route to independence, and the movements also had ideological similarities.

But Germany rejected Ukrainian independence. Bandera and Stetsko were detained within days and formally arrested that September. The OUN-B also refused a German demand to withdraw the proclamation. So the cooperation was real, but the OUN-B was not simply carrying out German policy. Its underground continued without them.

The political aim still required military power. In spring 1943, about five thousand Ukrainian auxiliary policemen deserted German service. The OUN-B wanted them before Soviet partisans or rival groups did. Some joined willingly; others were forced or had nowhere else to go. They brought weapons, training and experience.

That explains the UPA's capacity to attack Polish communities, but it does not prove a policy of killing civilians. For that, we need to look at what it did.

## PORYCK AND THE WIDER CAMPAIGN

On Sunday, July 11th, 1943, UPA units attacked nearly a hundred Polish localities in Volhynia. Some of those attacks struck churches where people were attending Sunday Mass.

One of those places was Poryck. At eleven in the morning, residents were gathered in the local church. Historian Grzegorz Motyka reconstructs how UPA units surrounded the building, fired through the doors and windows and threw grenades inside.

On the same page, Motyka reproduces survivor Jadwiga Krajewska's testimony. She remembered the first shots being fired during the Gloria, part of the Mass.

Poryck was one atrocity within the larger attack on nearly a hundred Polish localities that day.

One scholarly estimate puts the death toll from that Sunday at around four thousand Poles. It was the peak of a wider OUN-B and UPA campaign that killed about fifty to sixty thousand Polish civilians in Volhynia.

Polish researchers Władysław and Ewa Siemaszko also documented more than thirty-six thousand victims by time and place in a village-by-village registry. Many are identified by name.

The scale and timing make unrelated village attacks very unlikely. They show coordination, but not who authorised the campaign. For that, we have to look at the UPA command.

## WHAT THE EVIDENCE PROVES

We now have the removal idea, military capacity and coordinated attacks. What we do not have is the order connecting them to a named commander.

The UPA's regional commander in Volhynia was Dmytro Klyachkivsky, or Klym Savur. Order No. 11 carries that name and deals with defence, mobilisation and logistics. It says nothing about killing Polish civilians, but it does show a regional command issuing written orders.

The next piece comes from Yurii Stelmashchuk, a UPA commander who served under Klyachkivsky. During a Soviet interrogation in 1945, he said that Klyachkivsky had given him a secret oral directive for the physical destruction of the Polish population in western Ukraine.

A joint Polish-Ukrainian edition links the statement to a Soviet security file but labels it “copy, typescript.” Stelmashchuk gave it in Soviet custody before his execution. It connects the campaign to the regional commander, but I cannot treat it as a voluntary confession or proof of an original order's exact words.

This is where the paper trail stops. There is no authenticated extermination order carrying Klyachkivsky's signature in the accessible record. But the doctrine, attack pattern, command structure and testimony explain why historians describe the campaign as organised. They do not reconstruct the exact command chain or wording of a missing order.

That limit matters because the killing and organisation are not seriously in doubt. The argument is about the intention and the label it supports.

## POLISH VIOLENCE AND THE QUESTION OF CLASSIFICATION

Before deciding what genocide adds, we have to acknowledge Polish violence. Local self-defence units protected settlements, while Polish underground and partisan units also attacked Ukrainian villages and killed civilians, especially in 1944.

But I do not think the evidence supports treating the two sides as equal. The OUN-B and UPA began the mass campaign, wanted to remove the Polish population and killed far more civilians. Polish violence does not make this a spontaneous conflict between equal sides.

Nor was it simply Poles against Ukrainians as whole peoples. Some Ukrainians protected Polish neighbours and were killed for doing so. One was Petro Muzyka, a Ukrainian teacher and UPA member whom the UPA executed after he refused to denounce a Polish woman he had sheltered.

That is the context for the argument over labels. Ethnic cleansing means removing a population from a territory through force or intimidation. The Genocide Convention requires an intention to destroy, “in whole or in part,” a national, ethnical, racial or religious group.

Historians mostly agree that the OUN-B and UPA organised a campaign to remove Polish civilians. The disagreement is about what to call it. Most English-language historians use ethnic cleansing. Grzegorz Motyka calls it “genocidal ethnic cleansing.” Other historians call it genocide, while some reject that label.

Poland officially classifies Volhynia as genocide. The Ukrainian Institute of National Memory acknowledges mass killings of Polish civilians but frames them within a two-sided conflict and rejects Poland's one-sided genocide classification. These are official positions. They are not the same thing as an academic consensus.

So the disputed label does not make the campaign uncertain. Poland is reacting to Ukraine honouring the organisation that carried it out. That answers one half of the opening question. The other half is why Ukraine honours it.

## WHY UKRAINE HONOURS THEM NOW

The answer begins with another part of the UPA's history. It fought Soviet rule, and after independence in 1991, Ukraine could openly commemorate that resistance. The memory was strongest in western Ukraine and contested elsewhere.

After Russia annexed Crimea and war began in Donbas in 2014, resistance to Moscow became more useful. Decommunisation also removed Soviet names and monuments, creating room for replacement heroes. The UPA offered a ready story of Ukrainians fighting Moscow for an independent state.

In 2015, Ukraine recognised OUN and UPA members in law as fighters for independence. The 2026 decree extends that policy. This explains the honour, but not how every Ukrainian views it.

This does not mean Ukraine denies that Polish civilians died. In 1997, the Polish and Ukrainian presidents declared that their blood could not be forgotten. But acknowledging victims is different from accepting responsibility for the organisation that killed them. That is where the conflict remains.

One example of that selective memory appeared at a Bandera museum near Lviv. It displayed the 1941 proclamation without the sentence promising close cooperation with Hitler's Germany, even though that sentence is present in the original.

At state level, the dispute also affected the dead. Ukraine halted Polish exhumation work in 2017. After a new bilateral agreement in November 2024, work resumed in 2025. At Puźniki, a separate 1945 UPA massacre site, investigators recovered the remains of at least forty-two victims.

So Ukraine honours the UPA for fighting foreign rule, not for massacring Polish civilians. But the honour asks Poland to separate those parts of its history, and Poland refuses. The same commemoration can therefore represent resistance in Ukrainian state memory and an insult in Polish state memory.

This matters because Russia uses “Banderite” and this real crime to call modern Ukraine a Nazi state. The evidence does not support that conclusion, and Volhynia does not justify Russia's invasion. Poland can condemn the UPA and still support Ukraine because an independent Ukraine is part of Poland's security strategy.

## CLOSE

And of course, this is not unique to Ukraine.

In my own Belgium, we still have statues and public places named after King Leopold II, despite what happened in the Congo. I am not saying these cases are identical. I am saying that countries often put the useful part of their past at the centre and treat the rest as a separate discussion.

In my opinion, there is nothing wrong with looking to the past for heroes, especially when your country is fighting for its survival. But once a state honours an organisation, it also takes responsibility for telling its whole history. It cannot make the resistance official and treat the massacre as a separate problem for someone else.

That is the disagreement here. Ukrainian state commemoration honours the UPA as resistance against foreign rule. Poland points to the same organisation's campaign against Polish civilians. The first fact explains why the UPA is useful to Ukraine now. It does not answer the second one.
