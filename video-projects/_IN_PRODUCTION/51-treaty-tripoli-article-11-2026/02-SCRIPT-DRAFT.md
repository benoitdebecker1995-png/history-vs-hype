### [BEAT 1 — Cold open, 0:00–0:20]

**[On-screen: close-up of period-typography Article 11, slow zoom. Cut to cover of 1931 Hunter Miller volume.]**

In 1930, a Dutch scholar named Snouck Hurgronje examined the Arabic original of an American treaty.

The next year, the United States Government Printing Office published what he found.

What he found, the State Department couldn't explain. And almost a hundred years later, neither can anyone else.

### [BEAT 2 — The treaty + Arabic Chekhov's gun, 0:20–1:10]

**[On-screen: map of Barbary coast → engraving of Adams → Senate Journal page 244 → Arabic manuscript page (3-second flash with caption).]**

American merchant ships were being seized. Their crews were being held for ransom. The United States needed a treaty.

That document was the 1796 Treaty of Tripoli. It was signed at Tripoli on November 4th, 1796, then certified at Algiers in January 1797 by Joel Barlow, the United States Consul to Algiers.

President Adams transmitted the document to the Senate.

On Wednesday, June 7th, 1797, the Senate voted on it. Adams signed the ratification three days later.

**[Arabic manuscript flashes on screen — caption: "The Arabic original."]**

The Arabic original of that same treaty was held, for over a century, in the State Department file. It looks like this. We'll come back to it.

---

### [BEAT 3 — Article 11 read, 1:10–1:30]

**[On-screen: Article 11 fills the screen. Highlight key clause as it's read.]**

Here is Article 11 of what the Senate ratified.

> *"As the government of the United States of America is not in any sense founded on the Christian Religion, — as it has in itself no character of enmity against the laws, religion or tranquility of Musselmen, — and as the said States never have entered into any war or act of hostility against any Mehomitan nation, it is declared by the parties that no pretext arising from religious opinions shall ever produce an interruption of the harmony existing between the two countries."*

The Senate voted yes. Twenty-three senators. None against. The Senate Journal records it on page 244.

### [BEAT 4 — The Arabic reveal, 1:30–2:30]

**[On-screen: Arabic manuscript fills the screen. Highlight where Article 11 should appear. Cut to Hunter Miller volume cover, then page 371.]**

But here is what Hurgronje found.

The Arabic version of the treaty does not contain Article 11.

Not mistranslated. Not paraphrased. Missing.

In 1930, the United States State Department hired a Dutch scholar — Doctor Snouck Hurgronje, of the University of Leiden — to examine the Arabic original. He produced an annotated translation. The U.S. Government Printing Office published it the next year, in volume two of a multi-volume work edited by the State Department historian Hunter Miller.

And on page 371, Hurgronje wrote this.

**[Text overlay, verbatim:]**
> *"The eleventh article of the Barlow translation has no equivalent whatever in the Arabic. The Arabic text opposite that article is a letter from Hassan Pasha of Algiers to Yussuf Pasha of Tripoli."*

It is, in other words, the wrong document.

And it gets worse. Hurgronje read the letter itself. It wasn't a secret Islamic manifesto. It was a clerical mess. Hurgronje wrote that the document was *"drawn up by a stupid secretary who just knew a certain number of bombastic words... but entirely failed to catch their real meaning."*

A nonsense letter, bound in place of the most-quoted article in American treaty history.

### [BEAT 5 — Forensic interpretation + Cobbett + Dwight, 2:30–4:10]

**[On-screen: split layout — Senate ratification on one side, Arabic letter on the other. Cut to Joel Barlow portrait → portrait fades to dark frame.]**

So if the Arabic is a different document entirely, what exactly did the Senate ratify?

They ratified the English text. Hunter Miller, in 1931, called it *"that English text which in the United States has always been deemed the text of the treaty."* The Christian-religion language is real, ratified, and carries the legal force of treaty. No serious historian disputes that.

And Joel Barlow himself probably never knew.

As historian Denise Spellberg notes: neither Barlow nor any of his American colleagues could read Arabic. They relied on the consul of Spain to verify the Arabic seals. Barlow attested to the accuracy of the document, but, in Spellberg's phrasing, *"he probably never knew that Article 11 never existed in an Arabic translation."*

This isn't a forgery. It's a translation gap that nobody in 1797 was equipped to catch.

Which is why, in 1797, almost nobody said a word.

Adams said nothing publicly. Jefferson — then Vice President — said nothing publicly. The Senate Journal records no debate on Article 11. None.

Historians point to two preserved reactions.

The first came from William Cobbett, a Federalist editor in Philadelphia, in the *Porcupine Gazette* on June 23rd, 1797. Cobbett wrote that the article *"certainly looks a little like trampling upon the cross."*

The second came from Timothy Dwight, the president of Yale and Joel Barlow's former mentor. Per Sam Haselby, on page 109 of his 2015 Oxford monograph: Article 11 was the breaking point. Dwight rejected Barlow's radicalism, translated his own replacement Psalms, and took Barlow's portrait down at Yale.

That is, essentially, the entirety of it. One newspaper editorial. And a conservative Yale president taking down Joel Barlow's portrait in disgust.

Today, this passage sits at the center of the argument about whether America was founded as a Christian nation. That is not the argument this video is making.

### [BEAT 6 — Closing, 4:10–5:45]

**[On-screen: 1931 volume cover → page 384 of Miller's notes → text overlay of Crane quote.]**

The Arabic original does not contain Article 11.

But the strangest part of this discrepancy isn't the missing text.

It's what happened in the 133 years before anyone noticed.

Hurgronje read both languages. He saw the gap. Hunter Miller, on page 384 of his 1931 volume, called it *"most extraordinary, and wholly unexplained."* No scholar since has explained it.

But in those 133 years — before Hurgronje opened the file — something was already happening.

In 2020, the historian Jacob Crane published a study in *American Quarterly*. He argued that the modern reading of Article 11 — the reading that treats it as a definitive statement on American secularism — *developed*. Slowly. Across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. As the treaty was applied to fights about funding Christian clergy and Sunday alcohol laws.

Crane writes, on page 405:

**[Text overlay: `assets/quote-card-5-crane-405.html`]**
> *"the language of Article 11 was wielded as often by Christian nationalists as by militant secularists."*

Today this English text is wielded as the ultimate weapon in a modern culture war.
