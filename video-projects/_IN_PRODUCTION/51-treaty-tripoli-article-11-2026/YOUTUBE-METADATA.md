## Description (DRAFT — thesis-anchored, updated 2026-04-27)

```
In 1797, the U.S. Senate ratified a treaty article saying the United
States is "not in any sense founded on the Christian Religion." For
133 years, almost nobody mentioned it.

The Arabic original of that same treaty does not contain Article 11.
Not mistranslated. Not paraphrased. Missing.

In 1930, the State Department hired a Dutch scholar — Snouck Hurgronje
of the University of Leiden — to examine the Arabic file. He found
that the Arabic page where Article 11 should be is, instead, an
unrelated letter from Hassan Pasha of Algiers to Yussuf Pasha of
Tripoli — drawn up, in Hurgronje's words, by "a stupid secretary who
just knew a certain number of bombastic words."

The next year, the U.S. Government Printing Office published Hurgronje's
findings in volume two of Hunter Miller's Treaties and Other International
Acts of the United States of America (1931). Miller's note on page 384
called the discrepancy "most extraordinary, and wholly unexplained."

In nearly a century, no scholar has explained it.

This video reads the document. It does not argue politics.

⏱️ CHAPTERS
0:00  The 1931 publication nobody reads
0:20  The treaty, the Senate, Article 11
1:37  What Hurgronje found in the Arabic
4:55  133 years before anyone noticed

📚 PRIMARY SOURCES
• Hunter Miller (ed.), Treaties and Other International Acts of the
  United States of America, Vol. 2 — U.S. Government Printing Office,
  Washington, 1931 (Tripoli passage pp. 349–384, including Hurgronje's
  1930 annotated translation pp. 365–381)
• U.S. Senate Executive Journal, page 244 (June 7, 1797 ratification
  vote on the Treaty of Tripoli)

  Secularism," American Quarterly 72:2 (2020), p. 405

🏛️ CONTEXT
The Treaty of Tripoli was the first U.S. peace treaty with an Islamic
state, ratified during the Quasi-War with France and the early Barbary
piracy crisis. Article 11 has been cited in U.S. Supreme Court

History vs Hype investigates the stories we're told vs the documents
```
