# The Map They Drew Was Never For Africa

*Bismarck wasn't dividing a continent. He was managing his neighbours.*

---

In the autumn of 1884, the German Chancellor Otto von Bismarck sketched out his Africa strategy for a visiting explorer. He drew an imaginary map in the air with his hands.

> "Here is Russia and here is France, with Germany in the middle. That is my map of Africa."
>
> — Bismarck, in Pakenham, *The Scramble for Africa*, p. 291

Three months later, fourteen countries signed a binding international treaty in his living room that effectively assigned a continent. Not one African was in the room. Not one African government was consulted. The borders they drew with rivers and rulers and lines of latitude split 229 ethnic groups across what would become more than fifty modern states.

**Why it matters:** Most readers assume the Berlin Conference was a meeting about Africa. The documents show it was a meeting about Europe, held over a map of Africa. That distinction is the difference between an unfortunate decision and a structural choice that is still measurable in displacement camps in 2026.

---

## The Room Where Africa Wasn't

Fourteen countries. Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Portugal, Russia, the Ottoman Empire, the United States, and six others. The American delegation showed up because Bismarck thought it would be useful. America had no significant African territory. They wanted a seat at the table anyway.

The British representative, Sir Edward Malet, said this on the record during the proceedings:

> "I cannot forget that the natives are not represented amongst us, and that the decisions of the Conference will, nevertheless, have an extreme importance for them."
>
> — Sir Edward Malet, conference proceedings, in E.D. Morel, *King Leopold's Rule in Africa*

He said that, and then they continued the meeting. No African ruler. No African government. No African representative. Not one.

The conference produced a treaty called the General Act of Berlin, signed February 26th, 1885. It is a binding international agreement, still cited in international law journals. The legal scholar Antony Anghie put the partition logic on the page: the European powers shaped the rules to fit their own competition, not the rights of the people whose homelands were being assigned.

> "Any restrictions on the actions of European states towards non-European states resulted from conflicts between European states regarding the same territory, not from the rights of the non-European states. This was evident in the partition of Africa, which was determined in accordance with the needs of the major European states."
>
> — Antony Anghie, *Imperialism, Sovereignty and the Making of International Law*

The Cambridge historian Henk Wesseling put the substance of it more bluntly:

> "In Europe conquests preceded the drawing of maps; in Africa the map was drawn, and then it was decided what was going to happen."
>
> — H.L. Wesseling, *Divide and Rule*, p. 362, quoted in Michalopoulos & Papaioannou (2016)

Read that twice. The map came first. The decision about what the map meant came later.

## "All This Colonial Business is a Sham"

The conference had a humanitarian cover story. Bismarck's opening speech announced "the wish to bring the natives of Africa within the pale of civilisation by opening up the interior of that continent to commerce." Pakenham reproduces the protocol. It reads like a charity brochure.

That was the public version. In private, to a domestic political ally, Bismarck said this:

> "All this colonial business is a sham [Schwindel], but we need it for elections."
>
> — Bismarck, in Pakenham, *The Scramble for Africa*, p. 203

I sat with that quote for a while. The German Chancellor, the most powerful man in continental Europe, telling a colleague in the same year as the conference that the entire colonial project was theatre. Domestic theatre. Africa was not the audience, Africa was the prop.

That logic — use Africa to settle European rivalries — wrote the rules. Article 35 of the General Act introduced what historians call the doctrine of "effective occupation." If you wanted territory, you had to prove you actually controlled it. Technically the article only applied to the coasts. In practice it created what Förster's edited volume calls a "hegemony imperative." You couldn't just claim a region on a map. You had to plant a flag, build a post, send soldiers, draft a treaty with whoever was nearest. And if you didn't move, your rival would.

The Scramble for Africa already existed. Berlin didn't start it. What Berlin did was turn a scramble into a race with rules, signatures, and a binding treaty. That distinction is what I want you to keep in mind when someone tells you the conference didn't matter.

## The Map I Found

I want to be honest about the moment that changed how I read this story. I was looking for primary sources, and I came across the Murdock map.

George Peter Murdock's *Ethnolinguistic Map of Africa*, published in 1959, is a colour-coded mosaic of the continent's pre-colonial political and ethnic geography. The U.S. Library of Congress holds the CIA's 1996 reissue. It shows Africa before European pens touched it. Hundreds of polities. Kingdoms, caliphates, confederacies, lineage groups, trading cities, plus the territories of pastoralist and forager societies that Western legal categories never knew how to describe.

The popular version of pre-colonial Africa is empty space. A blank slate. Statelessness waiting to be administered. The Ghanaian historian A. Adu Boahen, who spent his career editing the UNESCO General History of Africa, described Africa in 1880 like this:

> "Africa was far from being primitive, static, and asleep or in a Hobbesian state of nature. On all fronts — economic, political, social, and even intellectual — Africa was in a mood of change and revolution..."
>
> — A. Adu Boahen, *African Perspectives on Colonialism*

The Sokoto Caliphate, in what is now northern Nigeria, was the largest state in West Africa. The Kingdom of Benin had ten thousand kilometres of earthen boundaries — a defensive network larger than the Great Wall of China. Great Zimbabwe had been a powerful trading kingdom with global reach. These were not blank spaces. They were states.

When European delegates and lawyers described Africa as empty, they weren't observing reality. They were constructing a legal fiction called *terra nullius* — "nobody's land" — to claim territory that millions of people already lived on. The fiction served the lawyers. The fiction is still doing work. Every time a headline treats African borders as if they had simply always been there, that fiction is still earning interest.

## 229 Groups, Cut

The borders the Europeans drew didn't follow the Murdock map. They followed rivers and parallels and the negotiating positions of European foreign ministries. In 2016, the economists Stelios Michalopoulos at Brown and Elias Papaioannou at London Business School published a study in the *American Economic Review* that put numbers on what that ignored map cost.

They overlaid Murdock's pre-colonial ethnic homelands onto modern national borders. Then they counted what was cut.

**By the numbers:**

- 229 ethnicities partitioned across at least two modern countries (27.7 percent of the sample)
- Approximately 40 percent higher conflict intensity inside partitioned homelands
- 57 percent increase in political violence in partitioned ethnic homelands
- Half a year less of schooling, on average, for individuals from partitioned ethnicities

That last one is the one that sticks. A child born today in a community that the Berlin generation cut in half receives, on average, half a year less schooling than a child born to an unpartitioned neighbour. The treaty was 141 years ago. The schooling gap was measurable in 2016.

Take the Somali people. One ethnic and linguistic group. Not a "tribe" in the colonial sense — a literate, Islamic, pastoralist nation that historians have been writing about since the medieval Arab geographers. The Berlin generation, working through bilateral treaties between 1888 and 1908, cut the Somali nation into five pieces. Italian Somaliland. British Somaliland. Ethiopia's Ogaden. Kenya's Northern Frontier District. French Djibouti.

The five-pointed star on the modern Somali flag represents those five pieces. It was designed as a programme. Reunify what the Europeans cut.

That programme caused three wars. The 1977 Ogaden War was fought directly over a boundary line drawn in 1897 between the British and Emperor Menelik. Historians call the ideology *irredentism* — the drive to recover territory inhabited by your people but governed by someone else. The Somali case isn't unique. The Ewe were split across Ghana, Togo, and Benin. The Bakongo, once a kingdom, are spread across four modern states. The Afar across Ethiopia, Eritrea, and Djibouti. Communities older than the conference, fractured by men who had never been there.

Every fracture is still producing conflict.

## One Man, A Country

The conference's most personal verdict was Leopold the Second of Belgium. The Belgian king, having failed for years to convince his own government to fund a colonial venture, lobbied the Berlin signatories directly and walked out with the Congo Free State. Two and a half million square kilometres. Roughly the size of France, Germany, and Spain combined. Not Belgium's colony — *his*. Personal property of the king.

Leopold's two voices are easy to line up. In a public speech to the 1876 Brussels Geographical Conference, he opened with this:

> "Need I say that in bringing you to Brussels I was guided by no egotism? No, gentlemen, Belgium may be a small country, but she is happy and satisfied with her fate; I have no other ambition than to serve her well."
>
> — Leopold II, Brussels Geographical Conference, 1876, in Hochschild, *King Leopold's Ghost*, p. 43

A year later, in a private letter to his ambassador in London, the same king described what he was actually after as "this magnificent African cake." Hochschild quotes the phrase directly from Leopold's letter to Solvyns, 17 November 1877.

Eight years after Berlin, the Free State was operating. By the early 1900s, international evidence had reached Brussels: forced rubber extraction, severed hands, a population reduced by at least half. In August 1908, the Belgian state finally forced him to surrender his private kingdom to public administration. Hochschild reconstructs the scene: a young military aide named Stinglhamber walked into the state offices next door to the Royal Palace and burned himself sitting on a radiator that should have been cool in midsummer. The janitor explained: they were burning the State archives. The furnaces ran for eight days. To Stinglhamber, the king said this:

> "I will give them my Congo, but they have no right to know what I did there."
>
> — Leopold II, August 1908, to Gustave Stinglhamber, in Hochschild, *King Leopold's Ghost*

What Leopold did inside the Congo Free State is its own story. I have a longer piece coming on the actual French-language decrees that authorised forced rubber extraction. What matters here is the structural fact: the Berlin signatories handed a private European individual a stretch of central Africa larger than Western Europe, on the basis of effective occupation he had not yet effected, with no representation from any of the millions of people whose homelands lay inside that line.

That was the system working as designed.

## The Lock-In

Here is the part of the story that I find hardest to write.

The Berlin Conference ended in February 1885. By 1914, ninety percent of the African continent was under European control. Ten million square miles, conquered in a single generation. Then came two world wars, the post-war collapse of European empire, and a wave of African independence between roughly 1957 and 1975.

When African leaders inherited their states, they inherited Berlin's borders. Borders that cut their nations in half. Borders that locked rivals into the same state. Borders that no one in living memory had agreed to.

They kept them.

The first president of Tanzania, Julius Nyerere, explained why in a sentence I still cannot quite reconcile:

> "Precisely because African boundaries were so absurd, it was essential to maintain them."
>
> — Julius Nyerere, in Crawford Young, *The Postcolonial State in Africa: Fifty Years of Independence, 1960–2010*

In 1964, the Organisation of African Unity put it in writing in a Cairo resolution. Existing colonial borders would stand. Not because they were just. Because the alternative was worse.

Think about the choice they were making. Redrawing every border along ethnic lines would mean every group on the continent has a claim. The Somalis would want their five pieces back. The Ewe would want to leave Ghana. The Bakongo would want to leave four states at once. Northern Sudan and southern Sudan, Hutu and Tutsi, Yoruba and Hausa, every artificial neighbour the Berlin generation had stapled together would have a legal opening to walk out. The continent would not heal. It would dissolve into what political scientists call irredentist war — the kind of war the Somalis were already fighting.

So Nyerere's generation chose. They kept the borders Europeans had drawn for European reasons in a European city in 1884. They kept them because the men who drew them had locked the continent into a system where dismantling the cage was more dangerous than living in it.

That is the part of the story that the conference centenary essays usually skip. The borders weren't just imposed. They were inherited and ratified by the people the original generation had excluded. Not because the heirs were collaborators. Because the architecture made it impossible to choose otherwise.

I cannot tell you the OAU was wrong. I cannot tell you what would have happened in 1964 if Nyerere's generation had said no. What I can tell you is what the documents show: a European decision in 1884 narrowed African options in 1964 so completely that the moral choice and the practical choice ran in opposite directions.

## The Borders Are Still Charging Interest

A hundred and forty-one years after the General Act was signed, the borders haven't moved.

In Sudan, the civil war that has displaced twelve million people — the largest displacement crisis on earth — is being fought along ethnic categories that British colonial administration imposed in the 1920s onto a region that had previously organised itself differently. Mahmood Mamdani at Columbia traces the modern Janjaweed-versus-non-Arab framing back to that colonial intellectual work. As Mamdani has put it, "the categories that drove the census were drafted in the period that began in the 1920s, and MacMichael was key to this intellectual work." Indirect rule, in his account, "translated tribe from a more or less benign cultural identity to a systematically malignant form of political discrimination."

In January 2025, Rwanda-backed rebels captured Goma, a city of one million people on the Congo-Rwanda border. The Belgian colonial administration drew that border through territory that pre-colonial Rwandan kingdoms had historically controlled, and moved Rwandan-speaking populations across it. Rwanda's president has, more than once, publicly questioned the border. Crawford Young's work on the postcolonial state documents the administrative choices that produced the modern population mix. Those are Berlin Conference borders. They are still being fought over.

If you've watched my piece on the Treaty of Tordesillas, the structure should look familiar. A continent. A line drawn by people who cannot see it. A measurable inequality that outlives the empire that drew the line. Christopher Rossi at Cornell calls this pattern the *Tordesillas Syndrome* — when superpowers face a vast space they cannot share, they draw a line and walk away. The line then does the work for centuries.

To be fair to the original signatories, the conference did not invent the Scramble for Africa. Wesseling and Förster both argue persuasively that bilateral treaties between European powers, signed before and after Berlin, did most of the actual partitioning. Emperor Menelik II of Ethiopia famously negotiated his way through the system and kept his kingdom. The conference is sometimes called a "safety valve" — a way to prevent European wars over African territory rather than a plan to carve a continent. That argument has weight. I want to give it weight.

What it does not change is the structural verdict. The room had no Africans in it. The treaty operated on a fiction of empty land. The borders did not follow the people who lived under them. The system worked exactly as designed for the people who designed it. Africa was not the point. Africa was the chessboard.

---

## What Do You Do With a Cage You Inherit?

I keep coming back to the photograph of the conference room. There is a famous engraving by Adalbert von Rößler, made in 1885, of the delegates around a long table with a giant map of Africa pinned to the wall behind them. Bismarck stands at the head of the table. The map is a cartoon. None of them have been to most of the places on it.

In that engraving, Africa is wallpaper. The argument is between the men in the chairs.

A hundred and forty-one years later, the wallpaper has become walls. Twelve million displaced in Sudan. A million civilians fleeing Goma. Five Somali pieces that have not closed. A poverty gap measurable in schooling years, decades after the last colonial administrator went home. The men in the chairs are gone. The map they drew for each other has become the only map there is.

The hardest question in this story isn't who is to blame. The men in the engraving knew exactly what they were doing, and most of them said so in private letters. The hard question is the one the Nyerere generation answered in 1964, and the one every African government has been answering since: what do you do with a cage that was built to hurt you, when the alternative is dismantling it on top of yourself?

That is not a question I can answer for them. It is not a question Bismarck ever had to ask.

The map he drew in the air is still on every wall.
