## ACT 1: THE CONFERENCE (0:50 – 3:30)

### The Room

`[TALKING HEAD]`

On November 15th, 1884, representatives from fourteen countries gathered at Bismarck's residence in Berlin. The major colonial powers — Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Portugal — plus Russia, the Ottoman Empire, and the United States, which had no significant African territory but wanted a seat at the table.

`[B-ROLL: Period illustration of the conference room]`

The British representative, Sir Edward Malet, said this during the proceedings:

`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "I cannot forget that the natives are not represented amongst us, and that the decisions of the Conference will, nevertheless, have an extreme importance for them." — Sir Edward Malet]`

"I cannot forget that the natives are not represented amongst us, and that the decisions of the Conference will, nevertheless, have an extreme importance for them."

`[TALKING HEAD]`

He said that. And then they continued without inviting anyone. No African ruler, government, or representative was present. Not one.

### Bismarck's Game

`[TALKING HEAD]`

So why did Bismarck call this conference? His opening speech framed it as humanitarian — "the wish to bring the natives of Africa within the pale of civilisation by opening up the interior of that continent to commerce."

That was the public version.

`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "All this colonial business is a sham, but we need it for elections." — Bismarck (Pakenham, p. 203)]`

In private: "All this colonial business is a sham, but we need it for elections."

And when a German explorer asked about his Africa strategy:

`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "Here is Russia and here is France, with Germany in the middle. That is my map of Africa." — Bismarck (Pakenham, p. 291)]`

"Here is Russia and here is France, with Germany in the middle. That is my map of Africa."

Africa wasn't the point. Africa was the chessboard. That logic — use Africa to settle European rivalries — set the template for every border they drew. Not for Africa. For Europe.

### The Rules

`[TALKING HEAD]`

The conference produced the General Act of Berlin — a binding international treaty — signed February 26th, 1885. But the clause that changed everything was Article 35 — the doctrine of "effective occupation."

`[DOCUMENT: Article 35 text on screen]`

It required any power claiming territory on the African coast to establish actual authority there. Technically, this only applied to the coasts. But in practice, it created a hegemony imperative — meaning you couldn't just claim territory on a map anymore. You had to prove you controlled it. And if you didn't move fast, someone else would claim it first.

The result was pre-emptive colonisation. A race to plant flags before rivals could.

If we're being fair, the conference didn't start the Scramble for Africa. That was already underway. But it wrote the rules that turned a scramble into a race. As the historian Wesseling put it: "In Europe, conquests preceded the drawing of maps. In Africa, the map was drawn, and then it was decided what was going to happen."

## ACT 2: THE MAP THEY IGNORED (3:30 – 6:30)

### What Africa Actually Looked Like

`[TALKING HEAD]`

The popular version treats pre-colonial Africa as a blank slate. Empty space that Europeans divided among themselves.

That's not what the academic sources say.

`[MAP: Pre-colonial African political entities — kingdoms, empires, confederacies]`

On the eve of the Berlin Conference, most African states were sovereign, governed by recognised rulers. The Sokoto Caliphate was the largest state in West Africa. The Kingdom of Benin had ten thousand kilometres of earth boundaries. Great Zimbabwe had been a powerful trading kingdom.

`[TALKING HEAD]`

The historian A. Adu Boahen describes Africa in 1880 as "far from being primitive, static, and asleep" — the continent was in a "mood of change and revolution."

When Europeans described Africa as empty or stateless, they weren't describing reality. They were constructing a legal fiction — terra nullius, nobody's land — to claim territory that millions of people already lived on. That fiction is still doing work. Every time a headline treats African borders like they've always existed — that's the fiction.

### 229 Groups Split

`[TALKING HEAD]`

The borders they drew didn't follow existing political or ethnic boundaries. They followed rivers, latitude lines, and European negotiating positions.

In 2016, economists Michalopoulos and Papaioannou published a study in the American Economic Review. They put numbers on it. They overlaid a pre-colonial map of ethnic homelands onto modern national borders.

`[SPLIT-SCREEN: Murdock map LEFT, modern borders overlaid RIGHT — partitioned groups highlighted]`

229 ethnic groups partitioned across at least two countries. Twenty-eight percent of all groups in their sample.

Conflict intensity in those homelands is forty percent higher. Political violence is fifty-seven percent higher. Split groups have less wealth, more discrimination, and half a year less schooling.

`[TEXT ON SCREEN: 229 groups split | 57% more political violence | 40% more conflict intensity — Michalopoulos & Papaioannou, AER 2016]`

### The Somali Case

`[MAP: Horn of Africa — Somali-speaking territory highlighted across multiple states]`

Take the Somali people. One ethnic and linguistic group, divided across four countries: Somalia, Ethiopia's Ogaden region, Kenya's Northern Frontier District, and Djibouti.

`[B-ROLL: Somali flag — five-pointed star]`

The five-pointed star on the Somali flag represents five partitioned regions. Each point is a piece of Somali territory placed under a different colonial power through bilateral treaties between 1888 and 1908.

`[TALKING HEAD]`

That partition caused three wars. All driven by the same thing — the desire to reunify separated people. Historians call it irredentism. The 1977 Ogaden War was fought directly over a boundary drawn in 1897.

And the Somali case isn't unique. The Ewe were split across Ghana, Togo, and Benin. The Bakongo — once a kingdom — divided across four countries. The Afar, between Ethiopia, Eritrea, and Djibouti. Communities that existed before 1884, fractured by people who had never been there. Every one of those fractures is still producing conflict.

## ACT 3: LEOPOLD AND THE LONG SHADOW (6:30 – 9:30)

### The Man Who Got a Country

`[TALKING HEAD]`

But the biggest thing to come out of Berlin wasn't a border — it was the conference giving one man a country.

In 1876, King Leopold the Second of Belgium told the world:

`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "I was guided by no egotism. Belgium may be a small country, but she is happy and satisfied with her fate." — Leopold II, 1876]`

"I was guided by no egotism. Belgium may be a small country, but she is happy and satisfied with her fate."

A year later, in a private letter:

`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "I do not want to risk... losing a fine chance to secure for ourselves a slice of this magnificent African cake." — Leopold II, 1877]`

"A slice of this magnificent African cake."

The conference gave Leopold the Congo Free State — two and a half million square kilometres — as his personal property. Not Belgium's colony. His. What he did with it — the rubber quotas, the severed hands, a population cut by at least half — that's a full video on its own, and it's coming. But his final words tell you everything. When Belgium forced him to hand it over in 1908:

`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "I will give them my Congo, but they have no right to know what I did there." — Leopold II, 1908]`

"I will give them my Congo, but they have no right to know what I did there."

Then his staff burned the archives. For eight days.

### Still Here

`[TALKING HEAD]`

The Berlin Conference ended in February 1885. A hundred and forty-one years later, its borders haven't moved.

In Sudan, the civil war that's displaced twelve million people — the largest displacement crisis on earth — is being fought across a racial line. A line that was drawn by a colonial census. In 1929, a British administrator categorised Darfuris by race rather than culture. Fifty-five percent spoke Arabic at home. The census classified only twenty-eight percent as "Arab." The rest were labelled "Other Negroid Westerners."

That label — from a century ago — is still how the world describes Darfur's violence.

`[MAP: Eastern DRC — Kivu provinces highlighted, Rwanda border marked]`

And in January 2025, Rwanda-backed rebels captured Goma — the capital of eastern Congo, a city of one million people. Belgian administrators drew the Congo-Rwanda border through territory that Rwandan kingdoms had historically controlled — and moved populations across it. Rwanda's president has publicly questioned those colonial borders, claiming parts of eastern Congo were always Rwandan land.

Those are Berlin Conference borders. And they're still being fought over.

`[TALKING HEAD]`

And after independence, African leaders looked at these borders — borders drawn by people who had never been there — and decided to keep them.

Julius Nyerere, the first president of Tanzania, explained why:

`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "Precisely because African boundaries were so absurd, it was essential to maintain them." — Julius Nyerere (Crawford Young, p. 303)]`

"Precisely because African boundaries were so absurd, it was essential to maintain them."

Because the alternative — redrawing every border along ethnic lines — would mean every group on the continent has a claim. The result wouldn't be justice. It would be endless war.

In 1964, the Organisation of African Unity put it in writing. Those borders would stand. Not because they were just. Because changing them was more dangerous than keeping them.

## CLOSE (9:30 – 11:00)

`[TALKING HEAD]`

If we're being fair, the Berlin Conference didn't start the Scramble for Africa. Historians call it a "safety valve" — a way to prevent European wars over African territory. And some African leaders, like Emperor Menelik the Second of Ethiopia, negotiated successfully within the system.

But the conference created the rules. It legitimised the race. And it gave one man a territory the size of Western Europe as personal property.

`[SPLIT-SCREEN: Murdock ethnic map LEFT, modern political map RIGHT]`

The map they ignored is still there. 229 ethnic groups, still divided. Borders drawn in three months, in a room with no Africans, still on every map you'll see today.

`[TALKING HEAD]`

A hundred and forty-one years later, we're still living with what they decided.
