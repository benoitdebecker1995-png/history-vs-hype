# The Crusades Were a Jihad

*The people who celebrate them would despise the people who fought them.*

---

In 1204, the head of the Byzantine civil service told his wife to smear mud on her face so she would not be targeted for rape. He picked up his infant and walked into the smoke. The greatest Christian city on earth was burning, and the men setting the fires had sworn to defend it.

They were Crusaders.

**Why it matters:** The U.S. Secretary of Defense has a "Deus Vult" tattoo. The Islamist ideologue Sayyid Qutb wrote that "the crusading spirit runs in the blood of all westerners." Both sides need the myth. Neither side reads the primary sources. When you do read them, the Crusades start to look less like a clash of civilizations and more like a case study in what religious zealotry does to its own people.

---

## A Recruitment Manifesto That Includes Eating the Dead

The most effective recruitment propaganda in the medieval world was written by a Christian chaplain. His name was Fulcher of Chartres. He marched with the First Crusade. On July 15th, 1099, his comrades breached the walls of Jerusalem and began killing. Fulcher wrote what happened next:

> "Not one of them was allowed to live. They did not spare the women and children."

Then he described the men who did the killing: still dripping in blood, they changed into "fresh garments," walked with "clean hands and bare feet" to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, and worshipped.

His fellow chronicler Raymond d'Aguilers was more direct:

> "Indeed, it was a just and splendid judgement of God that this place should be filled with the blood of the unbelievers."

Blood of the unbelievers. Praise God. The slaughter was the worship.

Fulcher did not write this as a private confession. Thomas Asbridge, whose history of the Crusades is the standard English-language reference, identifies his chronicle as a "recruitment manifesto," a physical document copied and carried across Europe to convince more men to take the cross. He included everything: the massacres, the miracles, and the cannibalism.

At the siege of Ma'arra in December 1098, months before Jerusalem, Fulcher recorded this:

> "Many of our people, harassed by excessive hunger, cut pieces from the buttocks of the Saracens already dead there, which they cooked and devoured with savage mouth."

Jay Rubenstein, whose *Armies of Heaven* reconstructed the First Crusade from primary sources, concluded this was not just famine. It was "deliberate cannibalism," used to underscore "holy war, whose rules of combat were inherently different." Riley-Smith and Throop note that Latin writers were "consistently uncomfortable" with these acts, often blaming the "poorest crusaders" to protect the movement's reputation.

Reading a recruitment manifesto that includes eating the dead broke something in how I think about the Crusades. The romantic ideal that gets portrayed in pop culture, the noble knight defending Christendom, cannot survive contact with the Crusaders' own writing.

## Gospel and Sword

The Pope who launched the Crusade knew exactly who he was recruiting. At the Council of Clermont in 1095, Urban II addressed the knights of France:

> "You, girded with the badge of knighthood, you are nothing but puffs of arrogance and pride. You rage against your brothers and cut each other in pieces... You, oppressors of children, robbers of widows... hired to shed Christian blood."

Europe was full of armed men with no external enemy. Frankish provinces had fractured into petty fiefdoms, each controlled by castle lords and their knights who, as Riley-Smith and Throop document, "so terrorized their neighborhoods that they came to represent the only authorities." The Crusade gave them a target.

But it also gave them a problem. Ralph of Caen captured the psychological torment of the Norman warrior Tancred, who recognized the gap between the Gospel and the sword:

> "The Lord enjoins that the struck cheek and the other one be offered to the striker, whereas secular authority requires that not even relatives' blood be spared... his mind was torn two ways, uncertain which path to follow, that of the Gospel, or that of the world."

The crusade indulgence resolved the contradiction. Urban II promised that "all who die by the way, whether by land or by sea, or in battle against the pagans, shall have immediate remission of sins." Kill for Christ, and your sins are forgiven. Ralph of Caen wrote that after the indulgence was announced, Tancred's "energies were aroused, as though he had earlier been asleep."

That framing stopped me cold. It is structurally identical to how modern extremist groups use stories of sacrifice and suffering as proof of divine commitment. The suicide bomber who gives everything for the cause. The soldier who wades through blood and then prays. The message in both cases is the same: look how far we were willing to go. That should tell you how right we are.

The Islamic shahid who dies in jihad receives the same structural reward: immediate Paradise, sins forgiven, skipping the wait for Resurrection. Michael Bonner, in *Jihad in Islamic History* (Princeton University Press), confirms that "no direct link has been found between them. The Latins must have taken this step for their own reasons, and not merely through imitation of their Islamic foe." They did not copy each other. They independently built the same machine.

## Before They Left Europe

The indulgence did not wait for Jerusalem. It found its first targets at home. In 1096, armies marched through the Rhineland and attacked Jewish communities in Speyer, Worms, Mainz, and Cologne. Between 3,000 and 5,000 Jews were killed. In Mainz alone, between 700 and 1,300.

Albert of Aachen recorded their logic:

> "The beginning of their expedition and their duty against the enemies of the Christian faith."

Why fight enemies of Christ in Jerusalem when they lived next door?

At Jerusalem itself, the targeting extended beyond Muslims. Ibn al-Qalanisi, the Muslim chronicler, recorded that the Jews "had gathered in their synagogue and the Franks burnt them alive."

The violence was not strategic. It was doctrinal. And it did not discriminate. First Jews. Then Muslims. Then, within a century, Christians themselves.

## The Senator's City

The stated goal of the Fourth Crusade had been Jerusalem, via Egypt. The actual target was Constantinople, the largest Christian city on earth. The Crusaders owed Venice 34,000 marks for transport. Venice had a solution: forget Egypt.

Venice wrote the terms into a document known as the March Pact. Jonathan Phillips, Professor of Crusading History at Royal Holloway, University of London, records what it said: Venice would take three-quarters of all spoils until 200,000 silver marks were paid. After that, everything would be split equally. The empire itself would be carved up, with one side choosing the emperor and the other the patriarch of the Hagia Sophia. Holy war had become a leveraged buyout.

Inside the city, Nicetas watched crusaders bring mules into the sanctuary of the Hagia Sophia to haul away gold. The animals slipped on the polished marble and "polluted the floors with blood and excrement." A prostitute sat in the Patriarch's throne, singing obscene songs. Soldiers broke open the purple sarcophagi of past emperors. The body of Justinian, undecayed after 639 years, was stripped of its valuables.

He called the crusaders:

> "Forerunners of Antichrist, agents of his anticipated ungodly deeds."

Even the Pope agreed. Innocent III, who had authorized the Crusade, wrote his condemnation:

> "They have spared neither age nor sex. They have exposed both matrons and virgins, even those dedicated to God, to the sordid lusts of boys."

Phillips documents that Pope John Paul II's 2001 apology showed "just how deep a wound had been left by this long-distant campaign."

## The Mechanism That Killed an Empire

The moral damage was obvious. The structural damage was worse.

**By the numbers:** The crusaders looted 3.6 million hyperpyra from Constantinople, roughly equivalent to stripping an entire nation's treasury in a single week. By the fifteenth century, the entire empire could barely pay 20,000 hyperpyra per year in tribute. Warren Treadgold's *History of the Byzantine State and Society* (Stanford University Press) documents the cascade: the bureaucrats fled, the navy died, the tax collectors vanished.

Anatolia, abandoned during the Latin occupation, grew "more Turkish and Muslim." When Michael VIII recaptured Constantinople in 1261, the state was so bankrupt his successor tried to save money by "doing without a navy or much of an army."

In 1453, Byzantium mustered 5,000 soldiers against 80,000 Ottomans. The walls had held for a thousand years. The foundation was gone.

If a "defensive" Crusade destroyed the one empire actually defending Christendom's eastern frontier, what exactly was it defending?

## The Mirror

Here is the parallel that I cannot stop thinking about.

According to the National Counterterrorism Center, over 80% of the victims of jihadist terrorism are Muslim. The people most likely to be killed by Islamic extremists are other Muslims. Entire communities forced into hiding, into silence, into compliance. Not because the extremists are fighting outsiders. Because the purity test always turns inward.

Now think about Nicetas. A Christian senator. Head of the civil service of the most powerful Christian state on earth. If the Crusaders were real defenders of Christendom, he should have walked into the street and been safe. Instead, he hid from his co-religionists while a wine merchant named Dominic, wearing a stolen crusader helmet, guarded his door. A Christian fleeing Christians in a Christian city.

That is the structural signature of religious extremism. It does not protect its own. It consumes them.

Riley-Smith and Throop name this pattern in the most recent edition of the standard Crusades history: "Holy war has a tendency to turn inward. Its participants come to believe that it can never be successful if the society that has bred it is unregenerate and impure." They draw the explicit parallel to Salafist jihadis inspired by the medieval theorist Ibn Taymiyya, whose priority was "not to wage war beyond the frontiers but to purge Sunni Islam of alien elements... to create by force a united and purified society dedicated to God." Two religious traditions, separated by centuries, arriving at the same conclusion: cleanse your own people first.

## What the Defenders Get Right

The defenders of the Crusades are not entirely wrong, and I want to be careful about that.

The Battle of Manzikert in 1071 was a real defeat. Emperor Alexius I did appeal to the Pope, though he wanted professional mercenaries, not 60,000 zealots. Islamic expansion in the 7th century was genuine conquest. The jizya tax was real, paid by adult males, at rates of 1 to 4 dinars per year. The Sixth Crusade in 1229 showed that negotiation was possible: Emperor Frederick II secured Jerusalem without a battle. Not all campaigns ended like Jerusalem or Constantinople.

But here is where the omission matters.

The viral video that frames the Crusades as "awesome" calls the jizya an "enormous tax." The actual rates: wealthy merchants paid 4 dinars per year. Laborers paid 1 dinar. Women, children, the elderly, and the disabled were exempt. A Crusader knight, by contrast, needed four to six times his annual income just to reach the Holy Land. Richard I of England spent half the crown's annual revenue in a single year of preparations. The jizya funded local government. The Crusade bankrupted kingdoms.

For comparison, Mark R. Cohen, Professor of Near Eastern Studies at Princeton, documents in *Under Crescent and Cross* that in Crusader states, Muslims paid a poll tax PLUS half their crops. In Christian Europe, Jews faced "numerous and often unreasonably high and arbitrary taxes," and were eventually expelled entirely: England in 1290, France in 1306, Spain in 1492.

Cohen's assessment: Jews "lived more securely in the medieval Arab-Islamic world than under Christendom."

And the Crusaders themselves proved the contradiction. Baldwin I of Jerusalem struck his own coins with Arabic inscriptions to make trade with Muslims easier. By 1251, Crusader gold minted at Acre carried the declaration "We take pride in the Cross of our Lord Jesus the Messiah," written not in Latin, but in flowing Arabic script. When money was involved, the language of the infidel worked just fine.

The Crusades did not save Christianity in Europe. Geography did. Carolingian defense did. Byzantine resilience did, until the Fourth Crusade broke it.

---

## Two Memories

Fulcher of Chartres and Nicetas Choniates both wrote histories of the Crusades. Both were Christians. Both were there.

Fulcher wrote a recruitment manifesto. He changed into fresh garments after the massacre and walked barefoot to worship. He included the cannibalism because it proved commitment. He framed the blood as a sacrament.

Nicetas wrote an autopsy. He smeared mud on his wife's face and carried his infant through the smoke. He spent the rest of his life in exile in Nicaea, documenting what was lost. Scholars describe his history as charged with "understandable indignation" and a "deep sense of loss."

Most people who admire the Crusades today are deeply opposed to Islamic extremism. They would recoil from a recruitment video that glorifies killing unbelievers, that frames atrocity as divine worship, that consumes its own co-religionists in the name of purity. They would call that jihadism.

Fulcher's chronicle is that recruitment video, written 900 years earlier in Latin by a Christian. Translated into Arabic, it would read as jihad; in its original Latin, it reads as crusade. The chronicle has not changed in nine centuries. Only the audience that claims it has.
