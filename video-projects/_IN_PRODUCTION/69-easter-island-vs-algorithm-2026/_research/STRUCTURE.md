### BEAT 2 · The first test — the roads · 2:00–5:00 · **THE KNOCKOUT**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Viewer question** | *Take the flagship claim. Is it true?* |
| **Exhibits** | `LipoHunt-2025-WalkingMoai` — road-width sentence; `HuntLipo-2011` p. 79 (Love); the walking experiment stills |

B's central discovery: the roads are narrower than the widest statue bases, therefore the roads were
never transport — they were ritual, and the paving stones ring when struck.

This one you can settle with a tape measure.

- **Lipo & Hunt 2025, verbatim:** *"The width of the moai road is ca. 4.5 meters."*
- The road statue they modelled, 12-220-01: *"7.35 m tall and 2.83 m wide."*
- And independently, in 2000 — geologist **Charlie Love**, who mapped five miles of it:
  *"the roadway width is quite consistent, measuring about eighteen feet across"* — about 5.5 m.
- And in the **1950s**, by excavation — **Thor Heyerdahl**, who believed something completely
  different about how the statues moved, dug around a fallen moai on a road and reported the roadway
  was *"slightly wider than the [moai] base [and] was as firm and even as if a steamroller had
  prepared the ground."*

The road is roughly **1.6 times wider** than the widest statue on it. B says narrower. **The claim is
not unsupported. It is inverted.**

> **Three researchers, three eras, three rival theories of transport — Heyerdahl, Love, Lipo & Hunt —
> and all three report the road is wider than the base.** This was not an open question a machine
> settled. It was answered by excavation in the 1950s and re-answered twice since.

⚠ **Honest limit, said on camera.** That ratio is for the road moai, and specifically for the statue
that was scanned and replicated. The giants are a different case — Paro is ~82 tonnes and ~9.8 m —
and we don't have their base widths. Say so, and say what the paper documents instead: of nine road
statues over 8 m, **six failed within 1.5 km of the quarry** and none got past 4.27 km, while smaller
ones reached nearly 7.7 km. Do not claim "no moai was ever wider than a road."

Two more things the same paper contains:

- The roads have **concave cross-sections that match the curve of a moai base** — read as engineering
  to guide the statue, not an obstacle to it.
- Its subtitle is **"and Response to Critics."** B's discovery is an objection the authors answer in
  the paper itself.

And the experiment: **4.35 tonnes, 18 people, 100 metres in 40 minutes**, on that road design.

> **Inference, stated plainly:** a system handed every piece of data on Easter Island would have had
> this paper. It is the most cited recent work on the exact question. The answer it produced is the
> one the paper spends a section refuting.

### BEAT 4 · The third test — the bearings · 7:00–9:30 · **REWRITTEN 3 Sep**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Viewer question** | *Where does a claim like this even come from?* |
| **Exhibits** | `HuntLipo-2011` p. 17–18; the 2013 Blogspot post; `Edwards-2018` alignment count + mapping-error line |

> ⚠ **This beat was "I looked and found nothing." It is now "I found it, and it's a blog."** The
> database pass located the claim. Full record: `STAGE-B-PART2-ORIGINS.md` §3.

B's largest claim: extend the direction each statue faces and the lines converge on **11 navigational
bearings** — Hawaii, the Marquesas, the Society Islands, New Zealand.

**Start with the method, because the method cannot work.** The statues face inland. Backs to the sea.
Hunt and Lipo: *"the vast majority face inland, watching over their descendants day after day. With
their backs to the sea, the moai had not been carved as sentries."* A line extended from the
direction a moai faces runs into the middle of the island.

**Then: the claim is not new, and it is not a machine's.**

There is a self-published Blogspot post — *"Rapa Nui: A Compass of the Pacific"* — dated **17 May
2013**, by an author whose profile reads "Unknown," self-described as a certified professional
surveyor. It uses **pairs of platforms with Volcano Terevaka as the apex**, and names four bearings:
Hawai'i, New Zealand, Lake Titicaca, Tikal.

And it reports its own hit rate: ***"42% of the protracted bearings pointed towards significant
centers of culture."***

Forty-two percent. Which means **58% of them pointed at open ocean**, and the author says so.

Specimen B: *"every single one of them corresponded to a navigational bearing toward a significant
destination."*

> **The beat:** somewhere between a surveyor's blog in 2013 and a video with half a million views in
> 2026, the failure rate disappeared. That is the same move as Video A dropping a scope limit, and
> the same move as the popular retelling dropping Diamond's hedges — except here it converts a weak
> claim into an impossible one.

⚠ **Say the limit out loud.** I cannot prove the video took it from that blog. Two destinations match
exactly, two don't, the counts differ, and **"11 convergence points" appears nowhere at all.** The
on-screen claim is: *this claim exists, it is thirteen years old, it is unreviewed, and its own
author reported it working 42% of the time.* Treat the blog's author like Erik Kiley — a real person
doing real work by their own lights, not the target.

**Then the complication — and this is the beat that makes it a History vs Hype video, not a dunk.**

Navigation *was* encoded here. Just not in the statues. Rapanui astronomy has **25 collected star
names**; **Antares — *Te Hau Vaero* —** is the zenith star that fixes the island's latitude, passing
at 89°19' overhead; *Nga Vaka* is recorded as important for navigation. And there is a real
archaeoastronomy of the island going back forty years: **58 registered alignments**, most of them
*tupa* — stone towers — oriented to the Pleiades and Orion's Belt. Liller remeasured Mulloy's field
notes across 23 shore platforms. His 1989 paper is titled *"Orientations of ahu and moai."*

So the question is not new, and the machine did not raise it.

On the 0.4° precision — **and this one has a real anchor too, which is the generous version.** A
genuine sub-degree alignment exists: **Ahu Huri A Urenga**, read by Mulloy in 1973 as a real solar
observatory, faces winter solstice sunrise **to within a single degree**, its platform twisted 20°
against its own plaza so the statue catches the sunrise. Roughly **20 ahu** are described as
deliberately astronomically oriented. Out of hundreds.

B reports that as an island-wide average error of 0.4° and says *"you do not accidentally build 900
statues on platforms aligned to celestial events with sub-degree accuracy."* **Twenty became nine
hundred. One site became an island.**

And the constraint that makes island-wide sub-degree precision impossible: the field's own stated
obstacle is *"degradation and site location due to **errors in the official mapping of the
island**."* You cannot claim accuracy to four-tenths of a degree off a basemap the specialists say is
wrong.

> **Inference:** every one of B's claims has a real, modest, named ancestor — one site, one blog, one
> literature — inflated by an order of magnitude and re-badged as something a machine found.

⚠ **Absence discipline.** On screen this is *"I could not find this in any published source, and here
is where I looked"* — never *"it is fabricated."* Pukao is the exception: that is a direct term search
of the actual paper, and can be stated as a finding.

> ⏺ **CAPTURE.** "Here is where I looked" has to be shown, not asserted — the database searches
> running, the result counts, the empty returns. That footage is what makes the restraint credible
> instead of evasive. It also means the database pass has to be **recorded while it is run**, not
> reconstructed afterwards.
