## HOOK (0:00-1:00)

[COLD FACT: 0:00-0:10]

Most border disputes are about two countries arguing over who got to the land first. The story of the Bakassi Peninsula is different.

[DEFINITION: 0:10-0:25]

Nigeria's case was built on one historical fact: in 1884, the local Kings didn't sell their land to Britain. They hired Britain to protect it. That's what the treaty says. And Nigeria argued that you can't give away something you never owned.

[CONTRADICTION: 0:25-0:45]

In 2002, the International Court of Justice — the highest court in the world — looked at that argument and rejected it. The court ruled that in the 1800s, a European "Treaty of Protection" was a synonym for sovereignty. If you protected it, you owned it. And with that, roughly 1,000 square kilometers — a swampy mangrove peninsula jutting into the Gulf of Guinea, home to between 150,000 and 300,000 people — belonged to Cameroon. Not Nigeria. Thirteen to three.

[PAYOFF: 0:45-1:00]

So I read the full judgment — all 155 pages — and three dissenting opinions. And what I found is a story about what happens when the truth on paper and the truth on the ground stop matching — and a court has to pick one.

**[B-ROLL: ICJ courtroom → 1884 Treaty scan → map of Bakassi → split: treaty text vs map of Nigerian settlements]**

## ACT 1: THE TREATY (1:00-3:00)

### The 1884 Treaty (1:00-1:45)

It starts with a document signed aboard a British warship.

On September 10th, 1884, the Kings and Chiefs of Old Calabar signed a Treaty of Protection with Britain. Old Calabar was a federation of Efik city-states in what's now southeastern Nigeria — they had their own courts, their own trade networks, and they controlled the palm oil routes along the coast.

The signing happened aboard a ship called HMS Flirt. The document still exists. You can still see the signature of British Consul Edward Hyde Hewett — and the X marks of the chiefs who couldn't write their names.

**[B-ROLL: Show treaty scan — X marks visible]**

The treaty says — and I'll put the exact text on screen:

> "Her Majesty the Queen of Great Britain and Ireland, in compliance with the request of the Kings, Chiefs, and people of Old Calabar, hereby undertakes to extend to them, and to the territory under their authority and jurisdiction, her gracious favour and protection."
> — ICJ Reports 2002, para. 204

Protection. Not ownership. The chiefs agreed to let Britain handle their external affairs. They didn't agree to give their land away.

[BEAT GAP — hold on treaty text]

### What Protection Looked Like (1:45-3:00)

On paper, this was a protectorate. On the ground, Britain ran it like a colony.

They set up their own courts. Collected their own taxes. Appointed their own local administrators — "Warrant Chiefs" — to replace traditional leaders. And when challenged, they acted like owners.

In December 1894, German soldiers crossed into Bakassi and arrested a local chief named Ekanem Esin. His crime? He refused to accept three bottles of beer as a bribe to let Germany rule his land.

Britain's Consul-General, Sir Claude Macdonald, fired off a protest letter to the German Governor:

> "Ekanem Esin is a British protected subject and as such I hold myself responsible for his action should he have done wrong, I will have him punished."
> — Sir Claude Macdonald, 18 December 1894

**[B-ROLL: Macdonald protest letter]**

Notice the language. "British protected subject" — but "I will have him punished." That's not how you talk about someone else's citizen. That's how an owner talks.

And that matters. Because the ICJ looked at exactly this kind of behavior — courts, taxes, administrators, punishment — and concluded that Britain hadn't just protected Old Calabar. Britain had governed it. The treaty said protection. The conduct said sovereignty. And the court ruled that the conduct is what counted.

And because Britain was the owner — not just the protector — they had the legal right to give the land away.

## ACT 2: THE TRANSFER (3:00-4:15)

### The 1913 Agreement (3:00-3:40)

On March 11th, 1913, Britain and Germany signed the Anglo-German Agreement in London. It drew the border between British Nigeria and German Kamerun — Cameroon's colonial name. The line ran from the north all the way down to the sea. And right there at the coast was Bakassi.

Article XX of that agreement:

> "Should the lower course of the Akwayafe so change its mouth as to transfer it to the Rio del Rey, it is agreed that the area now known as the Bakasi Peninsula shall still remain German territory."
> — Anglo-German Agreement, 11 March 1913, Article XX

"Shall still remain German territory." Signed over to Germany — and the ICJ judgment notes there's no evidence of any protest from the Kings of Old Calabar in 1913. Not because they agreed. Because nobody told them.

**[B-ROLL: Article XX text on screen, map showing boundary line]**

### Why the Court Upheld It (3:40-4:15)

So how does a modern court enforce a treaty signed by two colonial powers over territory they arguably didn't own?

The ICJ applied a principle called intertemporal law — in plain terms, you judge a treaty by the legal standards of the time it was signed. And in the late 1800s, European powers didn't draw a hard line between a colony and a protectorate. If you ran it, you owned it.

The court's conclusion, paragraph 209:

> "Under the law at the time, Great Britain was in a position in 1913 to determine its boundaries with Germany in respect of Nigeria."
> — ICJ Reports 2002, para. 209

That 1913 agreement — signed by two European powers, in London, without a single African present — is the exact document the ICJ used to award Bakassi to Cameroon in 2002. Britain's conduct on the ground proved they had sovereignty on paper. And once it was on paper, Cameroon inherited it.

**[B-ROLL: ICJ judgment page, para. 209 highlighted]**

## THE TURN (4:15-4:30)

So at this point, the story seems straightforward. Britain signed a protection treaty, ran it like a colony, gave it away. Nigeria's the victim.

But the court just established a principle: once a border is written on paper, it doesn't matter what happens on the ground. Britain's conduct made them owners on paper. Germany got that paper in 1913. Cameroon inherited it.

And here's the thing — if paper beats ground, then Nigeria had a problem. Because Nigeria had been conceding on paper for decades.

---

## ACT 3: NIGERIA'S SILENCE (4:30-6:00)

### The Plebiscite (4:30-5:15)

After World War I, Germany lost its colonies. The League of Nations split German Kamerun, and Britain took the strip along the Nigerian border — Southern Cameroons.

In 1961, the UN held a plebiscite — a direct public vote. The question: should Southern Cameroons join Nigeria, or join Cameroon? Bakassi was part of this vote. And according to the UN plebiscite data, seventy-three percent of voters in the Bakassi stations chose Cameroon.

That sounds strange. Most of the people on the peninsula were Efik — ethnically and culturally tied to southeastern Nigeria. So why would they vote to join Cameroon?

The answer, according to historian Piet Konings, is that it wasn't a vote *for* Cameroon. It was a vote *against* Nigeria. Britain had administered Southern Cameroons as part of Nigeria's Eastern Region, where the Igbo were the majority. The Efik felt dominated — economically and politically. So when the UN said "pick one," they picked the option that wasn't more of the same.

Konings puts it directly: the vote "appeared to be more of a rejection of continuous ties with Nigeria than a vote for union with Francophone Cameroon."

**[B-ROLL: 1961 plebiscite results map]**

Six years later, Nigeria created a separate state for the Efik — away from Igbo control. The reason people voted to leave disappeared. But the vote still counted.

### The Thirty-Year Silence (5:15-5:45)

Nigeria's federal government accepted the result. They voted *for* the UN resolution. No objection. No claim. And then — nothing happened. Nobody enforced the new border. The peninsula was swampy mangrove — no fence, no checkpoint. The local Efik population just kept living as they always had. Using Nigerian currency, sending their kids to schools they built themselves, paying taxes to nearby Nigerian councils. These weren't government schools or state hospitals. They were community-run, locally funded. The Nigerian federal government wasn't running Bakassi. The people on the ground were just ignoring a line on a map.

For thirty years, Nigeria's own official maps showed Bakassi inside Cameroon. In 1962, Nigeria's own diplomats sent a formal note describing oil blocks south of Bakassi as "offshore Cameroon." On paper, Nigeria had conceded. On the ground, the people never got the message.

### The Maroua Declaration (5:45-6:00)

Then came 1975. Nigeria's head of state — Yakubu Gowon — met Cameroon's president in Maroua to settle the maritime boundary. He took British Admiralty Chart 3433, and with eighteen officials watching, he drew the boundary line on the map. Then he signed it.

[BEAT GAP — hold on visual]

But an ocean boundary has to anchor to the coast. By drawing that line through the waters south of Bakassi, Gowon had signed away the peninsula in everything but name.

Fifty-eight days later, Gowon was overthrown in a military coup. His successor, General Murtala Mohammed, threw the map in the trash. Called it a "waste-paper basket" agreement. The new regime assumed that because they'd overthrown the man, they'd overthrown his signature.

But the ICJ applied the Vienna Convention — the international rulebook for treaties. When a head of state signs, that signature counts. The next government doesn't get to undo it.

On paper, Nigeria had conceded. On the ground, Nigeria was still there.

Eventually, those two realities had to collide. In 1994, Cameroon sued.

---

## ACT 4: THE RULING (6:00-7:15)

### Thirteen to Three (6:00-6:20)

On October 10th, 2002, the ICJ ruled. Thirteen to three. Bakassi belonged to Cameroon.

[BEAT GAP]

Two realities had been running in parallel for decades. Nigeria's diplomats were writing "offshore Cameroon." Nigeria's maps showed Cameroon. Nigeria's leaders were signing maritime boundaries. But on the peninsula itself — local courts, community schools, taxes paid to nearby regional councils, Nigerian passports. Over ninety percent of the population considered themselves Nigerian.

Two realities. One on paper, one on the ground.

### Why Paper Won (6:20-6:50)

The ICJ applied two principles. Intertemporal law: you judge a treaty by the standards of the era it was signed — and in the 1800s, protection meant sovereignty. The Vienna Convention: when a head of state signs, that signature binds the country. Intertemporal law validated the 1913 treaty. The Vienna Convention made Gowon's signature permanent.

And the court added a third argument: if countries could override written treaties just by occupying territory long enough, every military occupation would eventually become legal. Paper has to beat ground — otherwise the entire system of international borders falls apart.

[BEAT GAP]

That logic makes sense. But in this case, it meant that what 300,000 people experienced every day mattered less than what diplomats signed on paper.

### The Dissent (6:50-7:15)

Three judges disagreed. Judge Abdul Koroma from Sierra Leone called the ruling what it was:

> "This finding, in violation of the applicable treaty and clearly in breach of the principle of pacta sunt servanda — the principle that treaties must be honored — is not only illegal but unjust."
> — Judge Koroma, Dissenting Opinion, para. 7

Judge Ajibola — Nigeria's own appointed judge — argued the Bakassi provisions were "null and void." These weren't fringe positions. These were ICJ judges saying the majority got it wrong.

## ACT 5: WHAT HAPPENED TO THE PEOPLE (7:15-9:00)

### The Promises (7:15-7:40)

But the majority ruled. And now someone had to enforce that piece of paper on 300,000 people.

On June 12th, 2006, Nigeria and Cameroon signed the Greentree Agreement — the handover framework, witnessed by Kofi Annan and representatives from four countries. Article 3 guaranteed the people who stayed their property rights, fishing rights, freedom from forced nationality change, and language protections.

On paper, these people were protected.

### The Reality (7:40-9:00)

President Obasanjo signed the agreement in June 2006. Over a year later, in November 2007, Nigeria's Senate rejected it — said it violated the constitution. But the handover proceeded anyway.

On August 14th, 2008, Nigeria completed the withdrawal. The UN called it a "triumph for the rule of law."

On the ground, it looked different.

In 2011, in the main settlement of Akwa, two people were found eligible for Cameroonian identity documents. Two. The rest couldn't vote. Displaced residents who went back to Nigeria had been removed from the voter registers there, too. Citizens of nowhere.

**[B-ROLL: On-screen quote]**

> "Tens of thousands of people, possibly more, have lost access to nationality or citizenship rights, and now live in a legal limbo."
> — Chidi Odinkalu, Open Society Justice Initiative, 2012

[BEAT GAP]

Fishermen who'd worked the sea their entire lives got relocated to landlocked areas. Eighty-five percent said they were unsatisfied. The displaced residents started calling themselves "political orphans." A generation of children born after the handover grew up undocumented — legally invisible in both countries.

And by 2021, an armed separatist group calling itself the Black Marines started attacking Cameroonian forces on the peninsula. By 2023, Cameroon was responding with airstrikes. The territory the international community declared "settled" is now an active conflict zone.

---

## CLOSING (9:00-9:45)

This story has a pattern, and it repeats.

In 1884, a treaty said protection. On the ground, Britain ran a colony.

In 1913, a treaty said German territory. On the ground, nobody told the chiefs.

In 1961, a vote said Cameroon. On the ground, the people never left.

In 2006, an agreement said rights and protections. On the ground, two people got documents.

The International Court of Justice doesn't exist to make things fair. It exists to make things final. And in Bakassi, the court did what courts do — it picked the version of reality that was written down.

Two treaties promised these people protection — one in 1884, one in 2006. Neither one was kept.
