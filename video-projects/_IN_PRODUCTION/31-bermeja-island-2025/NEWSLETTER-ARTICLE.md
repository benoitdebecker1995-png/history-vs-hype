# The $22 Billion Island That Never Existed

A 1539 typo survived 382 years, shaped a treaty, and launched a CIA conspiracy theory.

---

In 2000, the United States and Mexico signed a treaty dividing the oil-rich seabed of the Gulf of Mexico. Mexico got 62% of the contested area. The US got 38%. And the reason Mexico didn't get more comes down to an island that showed up on maps for 382 years but was never there.

The island wasn't destroyed, wasn't eroded, wasn't sunk. The ocean floor at those coordinates has been undisturbed for over 5,300 years. Before the Spanish Empire, before the map itself existed, there was nothing but deep water. A sailor in the 1500s saw something reddish on the horizon, a bureaucrat in Seville wrote it down, and nearly four centuries of cartographers copied it without checking.

**Why it matters:** Maps aren't neutral pictures of the earth. They carry the errors of every cartographer who came before. And sometimes those errors cost billions.

---

## A Reddish Blur on the Horizon

In 1539, Spain's chief cartographer Alonso de Santa Cruz compiled reports from every ship coming back from the New World into a single atlas called the *Islario General*, "The General Atlas of All the Islands." In the section on the Yucatan, he wrote:

> "...y por el mismo viento y por veynte lenguas esta otra isla dicha la Bermeja por parecer de lexos de la tal color..."

"Twenty leagues distant is another island called Bermeja, as it appears to be of such a color."

*Bermeja.* From *bermejo*, meaning reddish. That's all it took. A color observed from a distance.

The problem was that Santa Cruz wasn't in the Gulf of Mexico. He was in Seville, compiling reports from sailors who had no reliable way to measure longitude. Their main tool was dead reckoning: you drop a log overboard, count how fast the rope unspools, and estimate how far you've traveled. But the method only measures speed in one direction. If an ocean current pushes the ship sideways, the navigator has no idea. You could end up 100 miles from where you thought you were.

So a sailor sees a real island (probably Cayo Arenas), logs it at the wrong coordinates. Santa Cruz draws it as a new discovery. Bermeja was born as a duplicate entry in a 16th-century database.

I've covered this pattern before with the Treaty of Tordesillas, where a line was drawn by men who couldn't measure the coordinate they chose. Bermeja is the same instinct operating in reverse: instead of an imaginary line creating real consequences, an imaginary island created real territory. In both cases, the map outlived the error.

Ortelius copied Bermeja in 1564. Mexico's official atlas still showed it in 1921. Nobody deleted it because in cartography, deleting is dangerous. If you're wrong, ships run aground. So they kept copying it. For 382 years.

That would have been embarrassing and nothing more, except for what was sitting beneath the Gulf.

## The Donut Hole

Under maritime law, every nation controls water 200 miles from its coast. The US measures south from Texas. Mexico measures north from the Yucatan. In the deep central Gulf, a wedge of international water opens up where neither zone reaches. This is called the "donut hole" — the treaty calls it the Western Polygon.

By 2010, Mexican senators were estimating more than 22 billion barrels of oil sitting beneath it. We have to keep in mind that this is a political estimate, not a geological survey. But even a fraction of that figure is still an enormous amount of oil.

If Bermeja existed at its historical coordinates, it would be Mexico's northernmost territory. Mexico's 200-mile zone would start there, not the mainland. That would push their boundary north by roughly 15%, giving Mexico sole ownership of oil fields currently in international waters.

In 1997, the Mexican Navy sent a ship to find the island and plant a flag. They came back with nothing. The island wasn't there.

On June 9th, 2000, the US and Mexico signed the treaty dividing the donut hole anyway. Mexico got 62% of the Western Polygon, roughly 5,092 square nautical miles. The US got 38%. The *Mexican Law Review* later noted that the loss of any portion of Mexico's continental shelf in the Western Polygon "could be catastrophic to the future of the Mexican economy."

The treaty was signed without Bermeja as a baseline. And that's when the conspiracy started.

## A Senator, a Car Crash, and the CIA

Senator Jose Angel Conchello had been the loudest voice opposing the negotiations. He warned of a secret plan to give away Mexico's oil to American firms.

In 1998, he died in a car accident. The official cause was mechanical failure. His supporters called it assassination.

By 2008, the theory had reached the Mexican Parliament: the CIA destroyed Bermeja Island to steal Mexican oil.

I knew the conspiracy existed before I started researching this. I expected the debunk to be complicated, some ambiguous piece of evidence that left room for doubt. It wasn't. The science is simple and total.

Bermeja was reportedly 80 square kilometers. That's roughly the size of Manhattan. According to Jaime Urrutia of UNAM and Saul Millan of the National Polytechnic Institute:

> "To obliterate an island of Bermeja's size, a hydrogen bomb would be required."

A hydrogen bomb in the middle of the Gulf of Mexico. And nobody noticed.

German oceanographer Hans-Werner Schenke pointed out that modern surveys of the ocean floor show nothing. No rubble. No debris. No underwater plateau. Zero evidence of a destroyed island.

---

## Seven Institutes, One Answer

In 2009, the Mexican Parliament commissioned UNAM (the National Autonomous University of Mexico) to settle this. Seven university institutes. A research vessel called the *Justo Sierra*. Two Navy support ships. Aerial surveys taking 933 photographs. And bathymetry, deep-sea sonar mapping the ocean floor.

From March 21st to the 27th, they searched 10,488 square kilometers around the official coordinates.

**By the numbers:** They found water. 1,472 meters deep. Nearly a mile of ocean.

But the sediment is what closes the case. Deep-sea sediment accumulates slowly, layer by layer, over thousands of years. If an island had been there recently and destroyed, the top layer would be chaotic: rocky debris, a geological mess. It wasn't. It was undisturbed. Perfectly stratified. Stable.

> "No island has existed at those coordinates for more than 5,300 years."

5,300 years. That predates the CIA. It predates the United States. It predates the Spanish Empire.

The island was never there.

To be fair, UNAM's Dr. Irasema Alcantara Ayala noted that the historical descriptions were "precise" and "detailed," suggesting someone really did see something. Just not where they thought they were. The most likely candidate is Cayo Arenas, a real island logged at the wrong coordinates because a 16th-century navigator couldn't account for sideways drift.

I knew cartographic errors existed. I made a video about how the Berlin Conference drew borders across territory the Europeans had never surveyed. But I assumed those errors were about imprecision, about lines being roughly in the wrong place. Bermeja is different. This isn't a line that was off by a few miles. This is an entire island that never existed, copied faithfully for centuries because no one had the tools or the incentive to check.

## Phantom Islands Are Not Rare

Bermeja isn't unique. In 2012, an Australian research ship sailed to Sandy Island near New Caledonia, an island that appeared on Google Maps, and found nothing but deep water. It had been on charts since 1876. Probably a raft of floating pumice mistaken for land.

Sandy Island was removed from Google Maps within days. Bermeja stayed on official Mexican maps until 2009 because in the analog era, nobody profits from admitting the map was wrong.

We've mapped Mars in higher resolution than we've mapped our own ocean floor. Some of those ocean charts are still echoing data from the 1800s. The US Hydrographic Office called Bermeja "more than doubtful" in 1885. Nobody acted on that for over a century.

## The Map Is Not the Territory

Bermeja proves something uncomfortable. Maps are not objective pictures of the earth. They're historical documents. They carry the assumptions, the errors, and the limitations of everyone who drew them before.

A reddish blur on the horizon, a tired sailor, and a bureaucrat in Seville who didn't want to delete anything. 382 years later, a treaty worth billions was signed without the one piece of land that could have changed the math.

The map was wrong. The oil was real. And for 382 years, every cartographer trusted the last one's work. It took billions of barrels of oil to make someone finally sail out and look.
