Calling it a legend implies the story drifted into folklore over time. But the historical record shows people engineered this narrative on purpose — and we can date every piece.

## STANDARD MYTH NARRATION [VERBATIM] (1:00-2:00)

[DELIVERY: dead-straight textbook voice. No irony. Sell the familiar version at face value so the Turn at 2:00 lands harder.]

Here's how the story goes. 1626. Peter Minuit steps ashore. He meets a group of Lenape who, supposedly, don't understand property. He hands them a chest of beads and trinkets. They hand him the island. Total cost: twenty-four dollars.

Depending on who's telling it, it's either the greatest real estate deal in history or the most famous swindle in American history.

But the visual iconography never changes. For over a century, textbooks, museums, and postcards have all reproduced the exact same image of a kneeling Native American examining a chest of glass beads under a tree.

It's a great story. Everybody knows it.

There's just one thing wrong with it: the primary sources don't support any of it.

## THE TURN [VERBATIM] (2:00-2:25)

<!-- TURN MOMENT -->

If the 1626 record is only one sentence, how did we get such a detailed legend? Historian Russell Shorto points to *"a gaping hole at the earliest period of New York's prehistory, which nineteenth-century historians filled in as best they could."* To understand why they filled it, we have to follow the paper trail.

## ACT 2: THE 1844 EXCHANGE RATE [VERBATIM] (2:15-3:15)

[VERBATIM]

A journalist named Edmund Bailey O'Callaghan manufactured the twenty-four-dollar conversion. In January 1844, he published an article in an Albany journal called the *Northern Light*. His exact words: *"the purchase of the Island of Manhattans, two hundred and seventeen years ago, by the Dutch, from the Indians, for the sum of sixty guilders, or twenty-four dollars!"*

[VISUAL: Show the quote on screen]

He showed no primary sources to back up that math. And applying an 1844 exchange rate to a 1626 transaction tells us nothing about seventeenth-century buying power. Currencies drift across two centuries — inflation, silver content, and what a guilder actually buys all shift with them. Converting 1626 money at an 1844 rate doesn't tell you what the money was worth in 1626 — it tells you what the number looked like to a reader in 1844. Shorto's point: the twenty-four-dollar figure dates to the mid-nineteenth century and has no relationship to buying power two hundred years earlier.

But look at what the archive actually shows. Sixty guilders in 1626 was real money. A West India Company soldier earned about a hundred guilders a year — so Manhattan cost more than half a year's salary. And when Dutch colonists sold land to each other, the numbers line up. In 1638, a hundred acres of Long Island sold Dutch-to-Dutch for fifty-two guilders. Shorto puts it plainly: *"in its wilderness state New World land was dirt cheap."* The idea that sixty guilders was a laughable sum wasn't a 1626 idea. It was invented in 1844, by a journalist who converted the currency so the price would sound absurd to a nineteenth-century reader.

[VERBATIM — bridge to Act 3]

That 1844 number is still the figure most people carry in their heads today. But if there was no money involved — where did we get a chest overflowing with beads?

## ACT 3: THE PAINTER'S INVENTION [VERBATIM] (3:15-4:15)

[VISUAL: Ranney's 1853 painting on screen]

This painting is the reason you think there were beads.

It was painted by William Ranney in 1853. According to historian Stephen McErleane, it "contains the first inclusion in an image or text of the myth's infamous beads."

[VISUAL: Side-by-side — Ranney painting LEFT, Benjamin West's 1771 *Penn's Treaty* RIGHT]

Look at these side by side. Ranney copied Benjamin West's 1771 painting of William Penn's treaty with the Indians — the tree, the kneeling figure, the composition. He took Penn's painting and turned it into Manhattan's origin story. And he added a chest overflowing with beads.

[VERBATIM]

The Schagen letter — the only contemporary source — mentions no beads. No trinkets. Just "the value of 60 guilders." Based on other Dutch-Lenape agreements from this era, sixty guilders' worth would have been practical European technology — iron kettles, metal axes, drilling awls, heavy cloth. Not cheap jewelry.

The first historian to put beads in text was Martha Lamb in 1877. She wrote that the Dutch "offered beads, buttons, and other trinkets." She was describing Ranney's painting, not a primary source.

[BEAT]

Ranney painted a fictional chest of beads, Martha Lamb wrote his fiction into a history book, and later generations cited her text as fact.

[VERBATIM — bridge to Act 4]

So Ranney invented the chest. But the flaw in this myth isn't the evidence we have. It's the evidence we lack.

## ACT 4: THE MISSING DEED [VERBATIM] (4:15-5:15)

[VERBATIM]

According to retired National Park Service archaeologist Robert Grumet, author of *First Manhattans*: *"Few documents — and, significantly, no deed or bill of sale — chronicle the purchase of Manhattan."*

[VISUAL: Show Grumet quote on screen]

If you're wondering why the details of this transaction were so easily manipulated by nineteenth-century writers, it's because the original seventeenth-century records no longer exist to prove them wrong. In 1821, the Netherlands government gathered up every pre-1700 Dutch East and West India Company document they still possessed and sold them to scrap paper dealers. Historian Russell Shorto calculates that *"eighty thousand pounds of records had vanished"* in this single bureaucratic purge. By the time historian John Romeyn Brodhead traveled to The Hague in 1839 to uncover the colonial archives, he found the void with *"surprise, mortification, and regret."* This staggering destruction means the entire history of the Manhattan purchase is suspended on a single sentence inside a 1626 letter from Pieter Schagen — a document that spends far more time inventorying dead beavers than it does explaining the real estate deal.

Which leaves one glaring question. If the sole 1626 document has no names, where did we get the identity of the tribe who sold it?

---

## ACT 5: WHO SOLD IT? [VERBATIM] (5:15-5:45)

There's a famous twist on this: the Canarsee — who lived in Brooklyn, not Manhattan — tricked the Dutch into paying for land they didn't own.

[VERBATIM]

Grumet is direct about this: "The identity of the Indians who sold the island is not known."

And the Canarsee trickster? Historian Stephen McErleane points to the most likely culprit: Nathaniel Benchley's 1959 magazine article.

[GUIDE]
- The "Manhattans" were a distinct community living on the island — Dutch director Johannes de Laet described them in 1624, and provincial secretary Isaack de Rasiere, writing a couple of years later, reported them as "about 200 to 300 strong, women and men, under different chiefs, whom they call Sackimas"
- But we don't know if they were the ones who sold it. Nobody recorded that detail

[VERBATIM — bridge to Act 6]

But even if we knew who sold it, we'd still hit a deeper problem. To understand what "purchased" even meant in 1626, we have to look at two incompatible legal systems that collided on that shoreline.

## ACT 6: WHAT "BOUGHT" MEANS [VERBATIM] (5:45-7:30)

[VERBATIM]

The Dutch and the Lenape weren't just speaking different languages. They were operating under completely different legal systems.

The Dutch held land under what's called fee simple — absolute private property. You buy it, you own it, it's yours forever. European law defined all land under Crown or corporate control as private property, and sales were final.

The Lenape held land under a different system. Anthropologist Robert Grumet, in *First Manhattans*, describes it as *"a custom called usufruct — the lawful right to something so long as it is being utilized."* Sachems and councils administered those rights on behalf of the community.

[VISUAL: On-screen definition card — "USUFRUCT: right to use land while actively using it"]

Usufruct doesn't mean land was free. It means land belonged to the community. You could use it, but you couldn't sell it away from everyone else. Shorto puts the Lenape view this way: access wasn't exclusive, and it wasn't permanent. A real estate deal, to them, was closer to a shared-use agreement — a rental with a treaty attached — than to European-style fee simple.

[BEAT]

Two completely incompatible legal systems were operating on the same beach. The Dutch believed they were acquiring exclusive property, while the Lenape believed they were negotiating a shared-use alliance.

[GUIDE — Dutch good faith as mechanical reason misunderstanding happened]
- And this wasn't because the Dutch were scheming to defraud. The West India Company's 1625 instructions explicitly ordered them to acquire land "without however forcing them thereto in the least"
- Compared to later English practice, this was formal protocol — and the Lenape weren't guileless dupes either
- As Shorto writes: "the Indians were far from guileless dupes, and in the short term, which was what mattered at the time, they got considerably more out of a simple land transaction than the amount of the purchase indicates"
- Within a few years, Lenape sachems were using European-style deeds themselves to extract military protection promises

[VERBATIM]

The Dutch weren't operating in bad faith. Two incompatible legal systems collided — and only one side walked away thinking the deal was permanent.

[GUIDE — Lenape strategic reasoning]
- And the Lenape didn't agree to share Manhattan to be friendly. They were facing devastating new diseases and attacks from rival tribes like the Mohawk
- Welcoming the Dutch was a calculated survival strategy — a way to secure powerful military allies and access to iron technology in a rapidly changing world
- Grumet has a term for this: **negative reciprocity** — "when one party accepts less for what both parties consider a more valuable commodity." He writes that the Lenape, "lacking sufficient power to completely refuse colonial demands," worked hard to make colonists promise to "protect them militarily, give them just treatment in colonial courts, and take on other obligations settlers might not otherwise have accepted" (*First Manhattans*, pp. 69–70)

[VERBATIM — CTA placement here, ~65% mark]

And we have direct testimony about this. Hitakonanu'laxk, a modern Lenape elder also known as Tree Beard, explained it in his 1994 book *The Grandfathers Speak*:

[VISUAL: Show full quote on screen]

"Land to us was like the air, sunlight and water, something that was necessary for our survival, for our very lives. Our thought was that we were giving the whites some land to live on for a while. We saw it as sharing with them, and did not consider giving them the use of the land permanently."

## WHY THE MYTH PERSISTS [VERBATIM + GUIDE] (7:30-8:30)

[VERBATIM — re-anchor after CTA]

So if the evidence is this thin, why has the story held for two hundred years? Because starting in the 1840s, a handful of writers filled in the blanks. And a city hungry for a founding story believed every word.

[VERBATIM]

O'Callaghan manufactured the $24 in 1844. That same year, at the New York Historical Society, lawyer Simeon DeWitt Bloodgood argued the purchase "places us on as high ground of justice and right in the occupation of the soil as the land of William Penn." In 1853, historian John Romeyn Brodhead extended the argument — directly comparing Manhattan to Penn's treaty at Shackamaxon. The same year, Ranney painted the beads. In 1877, Lamb put them in text. And in 1909, the Hudson-Fulton Celebration cemented the whole thing in mass culture — paper-mâché floats, over a million spectators.

[VISUAL: Timeline graphic — 1844, 1853, 1877, 1909]

[VERBATIM]

This wasn't drift. But it wasn't a conspiracy either. A few writers and painters filled in what the archive lacked — and a city that wanted the story treated their inventions as fact. The well-off New Yorkers at the NY Historical Society and the Collegiate Dutch Reformed Church — many of them descendants of the original Dutch colonists — didn't engineer the myth. They adopted it. They promoted it. They wrote it into their civic identity. They wanted an origin story they could be proud of — one as peaceful as William Penn's Pennsylvania. And they didn't want a swindle to brag about. As historian Stephen McErleane writes: *"Quite the opposite. The purchase story proved that an honest business deal had laid the foundation for their great city."* In their eagerness to prove it, they projected their own ideals backward onto a seventeenth-century shared-use agreement. It's one of the oldest mistakes in historiography — taking the laws and assumptions of your own century and applying them to a different people in a different time.

Historian Richard Howe calls the result "an imperialism of a more sophisticated sort than brute conquest or simple deceit." The myth doesn't just justify taking Manhattan. It makes taking Manhattan feel legal. Because as Howe puts it, "the Manhattan purchase was the primal Manhattan real estate transaction: from it is descended every other Manhattan property conveyance, on down to the present day."

Every property title on Manhattan traces back to this single deedless transaction.

We have twelve thousand pages of surviving Dutch colonial records — court cases, pig-stealing disputes, business failures — but the founding real estate deal of New York is documented by exactly one sentence.

They filled that void with a twenty-four-dollar receipt, a fictional chest of beads, a fabricated Canarsee trickster, and a bronze monument that dismisses its own story as a legend.

Everything else we know was built across different decades by journalists, painters, and historical societies looking for a founding story they could be proud of. A seventeenth-century shared-use agreement became a nineteenth-century bargain, and it stayed that way for almost two centuries.
