## Source A ✅ — Famine Inquiry Commission, *Report on Bengal* (Government of India, 1945), Part I

**Obtained and text-extracted 2026-07-30.** 113 pages, embedded text layer present (no OCR
required — an earlier "no text layer" conclusion was a file-path error on our side, now corrected).
**PDF:** `https://www.indiaofthepast.org/sites/indiaofthepast.org/files/pdf/1940s/bengalfamine/ficreportpart1.pdf`
⚠ Scan has OCR-era artefacts (broken ligatures, stray characters). **Every quotation below must be
eyeballed against the page image before it goes on screen**, especially digits.

### ✅ A1 — The Denial Policy: what it actually was (pdf p.31, printed Ch. V)

> *"The danger of a Japanese invasion compelled the military authorities to put into operation early
> in 1942, a 'denial' policy involving two important measures. One was the removal from the coastal
> districts of **Midnapore, Bakarganj, and Khulna** of the rice and paddy estimated to be in excess of
> local requirements until the end of the crop year, and the other was the removal of all boats
> capable of carrying 10 passengers…"*

**Bears on R1.** Denial was a **military anti-invasion measure in three named coastal districts** —
not export to Britain.

### ✅ A2 — THE KEY FINDING: denial caused hoarding, not scarcity (pdf p.32, printed p.26)

> *"There is no evidence to show that the purchases led anywhere to physical scarcity. But, on the
> other hand, they brought home to the people in the most emphatic manner, the danger of invasion;
> they increased local nervousness and probably **encouraged cultivators to hold on to their grain as
> an insurance against invasion and isolation**."*

**This is the strongest single finding so far and nobody in the competing videos uses it.** The
official commission's own judgement is that rice denial's damage was **psychological, not material** —
it triggered hoarding. That is a mechanism, not a moral claim, and it is exactly the channel's lane.

### ✅ A3 — Boat denial: scope and admitted harm (pdf p.32)

Ordered **1 May 1942**; area south of a line running Chandpur – Barisal – Khulna – Basirhat –
Diamond Harbour – Kharagpur (the Commission notes the military had originally proposed a
*larger* area). The report concedes:

> *"…the removal of a large number of boats from the delta, in which communications are almost
> entirely by river and not by rail and road, would cause considerable hardship and inconvenience."*

Relaxations followed (June 1942 inner-boat route; Nov 1942 harvest increase; Jan 1943 further; **all
restrictions removed June 1943**).

**This is the strongest showable number on the board.** It is a mechanism, not a moral claim:
66,563 boats accounted for in a delta the same page describes as communicating "almost entirely by
river and not by rail and road."

**⚠ Both sides of it are on the same page — use both.** The Commission's note: *"Owners of boats were
free either to take them into reception areas or to remove them to a place north of the 'denial'
line. Those deprived of their boats were granted compensation."* And its own hedge at para 7:
*"The measures were necessarily unpopular. From the reports of local officers it appears unlikely
that the area under cultivation was reduced. As regards the extent to which the movement of rice was
impeded, it is impossible…"*

### ✅ A4 — Provincial export bans: the "others" the title names (pdf pp.30, 35)

- **Bengal:** *"On the 1st June 1942, exports of paddy and rice to places outside the province were
  prohibited except under permit"*; and on **16 July** the Government *"prohibited all exports of rice
  and paddy from the province except under permit."*
- **Bihar:** *"decided to prohibit the exports of rice to any market outside the province by rail, road
  or river, except under permit. This prohibition took effect from the **1st July 1942**."*
- **Orissa:** *"in the same month, the Orissa Government also imposed an embargo…"*

**Directly supports R4.** Inter-provincial barriers were real, dated, and imposed by **Indian
provincial governments**. This is the documentary basis for the locked title's promise.

### ✅ A5 — The Commission's own mortality estimate and its verdict on profiteering (pdf p.89)

> *"The results were on the one hand unprecedented profiteering and the enrichment of those on the
> right side of the fence; on the other, the rise of prices to fantastic heights and the death of
> perhaps **one and a half million** people."*

Also on the same page: unusual profits on rice trading in 1943 *"reckoned"* at **150 crores**, with
the Commission's caveat *"We cannot vouch for the accuracy of this figure."*

⚠ **1.5M is the Commission's 1945 figure and sits at the bottom of the modern defensible range

estimate, not as the answer.

## Source E ✅✅ — **THE WAR CABINET MINUTE, 4 AUGUST 1943** — the killing test, answered

**Mansergh (ed.), *Constitutional Relations Between Britain and India: The Transfer of Power
1942–7*, Volume IV, "The Bengal Famine and the New Viceroyalty, 15 June 1943 – 31 August 1944"**
(HMSO, 1973). **Printed pp. 155–158.**

**⭐ The National Archives are not required.** Mansergh reproduces the War Cabinet record itself —
`W.M. (43)` conclusions are CAB 65 and `W.P. (43)` papers are CAB 66. TNA's Discovery API returned
403; this volume supplies the same documents, **free and fully public** on archive.org
(`transferofpower104nich`, 1,418 pages, unrestricted, full text layer).

**The meeting:** 10 Downing Street, **4 August 1943, 6 p.m.** — **Mr Churchill in the Chair**, with
Attlee, Eden, Lyttelton, Morrison; and for this item Amery (India), **Leathers (War Transport)**,
Woolton, Cherwell, Cripps, Grigg, Sinclair, Cranborne, Kingsley Wood.
Before them: `W.P. (43) 348` (Shipping Committee report) and `W.P. (43) 349` (Amery's memorandum).

### ✅ E1 — The ask, and the supply ceiling
India requested **500,000 tons of wheat**, September 1943 – February 1944. The Shipping Committee
found: a **maximum of 30,000 tons a month** liftable from Australia, with India competing against
Ceylon and the Middle East, plus up to **100,000 tons of Iraqi barley** —

> *"**Less than one-third, however, of the Indian demand could be met in these ways.**"*

### ✅ E2 — **THE DECISIVE LINE: the Cabinet's diagnosis**

> *"**In the view of the War Cabinet, the shortage of grain in India was not the result of physical
> deficiency but of hoarding, due to inflationary conditions.**"*

**This single sentence is the video.** London did not conclude "let them starve." It concluded the
famine was a **hoarding-and-inflation problem, not a food-availability problem** — and therefore that
shipping grain was not the answer.

Their prescribed remedies follow from that diagnosis: propaganda *"to induce hoarders to release
grain"*, and — note this — *"**It was most important to reverse the tendency which existed in one or
two provinces for those in authority to encourage peasants to hold on to their grain.**"* The Cabinet
is pointing at the provinces. Compare **A4** and **A2**.

### ✅ E3 — Leathers's advice: why the grain did not sail
> *"**the minister of war transport** reminded the War Cabinet that in January 1943 a demand had been
> made for an additional 600,000 tons of wheat, but **in the result it had not been necessary to ship
> anything approaching this quantity. He expected that much the same would happen on this occasion.**"*

He proposed *"arrangements should now be made to import **not more than 50,000 tons as a token
shipment**. This should, however, **not be earmarked for India but should be ordered to Colombo to
await instructions there**."*

**This is the mechanism.** Relief was constrained because the Minister of War Transport advised the
Cabinet that India's previous request had proved inflated and that this one would too. It is the
documentary origin of Behrens's *"demands out of all reason"* (**B2**).

### ✅ E4 — Amery objected, and lost
> *"**the secretary of state for India asked the War Cabinet not to take a firm decision to limit the
> help which would be afforded to India to the amount proposed by the Minister of War Transport.**"*
>
> *"The general view of the War Cabinet was that **no further action should be decided upon at the
> present time**…"*

**Conclusions as recorded:** (1) approval for Leathers's plan — the token 50,000 tons to Colombo;
(2) Amery to telegraph India urging action *"to combat inflation and to induce producers to part with
grain more freely"*; (3) a Standing Committee on Indian Financial Questions appointed.

### ✅ E5 — Churchill did escalate to Roosevelt
Vol IV documents Churchill's telegrams to Roosevelt on the Indian food situation (Docs. 485, 486)
and Roosevelt's reply, plus Wavell thanking Churchill for *"his generous assistance on food
imports."* Mansergh's own editorial summary describes Wavell *"pressing for the diversion of
additional food supplies to India, a man not easily to be deflected from his considered course."*
**Include this. A referee does not omit it.**

### ✅ E6 — Contemporary official testimony on provincial indifference (p.326)
> *"The fact is that **no Province in this country is moved in the slightest degree by the sufferings
> of any other Province**, and equally no community has the slightest consideration…"*

Corroborates **A4** from inside the administration.

### ✅ E7 — **24 SEPTEMBER 1943**: the pressure comes from inside, and Bengal is weighed against the Balkans
`W.M. (43) 131st Conclusions, Minute 1` — Prime Minister's Room, House of Commons, **3 p.m.**,
**Churchill in the Chair**. Printed pp. 317–319. Exhibits `TOP4-p427/428/429-warcabinet-24sep1943.png`

Present for item 1: **Wavell (Viceroy-Designate)**, Amery, Leathers, Woolton — and the **Chiefs of
Staff: Portal, Alan Brooke, Syfret**.

- **The Chiefs of Staff** endorsed the C-in-C India that it was *"of the highest importance, from the
  strategical point of view, that the Government of India should receive all possible aid,"* warning
  that *"the efficient prosecution of the war against Japan by forces based on India would be gravely
  jeopardised."*
- **Amery:** *"famine conditions were developing in the industrial areas in India, particularly in
  Bengal"* — and pressed for a firm assurance of **500,000 tons before March 1944**.
- **Wavell** supported him, recalling that as C-in-C he had made *"the strongest representations"*;
  Indian Army rations had been cut and *"the Army's food reserves had at one time fallen to a very
  low level."*
- **Leathers** offered a route to 210,000 tons — but only by taking grain from stocks *"being built
  up in the Middle East with a view to supplying the irregular forces operating against the enemy in
  the **Balkans**."*

**The explicit trade-off, minuted:** *"attention was drawn to the disadvantages of diminishing the
stocks which were being accumulated for possible use in the Balkans… It was felt, however, that **not
more than a maximum of 50,000 tons should be withdrawn** from these stocks."*

**Conclusion:** aim at **200,000 tons by end-1943** (the 150,000 already authorised + 50,000 from the
Balkan stocks); review at year end; Amery's request for a Q1-1944 assurance **refused**.

### ⭐⭐ E8 — **10 NOVEMBER 1943: THE CANADIAN OFFER** — the strongest prosecution exhibit in the project
`W.M. (43) 152nd Conclusions, Minute 2` — 10 Downing Street, **6 p.m.**, **Churchill in the Chair**,
with Attlee, Eden, Anderson, Bevin, Lyttelton, Morrison and **Smuts**; for item 2 Beaverbrook, Amery,
Cripps, Leathers, Woolton, Cherwell. Printed pp. 465–466.
Exhibits `TOP4-p575/576/577-warcabinet-10nov1943-canada.png`

> *"the secretary of state for India said that he had received that day a telegram indicating that the
> **Canadian Government could make available one 10,000-ton Canadian ship** for carrying to India part
> of the **Canadian offer of 100,000 tons of wheat**. The War Cabinet were informed that, if this
> Canadian ship were made available for this purpose, it would be withdrawn from the pool of shipping
> available for Anglo-American war purposes. In these circumstances the War Cabinet decided… **they
> must press the Canadian Government not to use this ship for a purpose which they regarded as wholly
> uneconomic from the shipping point of view.**"*

**Recorded conclusion (3):** *"**Invited the Prime Minister to send a personal message to the Prime
Minister of Canada deprecating the proposed allocation of a Canadian ship** to carry to India part of
the Canadian offer of 100,000 tons of wheat."*

**This is the true version of R3.** Not an Australian ship turned around — **a Canadian offer of
100,000 tons of wheat, with a Canadian ship to carry it, which the War Cabinet resolved to discourage,
by a personal message from Churchill, in November 1943, at the height of the mortality.**

Also at that meeting: Amery reported the Government of India's view that the minimum needed was
*"an import of 50,000 tons of food grains per month… maintained for a year."* The Cabinet granted
**100,000 tons over January–February 1944** and specified this *"did not imply any promise to
undertake a long-term programme."*

### ✅ E9 — **THE PRECEDENT TESTED**: Leathers was factually right and analytically wrong
**Mansergh (ed.), *Transfer of Power*, Vol. III, "Reassertion of Authority, Gandhi's Fast and the
Succession to the Viceroyalty, 21 September 1942 – 12 June 1943"** — `constitutionalre03nich`, free.

On 4 August 1943 Leathers justified restraint by saying an earlier 600,000-ton demand *"had not been
necessary"* (**E3**). That was the load-bearing exculpation. Vol. III contains the original.

**9 December 1942** — Government of India requests an **additional 600,000 tons of wheat** (Doc. 265).
**10 December** — Linlithgow to Amery: *"**Wavell concurs in this request.** Situation is a serious one
and I hope you will be able to help us."* (Doc. 266). The Council, with the Commander-in-Chief
present, *"unanimously agreed that increased imports of wheat were urgently needed."*

Amery's memorandum, *"The Indian Demand for Imported Food Grains"* (Doc. 330), records that he warned
Delhi of the shipping difficulty and *"notwithstanding the terms of this telegram **the Viceroy has
come back with a renewed statement of India's needs, unreduced in quantity**."*

**The verdict on E3:** Leathers's precedent was **true**. The December 1942 request was not met, and
by mid-1943 — as Amery himself told the Cabinet on 4 August — *"the harvest had proved better than
anticipated and it had been found possible to discontinue the import of grain."*

**But the analogy was false.** The 1942 reprieve came from a good harvest. By August 1943 the October
1942 cyclone had destroyed the winter crop and Burma was gone. **He was pattern-matching to a real
precedent that no longer applied.** That is the honest characterisation: not callousness, not
deceit — a defensible inference that was catastrophically wrong, made about a situation that had
changed underneath it.

### ⚠ E10 — Delhi supplied the "psychological" framing the Cabinet later used
The Government of India's own December 1942 telegram argued the wheat should arrive in a large
initial consignment because *"**the psychological effect in releasing stocks in India would be
enhanced**"* — i.e. Delhi itself framed imports partly as a device to break hoarding.

**This matters for fairness.** The War Cabinet's August 1943 diagnosis — *"not the result of physical
deficiency but of hoarding"* (**E2**) — did not appear from nowhere. Delhi had been making a version
of the hoarding argument in its own requests. The Cabinet took a real feature of the crisis and
made it the whole explanation.

⚠ Also in Vol. III: India **was** exporting foodgrains in late 1942 (to war theatres, not Britain),
which *"has already excited considerable public criticism."* Relevant to **R1** — the exports were
real but small, wartime, and regional. Read Doc. 243 in full before using.

## ✅ THE HINGE — **RESOLVED FROM THE PRIMARY RECORD, 2026-07-30**

**Was there actually a food shortage in Bengal in 1943? The Famine Inquiry Commission answered it
with a table, and the answer is "both sides are partly right."**

### ✅ F1 — The supply table (Part II, Appendix II, printed p.209)
Exhibit: `FIC2-p101-supply-table-1943-vs-1941.png`

The Commission expresses "current supply" as **weeks of the province's requirements**:

| Year | **1943** | **1941** | 1936 | 1928 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Current supply, in weeks of requirement | **43** | **39** | 44 | 45 |

> *"1943 was a lean year, comparable with the three years 1941, 1936 and 1928… The shortage of current
> supply was a little more serious during 1943 than in 1936 and 1928, **but less serious than in
> 1941**."*

Driver: the aman crop reaped end-1942 yielded only **29 weeks** against an average of **38** — a
9-week shortfall — partly offset by better boro and aus crops. *"External supply was about the same
during 1943 as compared with the average."*

**1941 had a worse supply position than 1943. 1941 produced no famine.**

### ✅ F2 — Why the earlier shortfalls killed nobody (Part I, printed p.13)
Exhibit: `FIC-p19-purchasing-power-no-starvation.png`

> *"In the course of the 15 years preceding 1943, there were 3 years (1928, 1936 and 1941) in which the
> supply obtained from the aman crop… was seriously short because of the partial failure of that crop
> from natural causes. During these years, distress prevailed… and relief measures on a considerable
> scale had to be organized. **When, however, purchasing power was provided by these measures, the
> necessary supplies became available for purchase, and no deaths from starvation occurred.**"*

And, on the same page: *"in normal years a section of the population suffers from insufficiency of
food… this is due to **lack of purchasing power rather than an over-all shortage of supplies**."*

**This is Sen's entitlement thesis, stated by the Commission in 1945 — thirty-six years before
*Poverty and Famines*.** You do not need Sen to establish it; you need Sen only to name it.

### ✅ F3 — But the crop failure was real (Part I)
The October 1942 cyclone affected **3,200 square miles**, of which **450** were swept by tidal waves
and **400** flooded; the standing aman crop, then flowering, was *"in large measure damaged"*, and in
the worst areas *"reserve stocks of the previous crop in the hands of cultivators, consumers, and
dealers were also lost."* Then:

> *"**After the cyclone came crop disease.** We have been told by several witnesses about the damage
> caused by fungus and 'root-rot'. The Government of Bengal have stated that their effects were **even
> more serious to the outturn** than the damage [from the cyclone]."*

### 🔨 What this settles

| Camp | Right about | Wrong about |
|---|---|---|
| **Food-availability-decline** (Bowbrick, Tauger) | There *was* a genuine crop failure — cyclone plus fungus, aman down 9 weeks | It cannot explain the famine: **1941 was worse and nobody starved** |
| **Sen / entitlement** | The distinguishing factor in 1943 was purchasing power, not tonnage | Understates the real supply shock, which the Commission does document |
| **The War Cabinet** (E2) | *"Not physical deficiency"* was **broadly right** | Drew the wrong conclusion from it — the 1928/1936/1941 precedent showed the remedy was **to supply purchasing power and grain**, which they declined |

**The Cabinet's diagnosis was closer to correct than the popular prosecution allows — and that makes
the failure worse, not better.** They identified that this was not a simple tonnage problem, and then
used that insight as a reason to do nothing, when their own administration's record showed exactly
what stops a shortfall becoming a famine.

**This is the video's verdict, and every element of it is now sourced to a primary document.**

## 🚩 THE ORIGINAL FRAMING OF THE HINGE (superseded by F1–F3 above, retained for the record)

**Was there actually a food shortage in Bengal in 1943?**

Everything above documents *what the War Cabinet decided and why*. It does **not** establish whether
their central factual premise was true. That premise (**E2**) was:

> *"the shortage of grain in India was not the result of physical deficiency but of hoarding, due to
> inflationary conditions."*

**Amartya Sen's entitlement thesis says substantially the same thing** — the 1943 famine occurred
without a large decline in food availability; purchasing power collapsed while food existed. Against
him, a food-availability-decline school (Bowbrick; Tauger; Goswami) argues the October 1942 cyclone
and the brown-spot fungus produced a genuine harvest failure.

**The two outcomes are different videos:**

| If… | Then the Cabinet… | And the verdict is… |
|---|---|---|
| **Sen is right** (no real FAD) | **diagnosed it correctly** | the failure was the *remedy* — they saw the mechanism and did nothing that worked. Leathers's mis-analogy (**E9**) becomes the decisive error |
| **The FAD school is right** | **was wrong on the facts** | grain shipments *were* the answer; refusing them was negligent, and the "hoarding" framing (partly supplied by Delhi itself — **E10**) was a comfortable misreading |

Both are damning. They are damning in incompatible ways, and they need different exhibits, a
different closing, and a different title emphasis.

**Do not script until this is resolved.** Sources: Sen (free, `povertyfamineses0000sena`), Bowbrick
(*Food Policy*, 1986), Tauger (*J. Peasant Studies*, 2003) — see `SOURCES.md` Tier 1. Cross-check
against FIC Part I on the 1942 crop and Afzal Husain's minute (**D5**), both already held.

### 🔨 THE VERDICT THESE DOCUMENTS SUPPORT

**Not "Churchill deliberately starved Bengal." Not "Churchill was blameless."**

On **4 August 1943**, with Churchill in the chair, the War Cabinet decided the Bengal shortage was
**hoarding, not scarcity**; accepted its transport minister's judgement that India's numbers were
inflated; approved a **token 50,000 tons sent to Colombo rather than earmarked for India**; overruled
the Secretary of State for India; and resolved that **"no further action should be decided upon at
the present time."**

They were wrong. About 1.5 million people died (**D1**).

The diagnosis was not baseless — hoarding was real, and the Commission found the Army's own denial
policy had helped trigger it (**A2**). And the shipping was genuinely tight (**E1**, **B2**). But the
call was made, it is minuted, it is attributable, and it was made over the objection of the one
minister responsible for India.

**That is the honest charge, and it is now proved from the Cabinet's own record.**

**And it got worse as the famine peaked.** By late September the Chiefs of Staff, the Commander-in-
Chief India, the incoming Viceroy and the Secretary of State were all pressing the same case, and
the Cabinet still capped the diversion at 50,000 tons because the competing claim was partisan
supply in the Balkans (**E7**). In November, with Canada offering 100,000 tons of wheat *and a ship*,
the Cabinet resolved to press Canada not to sail it, and asked Churchill to say so personally
(**E8**).

**The strongest single sentence for the video is the Cabinet's own diagnosis (E2), and the strongest
single act is the Canadian message (E8).** Neither requires exaggeration, and both are minuted.

⚠ **The referee's obligation:** show the other half too — the shipping genuinely was scarce (B2, E1),
Leathers's advice was based on a real precedent (E3), Churchill did telegraph Roosevelt for help
(E5), Wavell pressed hard and was described as *"not easily to be deflected"* (E5), and the Cabinet
did progressively increase the allocation across the three meetings. A prosecution edit that omits
those is the thing this channel exists not to be.

### ✅ C2 — R5, second half: the Governor kept reserve powers
**"Power of Governor to issue Proclamations"** (printed p. 630; pdf p. 62). The Governor may suspend

> *"The functions of the Governor under this section shall be exercised by him **in his discretion**

**Verdict on R5: partly true, fundamentally misleading — confirmed from the statute.** Bengal's

sovereign: the Governor held special responsibilities and a discretionary power to take the
administration over, subject only to the Governor-General.

## The verdict these documents support

Not "Churchill starved Bengal," and not "Churchill was blameless." The record so far shows:

1. **The food did not leave for Britain** — Bengal was a net rice importer, and the province had
   banned exports by mid-1942 (A8, A4).
2. **Indian provincial governments sealed the borders** — Bihar 1 July 1942, Orissa the same month,
   under powers that were genuinely theirs (A4, C1).
3. **The Army's denial policy did not remove the food, but it broke the transport and triggered
   hoarding** — 66,563 boats accounted for, 3,373 destroyed, and the Commission's own finding of
   "no evidence… of physical scarcity" but increased "nervousness" (A2, A3b).
4. **London delayed and constrained relief, in a genuinely tight shipping year** — 58,000 tons
   delivered against 600,000 requested, on the same page as "demands out of all reason" (B1, B2).
5. **And nobody in London understood the Indian rice economy in the first place** (B3, July 1941).

Three governments, one army, and a market. That is the video, and every step of it is a document.
