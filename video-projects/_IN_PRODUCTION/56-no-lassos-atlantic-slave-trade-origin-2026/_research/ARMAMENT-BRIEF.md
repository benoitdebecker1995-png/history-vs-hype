### Claim 4 — "They Were Already Slaves" (The Pre-Existing Status Framing)

**Asmongold's verbatim:**
> "They were already slaves to begin with. They were slaves before they even made it to America."

**Why it lands:** For a portion of the people sold into the Atlantic trade, this was technically accurate. African societies did practice slavery, and some people sold to European traders were already enslaved within African social structures.

**Why it fails:** Two-part failure. First, factual: Afonso's 1526 letter documents European traders seizing *free people* — not pre-existing slaves — including his noblemen. Zurara's 1444 account documents the capture of free coastal peoples in direct Portuguese raids. The "already slaves" claim cannot account for these documented cases. Second, categorical: African slavery and Atlantic chattel slavery were categorically different institutions. Kopytoff and Miers (*Slavery in Africa*, 1977) document that African slavery preserved assimilation pathways, partial human rights, and paths to freedom through integration. Atlantic chattel slavery — derived from Roman law codified in the *Las Siete Partidas* and applied via the *Romanus Pontifex* — classified people as permanent, inheritable, transferable property with no legal personhood. The *Romanus Pontifex* (1455) grants the right to "reduce their persons to perpetual servitude" — the Latin *in perpetuam servitutem redigendi*. "Perpetual" is doing load-bearing work. African slavery was not perpetual in the same sense.

## Section 3 — The Rhetorical Pattern (Meta-Armament)

**The tactic's name:** **Anecdotal Generalization with Blame Inversion**

This is a three-step move Asmongold runs twice in the clip (once on the Lagos comment, once in the general "no lasso" framing):

**Step 1 — Anecdote as evidence:**
He reads a single comment about one specific historical figure (the Oba of Lagos) and immediately treats it as confirmation of a continent-wide general principle ("black people sold them to us"). The sample size is 1. The generalization is 400 million people across 366 years.

*Clip verbatim illustrating Step 1:*
> "So he literally was selling slaves. All right, well, there we go. Great, exactly."

The "there we go" construction — as if one anecdote closes the historical question — is the tell.

**Step 2 — Blame inversion:**
He reframes the victims of a coercive system as the system's architects. The Oba who ceded Lagos "under threat of military bombardment" becomes "a slave trafficker." The descendants become complicit. The buyers disappear from the frame.

*Clip verbatim illustrating Step 2:*
> "She's a slave trafficker? Yeah, definitely."

**Step 3 — "We didn't do X, we did Y" as exoneration:**
He substitutes a technically-true narrower claim ("we didn't use lassos") for the broader question ("did Europe build and sustain the Atlantic slave trade?"). If the narrow claim is accepted, the broader question appears answered.

*Clip verbatim illustrating Step 3:*
> "Europeans and Americans didn't go over to Africa with a lasso and start wrangling up random black people. We didn't do that. We went over there and other black people sold them to us."

**How to identify it in real time — equip the viewer:**

> "He's doing Anecdotal Generalization with Blame Inversion. Watch for: (1) One anecdote → 'there we go' → continent-wide claim. (2) The buyer disappears from the frame, only the seller remains. (3) A narrow true claim stands in for a broader false conclusion."

**The structural counter — what to ask back:**

When you see this pattern, ask three questions:
1. **"What's the sample size?"** — One Oba is not "black people sold them to us." 365 years of trade records exist.
2. **"Where's the buyer?"** — Any account of "Africans sold slaves" that doesn't ask "who bought them, at what price, with what incentive structure" is half the argument.
3. **"True for which period?"** — The "no lasso" claim is defensible for 1700. It is not defensible for 1441. The claim collapses time.
