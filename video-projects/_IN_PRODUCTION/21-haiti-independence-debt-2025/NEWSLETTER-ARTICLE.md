# Haiti Paid for Its Own Freedom

*The enslaved didn't just win independence. They were invoiced for it.*

---

In 1947, the Republic of Haiti made its final debt payment to a bank in New York. The same year, prosecutors at Nuremberg sentenced Nazi war criminals for, among other things, the use of slave labor. In one courtroom, the civilized world declared that forcing human beings to work without pay was a crime against humanity. Across the Atlantic, a Black republic was still settling the bill for having freed itself from slavery 143 years earlier.

Haiti is the only country in history where the formerly enslaved paid their former enslavers for their own freedom. Not symbolically. In cash. Over 122 years. The total: roughly $560 million in today's dollars. The profits helped finance the Eiffel Tower, the Suez Canal, and railroads on three continents.

**Why it matters:** Most people explain Haiti's poverty through corruption and bad governance. The evidence shows the country spent more than a century losing capital to France and then to American banks, not because it owed a debt, but because it was charged for the crime of winning a revolution.

I've made videos about how colonial legal frameworks outlive the empires that created them. Haiti is the clearest case I've found. The math is not ambiguous.

---

## The Man Who Saw It Coming

On August 30th, 1822, a darker-skinned Haitian named Félix Darfour walked into the Chamber of Deputies in Port-au-Prince and presented a petition. He accused President Jean-Pierre Boyer of having "vendu le pays aux blancs" (sold the country to the whites).

He was arrested that afternoon. Put in dungeons. In irons.

Boyer ordered a civilian tried by military commission. The trial began and ended the evening of August 31st. The historian Beaubrun Ardouin recorded what happened:

> "The accused defended himself; he showed a great deal of courage and did not seek to present any excuse."
>
> — Ardouin, *Études sur l'histoire d'Haïti*, Tome 9, p. 190 (trans. Daut, p. 161)

Darfour was shot Monday morning, September 2nd, 1822, before the garrison troops and a watching crowd. His petition was destroyed. Ardouin notes that "indignation caused it to disappear" (trans. Daut, p. 161).

Three years later, in 1825, every word of Darfour's accusation came true. Boyer sold the country.

The payment was blood first. Then cash.

## Fourteen Warships and a Velvet Case

On April 17th, 1825, King Charles X of France issued a royal ordinance demanding 150 million francs from Haiti. The price of recognizing an independence Haiti had already won by force. The sum rested on a legal fiction: that a human being is a "biens immeubles" (immovable property, like a building) that can be stolen. France did not just demand cash. It demanded that Haiti legitimize the math of slavery to buy its way out. Some 25,838 colonists filed claims for what they had "lost."

The demand arrived at gunpoint: 14 warships, 528 cannons. Baron de Mackau's sealed orders left no room:

> "My mission is not a negotiation... I lack the powers to make the slightest modification."
>
> — Baron de Mackau, 1825 (trans. Daut, p. 290)

The 150 million was a discount. The full valuation was 1.5 billion francs. Haiti got 10% off.

Boyer's own commission rejected the terms "immediately and categorically." But Boyer convened a secret council and never showed them the French document. He presented three leading questions he had written himself. Ardouin records that the council acted under pressure, believing the terms resembled earlier negotiations.

Boyer accepted on July 11th. The ordinance was locked in a velvet case. Ordinary Haitians learned the terms months later, from French newspapers.

## A Short Trip Across Paris

**By the numbers:**

Haiti could not pay 150 million francs. So it borrowed the first installment of 30 million from a French bank, Ternaux Grandolphe. After fees, Haiti received only 24 million. It owed back 30 million. A double debt.

The legal researcher Anthony Phillips traced what happened to the money:

> "The remaining 24,000,000 francs made its way from the bank's vaults to the French treasury, a short trip across Paris that began Haiti's long spiral into impoverishment."
>
> — Anthony Phillips, "Haiti, France and the Independence Debt of 1825," p. 1007

The money never left the city. Haiti borrowed from France to pay France.

From there, the profits multiplied. The Crédit Industriel et Commercial cleared returns of 15% per year (some years 24%) while helping finance the Eiffel Tower. The New York Times found that CIC's profits from Haiti "sometimes exceeded Haiti's entire public works budget."

Piketty calculated that French foreign holdings, including Haitian payments, funded the Suez Canal, the Panama Canal, and railroads on three continents.

Haiti paid for French industrialization. The bill consumed 50% of its national budget by 1898. By 1914, it consumed 80%.

Other empires abolished slavery too. They did it differently.

## The Comparison Nobody Makes

In 1833, Britain abolished slavery across its empire. British taxpayers paid slaveholders £20 million in compensation. The enslaved paid nothing. France took the opposite approach: in Haiti, the enslaved paid their enslavers directly. One country asked its citizens to absorb the cost of ending an injustice. The other sent warships and charged the victims. The New York Times put it plainly: "Haiti became the world's only country where the descendants of enslaved people paid reparations to the descendants of their masters."

Two abolitions. One bill.

France eventually stepped back from Haiti. The debt did not disappear. It changed hands.

## The American Robbery

By December 1914, the United States replaced France as Haiti's creditor. Marines from the USS Machias walked into the Banque Nationale d'Haïti, removed $500,000 in gold reserves, and shipped them to New York. The historian Laurent Dubois calls it "an international armed robbery."

The robbery had an inside man. Roger Farnham was simultaneously vice president of National City Bank in New York and vice president of the Banque Nationale d'Haïti. The gold seizure was a transfer between his own two institutions.

Farnham told Secretary of State William Jennings Bryan that Haiti's system "constitutes a certain form of slavery for the masses." His proposed solution: American financial control. By 1922, National City Bank had acquired all outstanding French debts on Haiti.

Marine General Smedley Butler later confessed the arrangement openly:

> "I helped make Haiti and Cuba a decent place for the National City Bank boys to collect revenues in."

The creditor changed. The debt did not.

## The Last Payment

In 1947, Haitian President Dumarsais Estimé sought what he called "financial emancipation." He pleaded with the United States to forgive the remaining debt. Washington refused.

Estimé floated an internal loan denominated in US dollars to clear the external balance. The final remittance went to National City Bank, by then renamed Citibank. Anthony Phillips wrote:

> "In the same year the Nazis paid for their crimes, including slavery, at Nuremberg, Haiti still labored to repay in cash the freedom its founding fathers had won with their lives."

The result: for the first time in nearly 70 years, the Haitian government regained the power to set its own fiscal policy. The American financial adviser withdrew. The national bank was Haitianized.

Freedom, finally purchased in full. 122 years after the invoice arrived.

But paying off the debt did not undo what 122 years of bleeding had done to the country.

---

## The Steelman

Haiti's problems did not end in 1947. The Duvalier dictatorships (1957 to 1986) stole an estimated $1.65 billion, roughly three times what France extracted directly. The economist Mats Lundahl assessed the colonial debt as "heavy" but not the "main cause" of Haiti's poverty.

He is not wrong. Haiti's problems have more causes than any single explanation can cover.

But Lundahl does not dispute that the debt drained capital for 122 years. No country can lose 80% of its budget to foreign creditors for generations and emerge with functioning infrastructure. The Duvaliers inherited a state that had been hollowed out before they ever touched it.

## The Claim That Disappeared

In 2003, President Jean-Bertrand Aristide demanded $21.68 billion in restitution from France. France responded: "This case has been closed since 1885."

Aristide was overthrown in February 2004. The new government dropped the reparations claim within a week.

In 2015, President François Hollande visited Haiti and acknowledged a "moral debt." Financial reparations, he added, were "out of the question."

France's own lawyers had already answered the legal question. The Dalloz Report of 1829 concluded that Charles X's ordinance was unlawful. France knew. The charge stood anyway.

The pattern was visible from the very first day.

## The Toast

At the 1825 banquet, after Boyer signed the ordinance, Haitian Senator Rouanez toasted the Abbé Grégoire, a French bishop who had defended Haiti's independence.

The French officers set down their glasses with "visible disapproval."

The Haitians drank with "noisy enthusiasm."
