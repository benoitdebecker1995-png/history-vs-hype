## What NO Competitor Covers (Our Unique Angles)

### 1. The Source Comparison Problem
**No YouTube video has ever placed Herodotus, Diodorus, and Ctesias side by side and shown where they contradict.**

The specific contradictions that exist in the primary texts but appear in zero competitor videos:

- **Persian army size:** Herodotus says 1.7 million (Histories 7.186). Ctesias says 800,000 (*Persica* via Photius). Diodorus says approximately 250,000 (drawing on Ephorus). Modern scholars: 120,000–300,000. The sources don't agree on order of magnitude.

- **The night attack:** Diodorus/Ephorus describe a Greek night raid on the Persian camp before the final day. Herodotus says no such thing. Modern historians call Diodorus's night attack "almost certainly fictional" — but competitors never tell viewers this debate exists.

- **How many men stayed for the last stand:** Herodotus: ~1,400 (300 Spartans + 700 Thespians + 400 Thebans). Diodorus: 500 (300 Spartans + 200 Thespians). Ctesias: gives no usable number on this. The famous "300" figure obscures that even the ancient sources can't agree on how many died.

- **Who betrayed the pass:** Herodotus names Ephialtes, a local Malian. Diodorus/Ephorus says "a Trachinian peasant." Ctesias says Xerxes sent 40,000 men over the path, guided by "the two leading men of Trachis." Three different accounts of the betrayal that made the whole battle possible.

- **Ctesias at Plataea:** Ctesias appears to place 300 Spartans at Plataea rather than Thermopylae — a detail so confusing that scholars call his epitome (preserved by Photius) "almost worse than useless."

- **The Spartan total contingent:** Herodotus: 300 full Spartiates. Diodorus: Leonidas "announced that only a thousand were to join him" (implying 300 Spartiates + 700 perioikoi). The "300" number is not even agreed upon between ancient sources.

### 2. Why No Persian Source Survives
**Zero competitors raise this.** We have only Greek accounts of a Greek victory. The Daiva Inscription of Xerxes exists but says nothing about Thermopylae. No Persian narrative of the battle has ever been found. What does it mean to "know" what happened at Thermopylae when we only have the victors' accounts? This is the deepest question in the whole topic and nobody asks it.

### 3. Who Was Herodotus Actually Writing For — and When
Herodotus finished the *Histories* approximately 440 BCE — 40 years after the battle. He had no contemporary written sources. He interviewed people who might have known people who were there. Competitors cite him as if he were a reporter on the scene. The 40-year gap is critical and never explained.

### 4. The "Correctionist" Payoff
Every viewer who knows anything about Thermopylae has been told one version. The moment they learn that ancient sources disagree on the number "300" — the single most famous number in the whole story — that's the "wait, everything I knew was wrong" hook. No competitor delivers this because no competitor has read the sources.

### 5. Ctesias as the "Persian-Adjacent" Voice
Ctesias served as physician to the Persian royal court. He had access the Greeks didn't. His account is fragmentary and problematic — but the *reason* it's problematic (preserved only through Photius, an 8th-century Byzantine patriarch who condensed it) is itself a story about how history is transmitted and lost. This is entirely absent from the YouTube landscape.

### 6. Teaching Source Criticism as the Skill
The meta-lesson — "this is how historians actually think about ancient sources" — exists nowhere in the high-view Thermopylae video space. Competitors are entertainment. We can be education.

## What Competitors Get Wrong or Oversimplify

### The singular "Herodotus says" construction
Every competitor treats Herodotus as a single reliable channel to the past. The phrase "Herodotus writes that..." is used to end debates, not start them. The History Channel special uses it nine times as an authority stamp. No competitor ever says "Herodotus claims, but..." or "Herodotus was writing 40 years later with no written records."

### The "300" number is presented as fact
Competitors state "300 Spartans" as settled history. In reality: Herodotus says 300 Spartiates; Diodorus implies the total Spartan contingent was 1,000; and Ctesias provides numbers so confused they're not usable. The single most famous number in the story is contested between the ancient sources, and no competitor tells viewers this.

### The "democracy was saved" teleology
The History Channel special ends with: "If the Persians had been victorious, democracy would have been stopped in its tracks." This is presented as fact, not interpretation. The causal chain (Thermopylae → democracy survives → Western civilization) is standard but unprovable. It also erases the Persian perspective — Xerxes' empire was tolerant by ancient standards, allowing conquered peoples to govern themselves and practice their own religion (the special even says this, then ignores it for the final framing).

### Ephialtes is treated as settled
Every competitor names Ephialtes as the betrayer and treats it as fact. Herodotus names him; Diodorus/Ephorus describes "a Trachinian peasant"; Ctesias says Xerxes was guided by "the two leading men of Trachis." The identity of the betrayer is not settled in the ancient sources. No competitor says this.

### No one explains the transmission chain
Diodorus Siculus wrote 400 years after the battle. He drew on Ephorus, who wrote 100 years after the battle, who drew on now-lost sources. Ctesias's account exists only as a Byzantine summary of a 5th-century text. Competitors treat all sources as equally "ancient" without acknowledging what that means for reliability.

## Agent Assessment

### What ALL competitors get wrong or oversimplify

The uniform error across all competitors is **treating Herodotus as a stenographer of events rather than an author with biases, limitations, and a 40-year gap from the battle.** This is not a subtle academic critique — it's the first thing any classics scholar would say about the sources. Yet in 3+ hours of combined competitor content, it appears nowhere.

The deeper error: competitors use Herodotus when it serves the story and ignore him when he gives inconvenient details (such as the enormous Greek force of 7,000 total, not 300). They do not acknowledge that Diodorus exists and disagrees. They have never heard of Ctesias. The result is a YouTube Thermopylae that is not history — it is the pop culture version with occasional Herodotus quotes for decoration.

It is not wrong — it does reflect the broad outline of events. But it treats the *narrative* of Herodotus as *the history* of Thermopylae, without distinguishing between the two.

Our video is the only one that treats the narrative as *evidence to be examined*, not a story to be told.
