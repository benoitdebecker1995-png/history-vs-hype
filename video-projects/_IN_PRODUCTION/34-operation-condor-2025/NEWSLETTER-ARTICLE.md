# They Kept Receipts for Their Murders

*Six dictatorships built a continental assassination network. Then a schoolteacher found the filing system.*

---

Six South American dictatorships coordinated assassinations across international borders for years, and the whole thing was dismissed as paranoid fantasy. Leftist propaganda. Conspiracy theory. Then, in December 1992, a former schoolteacher who had been tortured for four years walked into a police station basement in Paraguay and found 593,000 pages of documentation. Not rumors. Not testimony. The operational paperwork of a continental killing machine, signed and filed by the men who ran it.

The network was called Operation Condor. The paperwork included its founding charter.

**Why it matters:** The largest coordinated state terror campaign in the Western Hemisphere was proved not by a whistleblower, not by a journalist, not by the CIA releasing files. It was proved because the perpetrators kept their own records, and one of their victims went looking for them.

I knew about Operation Condor before I started this research. I knew the broad outline: dictatorships, CIA involvement, disappearances. But reading the primary documents changed something for me. These weren't shadowy operations run through deniable cutouts. They had country codes. Condor 1 for Argentina, Condor 2 for Bolivia, Condor 3 for Chile, Condor 4 for Paraguay, Condor 5 for Uruguay. They used encrypted telex machines. They filed paperwork on prisoner transfers the way a shipping company files invoices.

---

## The Schoolteacher and the Policeman's Son

Martin Almada was a teacher. In 1974, the Stroessner dictatorship in Paraguay arrested him for "intellectual terrorism." They held him for nearly four years.

While he was in prison, police called his wife. They told her he was dead. Then they played recordings of him being tortured over the phone. She died from the psychological stress.

After the dictatorship fell in 1989, Almada returned to Paraguay with one goal: find his file. Understand what had been done to his family and why.

The tip came from an unlikely place. A former policeman wanted a job for his son. In exchange, he drew Almada a map. Documents that were supposed to have been destroyed had been moved to a police station in Lambare, a suburb of Asuncion.

Almada took the map to Judge Jose Agustin Fernandez, who issued a court order. They went to the station with a television crew. The police tried to block them. Officers insisted the records had been destroyed. The judge threatened to jail them all for contempt. They found a padlocked door. Someone produced a crowbar. The lock broke.

What they found inside was not just Almada's file. It was rooms stacked floor to ceiling with paper. Three to four tons. The bureaucratic memory of a dictatorship that believed it would never be held accountable.

Almada named it the Archives of Terror.

This is a pattern I keep running into. When I covered the Berlin Conference of 1884 and how European powers carved up Africa, the same thing came through: bureaucracies don't destroy records as often as you'd think. They move them, reclassify them, forget about them. The instinct to file things is sometimes stronger than the instinct to cover your tracks.

## The Charter Nobody Was Supposed to Find

Mixed in with interrogation reports and prisoner files, the Archives contained the founding charter of Operation Condor itself.

Signed November 28th, 1975. Five intelligence chiefs from Argentina, Bolivia, Chile, Paraguay, and Uruguay. Brazil joined six months later. The meeting had taken place at the Army War Academy in Santiago, Chile. Manuel Contreras, the head of Chile's secret police (the DINA), had sent the invitation letter.

The founders described what they were building in their own language:

> "Something similar to Interpol, but dedicated to subversion."

In practice, this meant a continental assassination network organized into three phases. Phase I was intelligence sharing: surveillance, data exchange, tracking dissidents across borders. Phase II was covert operations within South America: cross-border kidnappings, torture, secret prisoner transfers. Phase III, codenamed Teseo, was international assassination. Special teams sent anywhere in the world, including Europe and North America.

The documents showed prisoner exchanges between countries. Files explicitly labeled for distribution to "Condor." Coordinated kidnappings with the bureaucratic precision of intergovernmental paperwork.

And the United States knew. Declassified CIA cables show American intelligence tracked Condor from the beginning and quietly supported the regimes running it. The CIA's own Hinchey Report, released in 2000, confirmed that "within a year after the coup, the CIA and other U.S. Government agencies were aware of bilateral cooperation among regional intelligence services to track the activities of and, in at least a few cases, kill political opponents."

On top of that, Contreras himself, the man who organized all of this, was a paid CIA asset from 1974 to 1977.

## Five Days

We have to keep in mind that historical evidence is rarely as clean as it looks after the fact. Scholars still debate whether specific warnings could have changed specific outcomes. But one sequence is hard to look away from.

On August 23rd, 1976, the U.S. State Department drafted a formal warning to South American governments about Condor's assassination plans. The language was direct. An internal report from the same month stated plainly:

> "The military regimes of the southern cone of South America see themselves as embattled... they have established Operation Condor to find and kill terrorists... in their own countries and in Europe."

On September 16th, 1976, Henry Kissinger, traveling in Lusaka, Zambia, canceled the warning. The cable reads:

> "SECRETARY DECLINED TO APPROVE MESSAGE TO MONTEVIDEO AND HAS INSTRUCTED THAT NO FURTHER ACTION BE TAKEN ON THIS MATTER."

Five days later, on September 21st, a remote-controlled car bomb detonated at Sheridan Circle in Washington, D.C. Orlando Letelier, a former Chilean diplomat and prominent critic of Pinochet, was killed. Ronni Moffitt, a 25-year-old American human rights worker, died beside him. Shrapnel sliced her carotid artery. She drowned in her own blood before the ambulance arrived.

The historian John Dinges argues the warning "probably would have prevented the assassination," since DINA would have called off the mission if the U.S. had expressed displeasure. A former State Department official, Harry Shlaudeman, disagreed: the operatives would have gone ahead regardless. The evidence supports Dinges more than Shlaudeman, but we cannot know for certain. What we do know is the warning was written, it was canceled, and five days later the bomb went off on American soil.

If you've followed my videos on how colonial legal frameworks outlive the empires that created them (the Haiti debt, the Tordesillas line in Brazil), the Condor story follows a different but equally stubborn pattern: Cold War security frameworks outliving the Cold War itself. The amnesty laws these dictatorships wrote to protect themselves took decades to overturn.

## The Reckoning That Took Thirty Years

Once those files were public, the denials stopped working.

Paraguay had no amnesty laws. Pastor Coronel, the torturer who ran the archive, went to prison. But the archive's evidence reached three continents, and the legal battles in other countries took far longer.

Chile had passed Decree Law 2191 in 1978, granting blanket amnesty for everything that happened during the dictatorship. Argentina passed two amnesty laws in 1986 and 1987. Uruguay passed one in 1986. Each one was designed to be permanent. Each one eventually fell. Chile's was circumvented through a legal theory called "secuestro permanente" (permanent kidnapping): if the body has never been found, the crime is still ongoing, and the amnesty doesn't apply. Argentina's Supreme Court declared its amnesty laws unconstitutional in 2005. Uruguay repealed its law in 2011, after the Inter-American Court of Human Rights ruled against it.

In 1998, Judge Baltasar Garzon in Spain used the Archives of Terror documents to arrest Augusto Pinochet in London. It was the first time a former head of state had been arrested abroad on a human rights warrant.

In Argentina, the 2016 Buenos Aires trial became the first court to formally recognize Operation Condor as a criminal conspiracy. The court called it "a vast and transnational asociacion ilicita" (criminal enterprise). Fifteen defendants were convicted. Former dictator Reynaldo Bignone got 20 years. Uruguayan Colonel Manuel Cordero got 25, the first foreign official ever extradited to Argentina for human rights crimes.

**By the numbers:** 107 perpetrators convicted across 45 proceedings. Justice for 262 confirmed victims. 805 transnational victims documented in total. 11 cases still in pretrial stage as of 2021.

In Italy, courts handed down life sentences, including as recently as October 2025.

Martin Almada died in March 2024. He spent his final decades as a human rights lawyer, using the archives he had discovered to pursue justice for other victims.

For years, the Archives of Terror sat in that police station basement because everyone who mattered assumed they would never be found.
