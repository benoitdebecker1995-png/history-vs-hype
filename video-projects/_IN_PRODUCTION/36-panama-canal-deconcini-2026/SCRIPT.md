## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:30) — specificity bomb

On November 18th, 1903, in a house in Washington, a Frenchman signed away the rights to the Panama Canal — a canal that wouldn't open for another eleven years.

[SHOW: the 1903 signature block — John Hay + Philippe Bunau-Varilla]

His name was Philippe Bunau-Varilla. He'd gotten himself named Panama's envoy only days before — a French citizen, speaking for a country that wasn't his. And he signed about two hours before Panama's own delegation could even reach the city. When their leader stepped off the train and Bunau-Varilla showed him what he'd already signed, the man nearly fainted on the platform.

[SHOW: title card — THE TREATY NO PANAMANIAN SIGNED]

The treaty that gave away their canal — not one Panamanian ever signed it.

## MYTH FRAME + BRIDGE (0:30–1:15) — open the debunk loop

And the most common thing people believe about this canal today is the opposite of what the treaty actually says. Go to any big video about the Panama Canal, and the top comment says the same thing: America secretly kept the right to take the canal back whenever it wants. And it's written right there in the treaty. President Trump said it from the inaugural stage.

[SHOW: Trump card — "We gave it to Panama, and we're taking it back." · Jan 20, 2025]

The actual clause they're all half-remembering is right there in the treaty — and it says almost the *opposite* of what they think. But to see why, you have to go back to the first time a treaty was used to take this country, by a man who wasn't even from it. What America got that day wasn't the canal exactly — it was a document that let it act like the owner for the next seventy years. So let's read it.

---

## ACT 1 (1:15–2:45) — how you take a canal with no one from the country in the room

How do you sign away a country's canal with nobody from that country in the room? It starts with a bankruptcy.

The French tried to dig this canal first, under Ferdinand de Lesseps — the man who'd built Suez. It destroyed them: as many as twenty thousand workers dead, and a collapse into bankruptcy and fraud trials. [SHOW: "de Lesseps · ~20,000 dead · 1881–1889"] What was left was a bankrupt French company holding the rights to a half-dug ditch — rights set to expire in 1904, which would hand the whole wreck back to Colombia for nothing. So it was desperate to sell to the United States for forty million dollars before that deadline hit. And one of its big shareholders was the same man who'd later sign the treaty — Bunau-Varilla. His forty million dollars depended on America choosing Panama. [SHOW: "New Panama Canal Co. → $40 million if the US picks Panama"]

There was just one problem: America preferred Nicaragua. The House voted for the Nicaragua route three hundred and eight to two. [SHOW: "House: 308–2 for Nicaragua"] So the company went to work on Washington. Its lawyer, William Cromwell, handed sixty thousand dollars to the Republican party through its chairman — and the party's enthusiasm for Nicaragua faded. [SHOW: "Cromwell → $60,000 to the GOP · LaFeber p.16"] And then Bunau-Varilla pulled a stunt. Nicaragua had been claiming its route was safe, because it had no active volcanoes. So when Bunau-Varilla found a Nicaraguan stamp showing one of its volcanoes erupting, he bought up every copy in Washington and set one on the desk of every single senator, labeled "Official testimony regarding volcanic activity in Nicaragua." [SHOW: the Momotombo stamp] Days later, the Senate switched the route to Panama.

But even buying the French company's rights wasn't enough. The land itself still belonged to Colombia, and America needed Colombia's permission to dig there. When Washington offered Colombia ten million dollars for the right to build, its senate turned the offer down — unanimously. So the plan changed: if Colombia won't make a deal, take Panama off Colombia. The Panamanians genuinely wanted out; they'd revolted against Bogotá dozens of times. [SHOW: "40+ Panamanian revolts vs Colombia, 19th c."] But in November 1903, it was American power that made the revolt succeed. A US gunboat sat in the harbor. And the only way across the isthmus was the railroad — run by an American company. So when Colombia sent troops to crush the revolt, the railroad's superintendent moved every train car to the far side and left them stranded. The whole revolution killed one man, a dog, and — by some accounts — a donkey. [SHOW: "Casualties: one man · one dog · one donkey"] Washington recognized the new country three days later. Fifteen days after that, the Frenchman signed.

## ACT 2 (2:45–4:00) — THE TURN: he made it worse than America asked

The strangest thing about that treaty isn't that Panama got a bad deal. It's that the Frenchman gave America more than America asked for.

He did it for a simple reason — he didn't care about Panama, he cared about his forty million, and that money only landed once the US Senate ratified this exact treaty. So he gave the Americans more, to make sure they couldn't say no. Washington had asked for a strip six miles wide; he made it ten. [SHOW: Art. II — "…a zone of land… of the width of ten miles…"] It had asked to lease that land for ninety-nine years; he handed it over *in perpetuity* — forever. [SHOW: Art. II — "…grants to the United States in perpetuity…"] And inside that strip, America got the right to act — in the treaty's own words — "as if it were the sovereign of the territory," to the entire exclusion of Panama. [SHOW: Art. III verbatim]

Everyone knew what that was. So they coined a phrase to soften it: "titular sovereignty" — Panama would keep the *title* of owning the place, while America ran every inch of it. Even the man who used that phrase, the future president William Howard Taft, called it privately a "barren scepter." [SHOW: Taft — "a barren scepter"] And the historian who wrote the standard account of all this, Walter LaFeber of Cornell, doesn't dress it up. What they built, he writes, was "a virtual colony of the United States." [SHOW: LaFeber — "a virtual colony of the United States"]

---

## ACT 3 (4:00–5:00) — the fiction lived (ammunition + the spine)

And for most of a century, that's what it was — not a canal America operated, but a piece of America sitting inside Panama. The Canal Zone: a ten-mile strip cutting the country in half, with its own police, its own courts, its own schools, its own post office.

And inside it, two classes of people, divided by one system everyone understood. Americans — almost all of them white — were on the "Gold Roll": paid in US gold, holding every skilled and management job. Everyone else was on the "Silver Roll": Panamanians, and the tens of thousands of Black Caribbean workers who'd actually dug the canal — all paid in cheaper silver, locked out of the top jobs. In 1947, a senior US official put it in writing: "The heart of the problem is the Gold and Silver rolls," through which "discrimination is practiced administratively." [SHOW: U.S. official, 1947 — "…discrimination is practiced administratively"] This was Jim Crow — the same racial segregation laws as the American South. And when it faded back home after the war, it stayed entrenched in the Zone — right down to separate drinking fountains, separate windows at the post office. And from the day the canal opened in 1914, the US ambassador William Jorden writes, not one Panamanian ever held a position of significance in running it. [SHOW: Jorden — "not one of their countrymen ever held any position of significance"]

The Americans on the Gold Roll lived well — about three times the average US wage, in subsidized towns with golf courses. And they kept themselves sealed off from the country around them. Only one in five spoke any Spanish. One of them put it plainly: there were people in the Zone who hadn't set foot in Panama in ten or twenty years — "and they're proud of it." [SHOW: Zonian — "…and they're proud of it"]

The highest hill in Panama City is called Ancon. It looks over everything. And since 1903 it had sat inside the Zone, topped with American antennas. For Panamanians, one of their own negotiators said, Ancon Hill was their Mount Everest — a foreign flag on the highest ground in their own capital, every single day. [SHOW: "Ancon Hill — inside the Zone since 1903"] That's the feeling Jorden called "a bone in the throat." A wealthy foreign country running a strip straight through the middle of yours, forever, on a treaty none of your countrymen ever signed. That arrangement lasted sixty years.

## ACT 4 (5:00–5:50) — MIDPOINT: 1964, the crack

January 1964. By then America had made one small concession to all that resentment: Panama's flag could fly beside the American flag at a few spots in the Zone. To the hardliners, even that was too much. At an American high school — Balboa High — the students raised the Stars and Stripes alone and surrounded the pole. [SHOW: "Jan 9, 1964 — Balboa High"]

So a couple hundred Panamanian students walked in carrying their own flag, to raise it themselves. In the scuffle, the Panamanian flag tore. [SHOW: the torn flag] That was the spark. As many as thirty thousand Panamanians poured into the streets. The rioting ran three and a half days. By the end, four American soldiers and twenty-four Panamanians were dead. [SHOW: "4 U.S. soldiers · 24 Panamanians killed"] The United States wanted to blame communists. The investigation found something simpler, and harder to wave away — that it was, in its words, "nineteenth-century nationalism." [SHOW: "nineteenth-century nationalism"] People were sick of a foreign flag flying over the middle of their own country.

Panama broke off relations the next day. And it would restore them on one condition: a brand-new treaty. Which left Panama an almost impossible job — to make the most powerful country on earth hand the canal back. Not with an army; 1964 had shown where force led. They'd have to do it the same way it was taken — with a treaty of their own.

---

## ACT 5 (5:50–7:45) — how Panama took it back (the mechanism)

And how Panama actually pulled it off comes down to one man: a general named Omar Torrijos, who seized power in 1968. His read on the Zone was blunt: there were two options, he said — "to smash it or lead it, and I'm not going to smash it." [SHOW: Torrijos — "to smash it or lead it"]

So instead of fighting America, Torrijos set out to embarrass it in front of the whole world. In 1973, he got the United Nations Security Council to hold a session not in New York, but in Panama City — so the world's diplomats would see the place for themselves. Washington scrambled to tidy up first; it even tore down a dividing fence and had a restaurant renamed. But you can't hide a capital full of slums sitting next to the Zone's golf courses. [SHOW: "UN Security Council · Panama City · March 1973"] Then came the vote: a resolution backing Panama's right to its own canal. Thirteen of the fifteen members sided with Panama. The United States killed it with its veto — the power any of the Council's five permanent members has to strike down a resolution on its own. [SHOW: "13–1 · the U.S. casts its veto"]

And the countries siding against America weren't only Panama's friends. They were the Soviet Union, China, even France. The United States stood almost completely alone — the only "no" vote in the room — defending something the rest of the world now openly called a colony. And we don't have to guess how Washington saw it. Years later the records were declassified — and there's Henry Kissinger, behind closed doors: holding onto the canal, he says, "is no issue to face the world on. It looks like pure colonialism." [SHOW: Kissinger, NSC 1975 (declassified) — "It looks like pure colonialism"]

A friendly Panama running the canal was simply worth more than a hostile one held at gunpoint. So America came to the table. In 1977, two new treaties were signed. One set a firm date to hand the canal to Panama — the last day of 1999. The other — the one that matters for our mystery — guaranteed the canal would stay permanently neutral, open to the ships of every nation. [SHOW: "1977 Torrijos–Carter Treaties · handover by Dec 31, 1999"] And that second treaty is where our viral claim finally comes in.

---

## ACT 6 (7:45–9:30) — the clause everyone quotes (debunk resolved)

Because when those treaties reached the US Senate in 1978, they nearly died — and to save them, a senator from Arizona named Dennis DeConcini forced a condition of his own into that neutrality treaty. It's the line people are half-remembering today. Here's what it actually says: if the canal were ever closed or interfered with, the United States could take steps — in the treaty's exact words — "including the use of military force in the Republic of Panama" — to reopen it. [SHOW: DeConcini condition verbatim]

Read on its own, that does sound like a standing right to send in troops. And that's exactly how DeConcini wanted it read. But even in the Senate, some saw where that could lead. Jacob Javits, who voted against the reservation, warned it could become "a hunting license for some future president." [SHOW: Javits — "a hunting license for some future president"]

So — does America actually have the right to take it back?

No. Because that line was only half of it. And when it was suggested, Torrijos threatened to walk away from the deal — because, according to him, it was just perpetuity in disguise. [SHOW: Torrijos — "Even if they dress it up as a clown, it'll still be perpetuity."] So the Senate fixed it, without touching DeConcini's words. They wrote a *second* line, into the treaty itself: any United States action, it says, "shall never be directed against the territorial integrity or political independence of Panama." [SHOW: Leadership Amendment verbatim] One line let America act to keep the canal open; the next made sure that could never become a right to invade.

And you can tell it was deliberate, because they got DeConcini himself to put his name on that second line. The author of the famous clause cosponsored the one that gutted it. [SHOW: "April 1978 — DeConcini cosponsors the limit"] It came down to a handful of senators on a Sunday — Byrd, Church, and Sarbanes — at a typewriter in a Senate office, drafting the words that would take the teeth out of DeConcini's clause. [SHOW: "April 16, 1978 — drafted in Sen. Church's office"]

And the law backs it up. A legal scholar named Harold Maier went through every word of both treaties, and his conclusion is flat. [SHOW: Maier — "there is no right for the United States to enter Panama to protect the Canal."]

So the document everyone quotes to prove America can take the canal back is the very same one that says it never can.

## CLOSE (9:30–10:45) — emotional climax + goosh

That's the whole strange story. The canal was signed away by a Frenchman, in a treaty no Panamanian ever signed. And they got it back the same way — not by an army, but by a vote at the UN, a clause typed out on a Sunday, and a date written into law.

On the morning the new treaties took effect, Panamanians did something they'd waited since 1903 to do. Before dawn, they started up Ancon Hill — the highest ground in the capital, foreign soil for seventy-six years. Thousands of them, climbing in the dark, on the kind of morning they'd tell their grandchildren about. At the top, as the sun came up, they raised a single Panamanian flag — fifty feet long, the largest in the country, big enough to be seen from every street corner in the city. And their president said it plainly, for everyone to hear: "A state within a state no longer exists." [SHOW: "Ancon Hill · the flag raised over Panama City, 1979"]

So — can the United States legally take the Panama Canal back? You can argue the politics all day. But on the documents themselves, the answer is no — and it has been since 1978. The country now talking about taking the canal back is the same one that signed the right away — and has been bound by that signature for almost fifty years.

And none of it is secret. The 1903 treaty, the clause everyone quotes, the line that cancels it — they're all there in the public record, for anyone to read. [SHOW: the documents side by side] In fact, the Panamanians had a name for how this all began. Back in the 1970s they made a documentary about that first treaty — the one the Frenchman signed. They called it "The Treaty That No Panamanian Signed." [SHOW: callback to the title card] They'd known the whole time. The rest of us just had to read it.
