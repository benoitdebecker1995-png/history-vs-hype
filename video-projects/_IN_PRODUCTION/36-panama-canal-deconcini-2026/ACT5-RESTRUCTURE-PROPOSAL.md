## Draft — Act 5 (voice-matched: plain, contractions, documents carry the verdict)

> And how Panama actually pulled it off comes down to one man, and one idea. The man is a general named
> Omar Torrijos, who seized power in 1968. The idea he gave to the American ambassador years later, in
> one sentence: **"To resolve a problem, the first thing you have to do is make it a problem."**
>
> [SHOW: Torrijos — "to resolve a problem, the first thing you have to do is make it a problem"]
>
> Because as long as this was a quarrel between Panama and the United States, he said, "the Americans
> would not give it importance." So he set out to make it everybody's quarrel.
>
> He started next door. He flew secretly to Bogotá, to Caracas, to San José — Colombia, Venezuela, Costa
> Rica. **"We could not go and ask for solidarity of the world,"** he said, **"if we didn't have the
> solidarity of our neighbours."**
>
> [SHOW: Torrijos — "…if we didn't have the solidarity of our neighbours"]
>
> Only then did he go global. In 1973 he got the United Nations Security Council to hold a session not in
> New York, but in Panama City — so the world's diplomats would see the place themselves. Washington
> scrambled to tidy up first: it tore down a dividing fence, had a restaurant renamed. But you can't hide
> a capital full of slums sitting next to the Zone's golf courses. Thirteen of the fifteen members backed
> Panama. The United States killed it with its veto.
>
> [SHOW: "13–1 · the U.S. casts its veto · March 1973"]
>
> **But embarrassment on its own doesn't move a superpower. What moved this one was a fact about the
> canal itself.**
>
> A canal is fifty miles of locks, dams and cuttings, run by tens of thousands of people — and almost all
> of those people are Panamanian. Torrijos put it in one line: the canal is **"as vulnerable as a newborn
> baby."** Which meant the only real defence against sabotage wasn't soldiers. It was — in the American
> ambassador's own words — **"the goodwill and cooperation of the Panamanian people and their National
> Guard."**
>
> [SHOW: Jorden — "the goodwill and cooperation of the Panamanian people and their National Guard"]
>
> And this is the part that settles it. **The Americans agreed.** The State Department and the Chairman of
> the Joint Chiefs, General George Brown, accepted that the waterway was **"nearly impossible to protect
> against sabotage"** — and that if America ever had to defend it against the Panamanians themselves, it
> "could not expect much sympathy" from anyone in Latin America.
>
> [SHOW: "nearly impossible to protect against sabotage" — Gen. George Brown / State Dept]
>
> So the bases were beside the point. You cannot garrison your way out of needing the consent of the
> people who operate the locks. And once you see that, every American decision that follows makes sense.
> Kissinger, behind closed doors: holding on **"is no issue to face the world on. It looks like pure
> colonialism."** The chief negotiator warning President Ford that without a deal there'd be **"serious
> confrontation, possibly involving violence against the Canal Zone."** Carter's own security adviser:
> the canal's strategic value had **"diminished"** while its potential as a **"source of conflict"** had
> grown.
>
> A friendly Panama running the canal was simply worth more than a hostile one held at gunpoint. So
> America came to the table.

## 🛑 Guards carried into this act

1. **Never say "Panama was ready to blow up the canal."** LaFeber's word is **"afterwards"** — Torrijos
   announced it *once the treaties were safe*. Retrospective bravado, not an operational plan. Jorden has
   him vacillating, talking about "the other road" only "in his blackest hours." **The claim is the
   vulnerability, and the Pentagon conceding it. The boast is not needed and is not safe.**
2. **Jorden is a US ambassador.** He's openly contemptuous of Torrijos's Non-Aligned trip — *"a contrived
   political charade."* I've cut the NAM beat rather than launder his scepticism. If it goes back in, his
   scepticism goes with it.
3. **The Cuba card (C62) is cut for runtime,** not for sourcing. It's good and it's available if Act 1
   gives back more than expected.
4. **"General George Brown"** — LaFeber has State Dept officials agreeing *with* Brown. Keep the phrasing
   as agreement, don't promote it to a Brown quotation.
