## HOOK

August 8th, 1444.

Prince Henry the Navigator sits on horseback in Lagos, Portugal, watching 235 captured Africans being divided into lots. He takes his royal fifth — 46 people for his personal estate — while captives cling to one another, so close they can hardly be separated.

The viral talking point — mostly parroted in right-wing circles — says Europeans never kidnapped anyone. They just bought slaves from a pre-existing African market.

But look at the chronicle we opened with. It was written by Gomes Eanes de Zurara — Prince Henry's own court historian.

Europe did the kidnapping. And when those raids couldn't keep up with European demand, they built a system to do it at scale.

I want to ignore the modern culture war and read the primary sources from the people who built that system. Five documents — and they prove the same arc: Europe came kidnapping; the raids couldn't supply the demand; and they engineered the replacement.

## CONCEED-BEAT

You just saw a 1444 chronicle entry. There's a kernel of truth in the right-wing claim we have to acknowledge first: slavery did exist in Africa before Europeans arrived. But it existed in two very different forms — and neither was the system Europeans tapped into.

The first was the Trans-Saharan trade — the system Muslim merchants ran across the desert from West Africa to North Africa and the Middle East. It had its own legal framework. In 1614, the Timbuktu scholar Ahmed Baba was asked by trans-Saharan slave merchants which captives they could legally hold. His ruling laid out the boundaries:

"Non-belief is that which makes enslavement permissible, whether the enslaved were black or white."

In the Muslim parts of Africa, enslavement was bound to religion, not race. Any Muslim, of any color, could not be enslaved.

The second form was sub-Saharan inter-polity slavery. Olaudah Equiano — an Igbo man kidnapped as a child in 1756, later sold into the Atlantic trade — described it firsthand:

"They always carry slaves through our land; but the strictest account is exacted of their manner of procuring them before they are suffered to pass. Sometimes indeed we sold slaves to them, but they were only prisoners of war, or such among us as had been convicted of kidnapping, for adultery, and some other crimes, which we esteemed heinous."

Equiano's community had slavery. His own father owned slaves. But the limits were strict — only war captives or convicted criminals.

Faith on one side. Kinship and judicial limits on the other. Both regional systems had categorical lines around who could be sold.

The Atlantic system erased the lines — and engineered a new one: race. And it didn't just change the rules. It changed the scale.

The Trans-Saharan trade averaged about a thousand people a year per route.

The Atlantic system hit a peak of eighty thousand people a year. An eighty-times multiplier.

And it didn't borrow from the African systems. Historian Paul Lovejoy: before 1450, slavery in Africa was a marginal social institution. The Atlantic trade turned it into a mode of production.

What the talking point describes — mass commodity slavery, racially coded, demographically catastrophic — was created by the Atlantic trade, not borrowed from it.

## THE TURN

The first major crack in the "we just bought what they sold" myth comes from inside Africa itself.

By 1526, the King of Kongo, Afonso the First, wasn't managing a booming domestic market. He was writing desperate letters to the King of Portugal, begging him to stop the trade.

Here are Afonso's exact words to his European counterpart:

"The said merchants take our countrymen the sons of our land and the sons of our noblemen every day... our land is being all but depopulated."

He didn't stop there. He issued an explicit demand to shut the system down:

"Our will is that in this Kingdom there be no slave trade nor [any] outlet for it."

If the Atlantic system was just Europeans tapping into a pre-existing African market, why is a reigning African monarch on the record demanding a cessation of the trade while it was happening?

Because by 1526, the Atlantic market wasn't pre-existing anything. It had been running on European demand for three quarters of a century. The chronicle that documents the start of Europeans being part of this system was written by Prince Henry's own court historian — eighty years before Afonso ever wrote that letter.

## EVIDENCE 1 — Zurara

Let's look a bit closer at the claim that Europeans never went over to Africa and kidnapped anyone.

It's 1444. A Portuguese fleet attacks the Arguin Bank, off the coast of modern-day Mauritania. We know exactly what happened because Prince Henry's official court chronicler, Gomes Eanes de Zurara, recorded the entire military operation.

He wrote:

"But they, shouting out 'St. James', 'St. George', 'Portugal', at once attacked them, killing and taking all they could."

And he described the aftermath of the raid:

"Then might you see mothers forsaking their children, and husbands their wives... others stowed their children among the sea-weed, where our men found them afterwards."

What Zurara is describing here is a raid. People — children pulled from seaweed, mothers separated from their families — were taken back to Portugal. 165 of them, in that single raid alone.

This wasn't trade. It was kidnapping. And it wasn't a one-off — the records show constant raids throughout the 1440s.

---

## BRIDGE — Raid to System

The raids didn't stop because Europeans had a change of heart. They stopped because African forces shut them down. By 1445, Zurara himself records, Africans wielding poisoned arrows had killed twenty of twenty-two Portuguese on one expedition alone. The lasso wasn't working.

So Portugal changed tactics.

By the 1450s, a Portuguese trading factory at Arguim — off the coast of modern Mauritania — was supplying eight hundred to a thousand captives a year to the European market.

Meanwhile, Iberian sugar plantations were taking root on the Atlantic islands — Madeira, the Canaries, Cape Verde, São Tomé. These were the systems New World plantations would later copy.

And the numbers climbed. Between 1450 and 1500, eighty-one thousand enslaved Africans crossed into the Atlantic system. The next century: three hundred thirty-eight thousand.

This is what Afonso of Kongo was writing to João III about in 1526. Not a few raids — seventy-five years of papally-licensed extraction, drawing tens of thousands of people from coastal factories across West Africa.

So how did Europe respond?

## MID-PIVOT — João III

In 1529, the King of Portugal, João III, replied to Afonso. He told the King of Kongo that the depopulation he was protesting wasn't actually happening.

Read it. The denial contains the confession.

"You say in your letters that you do not want there to be any slave trade in your kingdom because it is depopulating your land. I can believe that you only say this as a result of the suffering that the Portuguese cause you, because I am told of the great size of Kongo and how it is so populated that it appears that not a single slave has left it."

Read it again. He's not saying "no slaves are being taken." He's saying Kongo is so large that even if slaves ARE being taken, you wouldn't notice it. The denial isn't "we're not doing this" — it's "you wouldn't notice that we're doing this at scale."

The buyer literally gaslights the supplier. He tells the King of Kongo his country is so big he won't even miss the people being taken.

## EVIDENCE 2 — De Marees

But what about the strongest version of the right-wing claim — that Africans were "already slaves to begin with" and Europeans just loaded them onto ships?

The answer comes from a Dutch slave-trader writing on the Gold Coast around 1602 — a buyer trying to fill ships, writing to his investors to explain why he can't.

"They also enslave one another, but not in the same manner as in Angola or Conge, for it would not be possible to load ships here with blacks. They do not have at their disposal a multitude of Captives, and so on the Gold Coast they cannot be purchased in large numbers."

De Marees is admitting two things at once.

First — yes, indigenous slavery existed even on the Gold Coast. He says so. African polities did enslave one another.

Second — and this is where the right-wing claim falls apart — the volume Europeans needed wasn't there. He couldn't fill his ships from existing captives. Indigenous markets couldn't supply Atlantic demand.

So if the Atlantic system was running — and by 1602 it was, in massive volume — the captives weren't coming from a pre-existing market. They were coming from people being newly captured because Europeans were buying.

Historian Patrick Manning's economic history proves this empirically: newly-captured prices reflected capture cost, not the value of a pre-existing slave.

The Atlantic system didn't tap into a market. It built one.

## EVIDENCE 3 — Romanus Pontifex

Which brings us to the fourth document — the one that licensed the system years before mass extraction scaled.

On January 8th, 1455, Pope Nicholas V issued a papal bull called Romanus Pontifex. The Pope was the highest legal and moral authority in Europe, and he issued this at the request of the King of Portugal, granting them an exclusive monopoly in Africa.

On the left, the original Latin from 1455 — on the right, the English translation.

"...illorumque personas in perpetuam servitutem redigendi..."

"...and to reduce their persons to perpetual slavery..."

And this wasn't the first time Nicholas V had issued that phrase. Three years earlier, in June 1452, he'd already signed Dum Diversas — with the same operative clause, word for word.

"...illorumque personas in perpetuam servitutem redigendi..."

Same Pope, same words — Romanus Pontifex was the upgrade, giving Portugal exclusive rights in Africa.

The bull authorizes Portugal to invade, search out, and capture —

— to vanquish and subdue all Saracens and pagans whatsoever —

— and to reduce their persons to perpetual slavery.

This was 1455. The legal architecture for open-ended, permanent, perpetual slavery was drawn up before the commerce existed. And the operators relied on it. Historian P.E. Russell documents that Prince Henry himself cited "various earlier papal bulls" as the "powerful authority" for his slave trade.

The Pope didn't license a market. He licensed a conquest.

---

## EVIDENCE 4 — Gorrevod Asiento

And we have the operational receipt for that conquest.

Sixty-three years after Romanus Pontifex, in August 1518, the King of Spain issued a license to a man named Lorenzo de Gorrevod. The terms:

"Know ye that I have given permission... to Lorenzo de Gorrevod... whereby he... may proceed to take to the Indies, the islands and the mainland of the ocean sea... four thousand negro slaves both male and female, provided they be Christians... without coming to Seville, as many as possible being taken from Cape Verde."

Four thousand people. In a single contract. Sourced from the Cape Verde Islands — by then a Portuguese entrepôt feeding African captives into the Atlantic system.

This is the asiento. The moment the papal license became a contract. The bull authorized perpetual slavery in 1455; by 1518, the Spanish crown was contracting for it by the thousand.

The Pope didn't just bless a market. He chartered an industry.
