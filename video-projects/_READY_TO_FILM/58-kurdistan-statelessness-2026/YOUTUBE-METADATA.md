## Description

Kurdistan's history doesn't start with a broken promise in 1920. The word "Kurdistan" was on the map by 1150 — and from 1514, self-governing Kurdish emirates ran their own courts, raised their own armies, and minted their own coins, for three hundred years.

We go to the documents: Ibn al-Athir recording Saladin's Kurdish lineage, the Sharafnama of 1597 — a Kurdish prince writing the history of his own ruling houses — Ottoman records that filed the emirates as hükûmet, "government," and Sheikh Ubeydullah's 1880 letter announcing "the first nucleus of a Kurdish state." Then the file everyone thinks they know: the Treaty of Sèvres section headed KURDISTAN, the Lausanne conference minutes where Lord Curzon and İsmet İnönü argued over whether a Kurdish people even existed, and the League of Nations report that concluded an independent Kurdish state should be created — right before Britain chose Mosul's oil instead.

In the Treaty of Lausanne, the treaty that fixed the borders thirty million Kurds still live inside, the word "Kurd" does not appear a single time. And in January 2026, Syria's new government offered its Kurds citizenship and language rights — everything except a country. The pattern is a century old, and this is the documented version of how it was built.
