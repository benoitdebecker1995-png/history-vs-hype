## COLD OPEN — the Saladin/Saddam paradox  [G1]

Everyone knows Saladin — the Muslim sultan who took Jerusalem back from the Crusaders. What not a lot of people know is that he wasn't an Arab or a Turk, but a Kurd.

And you don't have to take my word for it. [SHOW: classical painting of Saladin] Ibn al-Athir, a near-contemporary Arab chronicler, recorded Saladin's family as Kurds — "the noblest of the Kurds," in his words. [SHOW: Ibn al-Athir, *al-Kamil* p.176 — "Rawadi Kurds in origin, which lineage is the noblest of the Kurds" quote card] And the dynasty Saladin founded, the Ayyubids, became the most powerful Kurdish dynasty in the history of Islam. [SHOW: "most powerful Kurdish dynasty in the history of Islam" card] [SOURCE: Ibn al-Athir 53233ac0 / Eppel / C1]

Saladin came from a town called Tikrit. Centuries later, that same town produced another ruler obsessed with his legacy: Saddam Hussein. In 1987, his regime held a conference titled "The Battle of Liberation: From Saladin to Saddam Hussein." [SHOW: Saddam-era Saladin propaganda — the 1987 conference / the children's book "The Hero Saladin"] It styled him Saladin the Second — and quietly erased the one detail that didn't fit. That Saladin was a Kurd. [SOURCE: HNN, web-verified / C39 — "conveniently forgetting that Saladin was a Kurd, Saddam makes much of the fact that he and Saladin were born in the same little village of Tikrit." Conference full title + "Saladin II" CONFIRMED. STAMPS CUT (unverified, no source found).]

One year after that conference, in 1988, Saddam's army gassed the Kurds — the very people his hero Saladin came from. [SHOW: Halabja memorial, handled with restraint]

The people who gave Islam its greatest sultan are, today, the largest nation on Earth without a country to call their own. So how does that happen? You've probably heard some version of the answer — that they were betrayed at the end of the First World War, a people too divided to ever build a state of their own.

Almost none of that is what actually happened. The Kurds didn't fail to build a state — they built dozens of them. And then they watched them taken apart.

## ACT 1 — THEY HAD STATES  [C33, C23, C30, C1]

So let's get the facts straight, starting with the name. "Kurdistan" isn't a modern invention — the word is about nine hundred years old. [SOURCE: Cambridge / C33 / C21] In 1150, the Seljuk sultan Sanjar created an actual province called Kurdistan. [SHOW: map of the 1150 Seljuk province of Kurdistan (capital Bahar)] [SOURCE: Cambridge p.2 "In the year 1150, the Seljuk sultan Sanjar created the province of Kurdistan, with the town of Bahar as its capital" / Eppel p.10] And in 1597, a Kurdish prince named Sharaf Khan wrote a history of his own people's ruling houses — the Sharafnama. [SHOW: Sharafnama page] Four hundred years ago, the Kurds already had dynasties worth writing the history of.

But a name on a map isn't a state, and a dynasty on a page isn't self-rule. The real question is whether the Kurds ever actually governed themselves. And for three hundred years, they did.

After 1514, when the Ottomans beat the Persians at the battle of Chaldiran, the Kurdish mountains became the front line between two empires. Rather than garrison that rugged frontier directly, the Ottomans made a deal. Their diplomat Idris Bitlisi offered the Kurdish princes a bargain: stay loyal to the sultan against Persia, send troops when he called — and in return, you keep your lands, your titles, and the right to rule your own territory and pass it to your heirs. [SOURCE: McDowall / C23] The princes accepted. And it created a string of self-governing Kurdish states — the emirates — that ruled themselves for the next three centuries.

And this wasn't token, pseudo-independence — these emirates genuinely ran themselves. The empire even had an official category for the most powerful of them: hükûmet — a word that literally means "government." In Ottoman paperwork they were "separated from the pen, and cut off from the feet." Meaning: left alone — the empire took none of their taxes, and none of their sons. [SOURCE: Ateş / C37] The emirate of Bitlis ran its own courts, appointed its own judges, and could put twelve thousand cavalry in the field.

And one prince makes the point better than any document. Muhammad Kor of Soran minted his own coins, stamped with his own name. [SHOW: period Kurdish coin] And every Friday, he had the prayer read in his name — not the sultan's. In the Muslim world, those were the two classic marks of a sovereign: your own coin, and your own name in the Friday prayer. A subject never got to do either. Muhammad Kor did both. And this was no mountain chieftain: he ran plants that turned out his own artillery and rifles, and put an army of thirty thousand men in the field. [SOURCE: Eppel/Cambridge / C30 — Kor's "plants for the manufacture of artillery, shells, rifles, swords"; ~10,000 cavalry + 20,000 infantry]

But — and this is important — that was *a* Kurdish state, never *the* Kurdish state. These emirates were rivals; they fought each other as much as anyone, and each owed allegiance to a sultan or a shah. They had states. They just never had one — or, as one historian puts it, they were "never united under one political unit named Kurdistan." [SOURCE: Posch via Cambridge / C33]

So you've got a people running their own courts, minting their own coins, raising their own armies. Whatever you want to call that, it isn't a tribe — it's a state running itself.

## ACT 2 — THE DISMANTLING  [C23, C30, C32]

And these states didn't just disappear. They were taken apart — deliberately — by the very empire they'd served for three centuries.

By the 1820s, the Ottoman empire was modernizing — pulling power back to the capital, running its provinces directly. And a centralized state can't tolerate self-governing princes who pay no tax and raise their own armies. So it came for the emirates, one at a time. Historians call it a second Ottoman conquest. [SOURCE: Cambridge / C23]

Take Muhammad Kor — the prince who minted his own coins. In 1836, an Ottoman army three times his size had him surrounded. The commander sealed the offer in the most binding form he could: a Quran stamped with his own seal: if Kor surrendered, he'd keep his life and his throne. [SOURCE: Cambridge/C30] On that promise, Kor gave himself up and sailed for Istanbul. On the voyage home, across the Black Sea, he simply "disappeared under mysterious circumstances." Murdered, almost certainly. And with him went the emirate of Soran. [SOURCE: Eppel/McDowall / C30]

By now the princes could see what was coming. With their independence slipping away, the strongest still standing — Bedirxan of Bohtan — finally tried to unite them. In 1847 he pulled the neighbouring emirs into a league that Ottoman officers said openly "dreamt of establishing an independent state." [SOURCE: Ateş / C33] It was the closest the Kurds ever came to uniting. And it still came apart — crushed by the Ottoman army, but only after Bedirxan's own cousin defected and led the troops straight to his stronghold.

And that betrayal wasn't a fluke — it was the oldest weakness the Kurds had: rival tribes that could never trust each other long enough to stay united. One Kurdish historian calls that tribalism "the main barrier" to a nation of their own. So when people say the Kurds were "too divided" to build a state, the feuds they're pointing to were real. [SHOW: Kendal Nezan "the main barrier" card] [SOURCE: Kendal Nezan — "Tribalism seems to have been the main barrier to the emergence of a national consciousness"; revolts collapsed through "betrayals, switching of allegiances, divisions amongst the Kurds themselves" — NLM 75e966ef ⚠️ ADD CARD to 01-VERIFIED-RESEARCH] But that's only half of it. By 1850, the emirates were gone, the princes killed off, and power had fallen back to feuding tribes and local sheikhs — men who could run a valley, but not a country. [SHOW: Ateş "re-clanization of Kurdistan" card] And those emirates had been the closest thing the Kurds ever had to the foundation of a state — smash them, and there was nothing left to build a country around. [SHOW: Eppel "wiped out any potential nuclei" card] [SOURCE: Eppel 2008:240 / NLM 23945b11 — "wiped out any potential nuclei… could have become a nucleus for Kurdish statehood and nationalism after the First World War"; Ateş "re-clanization of Kurdistan" NLM 5ae2f56b / C23, C31; van Bruinessen "rapid devolution… a few steps back on the evolutionary ladder" NLM 07657ff8]
But the idea of Kurdistan outlived the princes. In the vacuum the emirates left behind, it was the Sufi sheikhs — religious leaders whose following crossed the tribal lines that had always split the Kurds — who picked it up. And in 1880, one of them did what no prince ever had. Sheikh Ubeydullah of Nehri raised fifteen thousand men from rival tribes and marched into Persian Kurdistan to carve out what he called the first nucleus of a Kurdish state. [SHOW: map of the 1880 Ubeydullah revolt] He'd put the aim in writing, to an American missionary doctor: "We are a nation apart. We want our affairs to be in our own hands, like other nations." [SHOW: Ubeydullah's 1880 letter — quote card] [SOURCE: Cambridge / C32 — Ubeydullah of Nehri, letter to Dr Cochran, 5 Oct 1880; verbatim "The Kurdish nation … is a people apart … We are a nation apart. We want our affairs to be in our own hands … and have privileges like other nations"; "the first attempt to reassemble Kurdistan," legitimized via "the idiom of Kurdish nationhood"] But the moment a Kurdish state looked real, two empires that agreed on almost nothing — Ottoman and Persian — joined forces and crushed it. Ubeydullah was arrested, and died in exile. [SOURCE: 1880 revolt grounded NLM 367ed08a (Eppel / Cambridge History of the Kurds): Oct 1880 Ubeydullah invaded Qajar Persia (Persian Kurdistan) with ~15,000–20,000 men in two columns — son Abd al-Qadir took Sawj Bulaq/Mahabad, Ubeydullah besieged Urumiya (~7,000); stated aim "establishment of an independent Kurdish state" / verbatim "first nucleus of his Kurdish state"; failed at Urumiya, withdrew as ~12,000 Qajar regulars approached; Ottoman forces then BLOCKED the retreating Kurds and helped Iran "annihilate" them — the two rival empires cooperated to suppress it; arrested 1881, exiled to the Hijaz/Mecca, died 1883. ⚠️ Miandoab sack (~2,000–3,000 Shi'i killed) OMITTED — not needed. Scholars DIVIDED on "first expression of Kurdish nationalism" (Eppel cit.3); script avoids the superlative, frames it as raising an army for a Kurdish state (uncontested). 2026-06-10.] And forty years later, the empire that ruled them collapsed.

## ACT 3 — THE FATAL WINDOW  [C22, C6, C19, C15, C8, C9]

The First World War ended the Ottoman Empire, and the victors sat down to redraw the Middle East. This was the one moment a Kurdish state was genuinely on the table. And the Kurds walked into it with almost nothing to bargain with.

Compare them to the Arabs. The Arabs had a royal dynasty, a written British promise of a state, and a prince — Faisal — who'd fought alongside the British and arrived in Paris with a seat at the table. [SHOW: Faisal at the Paris Peace Conference, 1919] [SOURCE: MacMillan, *Paris 1919* — "Feisal had led an Arab revolt against the Turks," "the cavalry of St. George (gold sovereigns), British weapons and advisers"; British "made a promise to establish an Arab state and intended to keep it" — NLM 68c36cea / 5ed5ec15; McMahon–Hussein / C22] The Kurds had none of that: no royal house, no single leader their old rivalries would ever let rise [SHOW: scholar card — "schisms within the Kurdish society prevented the emergence of a leadership that could fill a role akin to that held by the Hashemite emirs"], and no favour to call in — because most Kurds had fought for the Ottoman side, and lost. [SHOW: Kurdish troops in Ottoman service — "the greater part of the Ottoman forces in the region were Kurdish" (McDowall) card] [SOURCE: McDowall — "The greater part of the Ottoman forces in the region were Kurdish" NLM 5ab96a08; Zeki Bey estimate "some 300,000 Kurds lost their lives as a result of the conflict" NLM 23945b11 / C22] The Kurdish case was so weak that when Lloyd George ran down the list of Ottoman territories to hand out, he forgot the Kurds entirely. [SOURCE: MacMillan / C22]

And yet — forgotten or not — a year later, the Kurds were in the formal peace treaty. In 1920, they really were promised a state, in writing. [SHOW: Sèvres "Section III — KURDISTAN" page] The Treaty of Sèvres has a section headed, in capital letters, "KURDISTAN," and an article that promised them independence. [SOURCE: treaty text / C6]

But read the fine print. That independence came with conditions — the Kurds had one year just to ask for it, and a League of Nations commission first had to rule them "capable" of governing themselves. And then none of it happened — because of who had signed it. Sèvres was signed by a defeated Ottoman government with no power left to hand anything over. The man who actually held the power — Mustafa Kemal Atatürk — had rejected the treaty, and then won a three-year war of independence. The Allies could have enforced Sèvres, but they had no army left in the field to do it. [SOURCE: MacMillan / C11 — "at least twenty-seven divisions to enforce the terms, divisions they did not have" (NLM 68c36cea); Curzon, 1923: "we are negotiating with the enemy who has an army in being while we have none" — NLM-verified 2026-06-05] So the nationalists tore it up. [SHOW: McDowall card — "Sèvres, as far as the Turks were concerned, was utterly void before the ink was dry" (p.137)] [SOURCE: McDowall / C19] You could call it a betrayal — but really, the Kurds had been promised something by a government too weak to deliver it. [SOURCE: Eppel — "The Treaty of Sèvres gave the Kurds, for the first time, an international assurance of a future Kurdish state. This window of opportunity, however, was short-lived," p.11; McDowall — "an unwilling and token government that lacked a constituency even in Istanbul," p.137; Bajalan — Sèvres self-rule "contingent on the will of Britain, France and Italy," p.401; Kemalists "rejected it in its entirety" (Mango p.127 / Radpey p.1196) — NLM 367ed08a / C19, C6. ⚠️ SCHOLARSHIP DIVIDED — betrayal/genuine-window view (Eppel, Bozarslan, Kendal) vs dead-letter view (Tusan, van Bruinessen); script lands the honest middle: a real promise, never deliverable.]

And the borders that replaced it — the ones the Kurds still live inside today — were fixed three years later, by a treaty far less famous than Sèvres — the Treaty of Lausanne, 1923. [SHOW: Treaty of Lausanne]

By 1923, the chance of a Kurdish state had all but closed. So at Lausanne, what was left to fight for was smaller, but it still mattered: recognition. [SHOW: Lausanne negotiations] Would the Kurds even be acknowledged as a distinct people — a minority? [SHOW: Curzon quote card] Turkey's İsmet İnönü argued the opposite — the Kurds and the Turks were one and the same, "a single unit in respect of race, religion and manners." And if the Kurds were just Turks, there was nothing for the outside world to protect. Curzon didn't buy it. Atatürk's nationalists were building a single Turkish nation — one language, one identity — with no place for a separate Kurdish people; a few years on, they banned the word "Kurd" outright. [SHOW: 1926 decree on ethnic names] Curzon being right didn't change a thing. So why did Britain let it happen — right after Curzon had argued the Kurds deserved autonomy? It came down to one province on the new Turkey–Iraq border: Mosul. [SHOW: Mosul vilayet map, Turkey–Iraq border] And Mosul was overwhelmingly Kurdish — so Kurdish that the League of Nations itself concluded, on ethnic grounds alone, that "an independent Kurdish State should be created" there.

But under that ground sat oil — the Kirkuk fields, already famous as a "lake of petroleum." And Britain's brand-new Iraq couldn't survive without it. A Kurdish state would have taken Mosul, and its oil, straight out of Iraq — the one thing Britain couldn't afford.

But Britain still needed the Kurds — at least for a while. Mosul's fate was going to the League, and it helped Britain's case if the Kurds said they'd rather live under Baghdad than under Turkey. So — in McDowall's words — "the Kurds would have to be bought off." In December 1922, Britain put out a public declaration: the Kurds could set up their own government inside Iraq. That promise lasted about four months. The moment Mosul was secured, it was "quietly junked." Britain kept Mosul, and folded the Kurds into Iraq. There would be no Kurdistan — not even the slice of self-rule they'd been promised. The land they sat on had become too valuable to give to them. ## ACT 4 — THE COST, AND THE RHYME  [C36, C35, C31, M1–M3, G1]

Stateless on paper, the Kurds spent the next century useful to everyone and trusted by no one — armed when a bigger power needed them, dropped the moment it didn't.

By the late 1980s, Saddam Hussein's Iraq was deep into a brutal war with Iran. And the Iraqi Kurds had thrown in with Tehran — taking its weapons, opening a second front against Baghdad, betting the war was their chance at last. So when it turned Saddam's way, he turned on them at home. [SHOW: Anfal, handled with restraint] [SOURCE: Anfal motive grounded NLM 5ab96a08 (McDowall) + 23945b11 (Bozarslan/Hiltermann) + b61a0002, confirmed 2026-06-10: KDP "received military aid and logistical support from the Iranian state"; Nov 1986 Barzani–Talabani coalition in Tehran opened a northern front "in concert with Iranian formations," even helped Iran take Halabja; as the war turned Iraq's way in 1988 the Kurdish–Iranian challenge "provided the opportunity and the troops" al-Majid needed; Anfal = retaliation/punishment for "collaborat[ing] with the enemy" (Taha Yasin Ramadan: the region got "the same treatment as the enemy"); most Anfal ops ran AFTER the war ended = not military necessity (Hiltermann).] The Anfal campaign destroyed around four thousand Kurdish villages and killed well over a hundred thousand people.
