## COLD OPEN — the Saladin/Saddam paradox  [G1]

The most famous Muslim hero of the Crusades was a Kurd.

Saladin — the sultan who took back Jerusalem — came from a Kurdish family. [SHOW: classical painting of Saladin] His dynasty, the Ayyubids, was, in one historian's words, "the most powerful Kurdish dynasty in the history of Islam." [SOURCE: Eppel / C1]

And his people are the largest nation on Earth without a country. More than thirty million Kurds, spread across Turkey, Iran, Iraq and Syria. [SOURCE: McDowall/Cambridge / C20]

Saddam Hussein was born in the same town as Saladin — Tikrit. And he built a cult on it: in 1987, a conference titled "The Battle of Liberation: From Saladin to Saddam Hussein." [SHOW: Saddam-era Saladin propaganda — the 1987 conference / the children's book "The Hero Saladin"] His regime styled him Saladin the Second — and quietly erased the one detail that didn't fit. That Saladin was Kurdish. [SOURCE: HNN, web-verified / C39 — "conveniently forgetting that Saladin was a Kurd, Saddam makes much of the fact that he and Saladin were born in the same little village of Tikrit." Conference full title + "Saladin II" CONFIRMED. STAMPS CUT (unverified, no source found).]

One year after that conference, in 1988, Saddam's army gassed the Kurds. [SHOW: Halabja memorial, handled with restraint]

So how does the people that gave Islam its greatest king end up with nothing? You've probably heard the answer: betrayed by everyone, carved up by Sykes-Picot, a people who simply never had a state.

Except that last part isn't true. The Kurds didn't fail to build a state. They built dozens. And then they watched them taken apart.

## ACT 1 — THEY HAD STATES  [C33, C23, C30, C1]

Start with the thing nobody tells you. "Kurdistan" is not a modern invention. An 11th-century geographer drew "the land of the Kurds." [SHOW: al-Kashgari's 11th-c. map] [SOURCE: Cambridge / C33] And in 1597 a Kurdish prince, Sharaf Khan, wrote a history of his own people's ruling houses — the Sharafnama. [SHOW: Sharafnama page] A dynasty writing down its own kings, four centuries ago.

What were these kings?

After 1514, when the Ottomans beat the Persians at the battle of Chaldiran, an Ottoman diplomat named Idris Bitlisi cut a deal with the Kurdish princes. Back the sultan against Persia, and you keep your lands. [SOURCE: McDowall / C23] The result was a string of Kurdish principalities — call them emirates — that governed themselves for the next three hundred years.

And I don't mean lightly. The top tier had a status the Ottomans called hükûmet — government. In the empire's own paperwork they were "separated from the pen, and cut off from the feet" — meaning no tax assessors, no inspectors, no conscription. [SOURCE: Ateş / C37] The emirate of Bitlis ran its own courts, appointed its own judges, and could field twelve thousand cavalry.

And one detail ends the argument. One of these princes — Muhammad Kor of Soran — minted his own coins, with his own name on them. [SHOW: period Kurdish coin] He had the Friday prayer read in his name — instead of the sultan's — the medieval signature of a sovereign ruler. He built workshops that cast his own cannons. [SOURCE: Eppel/Cambridge / C30]

And his state worked. A British traveller who rode through Soran in 1834 found that robbery had been stamped out so completely that, in his words, "were any man to see a purse of gold lying on the road, he would not touch it" — he'd report it to the nearest headman, who kept it until the owner came to claim it. [SHOW: Fraser's 1834 account] [SOURCE: Fraser via van Bruinessen / C37]

That is not a tribe. That is a state.

The honest part, because this matters. These were never one country. They were rival statelets that fought each other and bowed, on paper, to a sultan or a shah. As one historian puts it, the Kurds were "never united under one political unit named Kurdistan." [SOURCE: Posch via Cambridge / C33] So hold both halves of the truth at once. The Kurds had states. They never had a single nation-state. Remember that — it's the whole story.

## ACT 2 — THE DISMANTLING  [C23, C30, C32]

So if the Kurds had states, the real question isn't why they never built one. It's who took the ones they had.

The answer is the empire they served. In the 1830s and 40s a modernizing Ottoman state could no longer tolerate princes who paid no tax and raised their own armies. So it came for the emirates — historians call it a second Ottoman conquest. [SOURCE: Cambridge / C23]

Watch how they did it. Muhammad Kor — the prince who minted his own coins — was surrounded in 1836. The Ottoman commander sent him a Quran, sealed with his own seal, swearing safe conduct and a throne to keep. [SOURCE: Cambridge/C30] On that promise he surrendered and sailed for Istanbul, where he spent six months as the sultan's guest. And on the voyage home, across the Black Sea, he simply "disappeared under mysterious circumstances." Murdered, almost certainly. And with him, the emirate of Soran. [SOURCE: Eppel/McDowall / C30]

The last great Kurdish prince, Bedirxan of Bohtan, came closest to something bigger. In 1847 he pulled the neighbouring emirs into a league that Ottoman officers said openly "dreamt of establishing an independent state." [SOURCE: Ateş / C33] The closest the Kurds ever came to unity. And it was crushed — after Bedirxan's own cousin defected and guided the imperial army to his stronghold.

By 1850 the emirates were gone. And the consequence is the thing the rest of this story hangs on. Destroy the princes and you don't get a modern province — you get a vacuum. Power fell back to the tribes and the religious sheikhs, what one scholar calls a "rapid devolution," society stepping back down the ladder. [SOURCE: van Bruinessen / C23] The Kurds didn't enter the modern age too fractured to build a state. They were fractured in large part because their states had just been demolished — though some historians put part of it down to the Kurds' own divisions, the tribal rivalries that never quite cohered into a nation.

The first man who tried to put it back together was a sheikh named Ubeydullah, in 1880: "We are a nation apart. We want our affairs to be in our own hands, like other nations." [SOURCE: Cambridge / C32] He failed. But he'd named the thing — thirty years before the world would carve up his region, with no king left to speak for it.

## ACT 3 — THE FATAL WINDOW  [C22, C6, C19, C15, C8, C9]

World War I ended the Ottoman Empire, and the winners sat down to redraw the Middle East. This was the Kurds' one chance. And they walked into it with no card to play.

Compare them to the Arabs. The Arabs had the Sharif of Mecca — a descendant of the Prophet, guardian of the holy cities — who revolted against the Ottomans and won a written British promise and a seat at the Paris Peace Conference. [SOURCE: McMahon–Hussein notebook / C22] The Kurds had fought *for* the Ottomans, not against — hundreds of thousands died in Ottoman uniform. [SOURCE: Cambridge/McDowall / C22] Their one man in Paris was brushed off by a British official as "a Parisian pamphleteer" with no following back home. The Kurdish case was so faint that Lloyd George, listing the lands to be carved off, simply forgot Kurdistan. [SOURCE: MacMillan / C22]

Now, the famous part — the part everyone gets half-right. In 1920, there really was a treaty that promised the Kurds a state. The Treaty of Sèvres. [SHOW: Sèvres "Section III — KURDISTAN" page] It has a section headed, in capital letters, "KURDISTAN," and an article dangling independence. [SOURCE: treaty text / C6]

But read it carefully. The independence was conditional, ran on a one-year clock, and hinged on a League panel deciding the Kurds were "capable" of it. And the treaty was never ratified — the Turkish nationalists tore it up. Sèvres, one historian writes, was "void before the ink was dry." [SOURCE: McDowall/Tusan / C19] So Sèvres wasn't a promise betrayed. It was a promise that was never real.

The real document is the one that replaced it. The Treaty of Lausanne, 1923. And here's where it gets interesting — because the erasure is right there in the wording. [SHOW: Lausanne minority articles] Britain's Lord Curzon actually argued for the Kurds, calling them a people who "ought to be an autonomous race." Turkey's İsmet İnönü insisted the Kurds and Turks were "a single unit in race, religion and manners" — one people, no minority, nothing to protect. [SOURCE: Lausanne Proceedings / C15] [NLM-VERIFIED: Lausanne Proceedings (src 7d37c3f5) — Curzon "ought to be an autonomous race" p.357; İnönü "form a single unit in respect of race, religion and manners" p.343. ON-SCREEN QUOTE CARDS: use full verbatim incl. "in respect of".]

And look at what the treaty does. [SHOW: Article 39] It protects "non-Moslem minorities." Greeks, Armenians, Jews — protected by name. The Kurds are Muslims. Which means they fall outside the category completely. Not one line. The erasure wasn't an accident. It was a definition. [SOURCE: treaty text/Tusan / C8]

Why did Britain let it happen? It wanted oil-rich Mosul folded into its new Iraq, and Turkey's signature was the price. [SOURCE: Sluglett/Radpey / C9] Though, to be fair, the Kirkuk oil wasn't even struck until 1927 — after the deal. A strategic bet on a workable Iraq, with oil as the promising wager, not yet a proven prize. [SOURCE: Pedersen / C27]

Either way, the result was the same. A people who'd had kingdoms for three centuries got a treaty that didn't mention them by name.

## ACT 4 — THE COST, AND THE RHYME  [C36, C35, C31, M1–M3, G1]

From there, the twentieth century becomes the story you already know. The Kurds get used and dropped. Armed by America and the Shah in the 1970s, then sold out overnight the moment the Shah cut a border deal with Saddam. Congress's own investigators didn't flinch. The Pike Committee called it "a cynical enterprise, even in the context of a clandestine aid operation" — and found that "neither the Shah nor the President and Dr. Kissinger desired victory for our clients." Washington wanted the Kurds bleeding Iraq, not winning. [SHOW: Pike Committee report, 1976] Kissinger's defense: "covert action should not be confused with missionary work." C36/C39.] Called to rise against Saddam in 1991, then left to be slaughtered. "No friends but the mountains," the saying goes. [SOURCE: Cambridge / C36]

And it reaches its darkest point in 1988. Saddam's Anfal campaign — chemical and conventional attacks that destroyed around four thousand villages and killed somewhere between one hundred and one hundred eighty thousand people. [SOURCE: McDowall/Cambridge / C35] At Halabja, in a single day, gas killed about five thousand civilians. Survivors said the air smelled of apple and garlic. A Kurdish filmmaker who walked in afterward found "a dead woman holding her son, her arm outstretched as if to beg for help." [SOURCE: McDowall / C35]

And the world stayed quiet — because Saddam was the West's man against Iran. A US sanctions bill died partly because American farmers wanted to keep selling wheat to Baghdad — while the West had helped supply the gas in the first place, a historian writes, "knowingly." [SOURCE: McDowall / C35]

But the deeper truth is this. The Kurds were always the pawn and never the player — not because they wouldn't fight, but because they had no state to fight from. The empires had made sure of that back in 1850. To all of them — Ottoman, British, Iraqi — the Kurds were more useful divided and in place: a buffer, an auxiliary, a borderland on someone else's oil. Never a country. [SOURCE: McDowall/Eppel / C31]

And if you want to know whether that's history or something still happening — look at January 2026. The last patch of Kurdish self-rule, in northern Syria, was just dismantled and folded back into the state. In a single decree — Decree Number Thirteen — the Kurds got citizenship, and their language recognized as "national." They did not get a country. And the oil fields, in the east, went back to the capital. [SOURCE: MEC / Al Jazeera / C40] A hundred years after Lausanne, the same deal: rights from above, never a state of their own.

The Kurds gave Islam its greatest king. They built kingdoms that minted their own coins. [SHOW: the coin again] The one thing they were never allowed to keep was the one thing every empire around them needed them not to have.

A country.
