The most famous Muslim hero of the Crusades was a Kurd.

Saladin — the sultan who took back Jerusalem, the warrior even Richard the Lionheart admired — came from a Kurdish family. His dynasty, the Ayyubids, was, in one historian's words, "the most powerful Kurdish dynasty in the history of Islam."

And his people are the largest nation on Earth without a country. More than thirty million Kurds, spread across Turkey, Iran, Iraq and Syria — and not one state of their own.

Here's how sharp that irony cuts. Saddam Hussein was born in the same town as Saladin — Tikrit. And he built a cult on it: postage stamps with the two of them side by side, a 1987 conference titled "From Saladin to Saddam Hussein." He called himself Saladin the Second — and quietly erased the one detail that didn't fit. That Saladin was Kurdish.

One year after that conference, in 1988, Saddam's army gassed the Kurds.

So how does the people that gave Islam its greatest king end up with nothing? You've probably heard the answer: betrayed by everyone, carved up by Sykes-Picot, a people who simply never had a state.

Except that last part isn't true. The Kurds didn't fail to build a state. They built dozens. And then they watched them taken apart.

Start with the thing nobody tells you. "Kurdistan" is not a modern invention. An 11th-century geographer drew "the land of the Kurds"; the Seljuk Turks named a province "Kurdistan" in the 1150s. And in 1597 a Kurdish prince, Sharaf Khan, wrote a history of his own people's ruling houses — the Sharafnama. A Kurdish dynasty writing down its own kings, four centuries ago.

So what were these kings?

After 1514, when the Ottomans beat the Persians at the battle of Chaldiran, an Ottoman diplomat named Idris Bitlisi cut a deal with the Kurdish princes. Back the sultan against Persia, and you keep your lands. The result was a string of Kurdish principalities — call them emirates — that governed themselves for the next three hundred years.

And I don't mean lightly. The top tier had a status the Ottomans called hükûmet — government: they paid no tax to the capital, took no inspectors, owed no soldiers to the imperial army. The emirate of Bitlis ran its own courts and could field twelve thousand cavalry.

Here's the detail that ends the argument. One of these princes — Muhammad Kor of Soran — minted his own coins, with his own name on them. He had the Friday prayer read in his name, the medieval signature of a sovereign ruler. He built workshops that cast his own cannons.

That is not a tribe. That is a state.

Now, the honest part — because this matters. These were never one country. They were rival statelets that fought each other and bowed, on paper, to a sultan or a shah. As one historian puts it, the Kurds were "never united under one political unit named Kurdistan." So hold both halves of the truth at once. The Kurds had states. They never had a single nation-state. Remember that — it's the whole story.

So if the Kurds had states, the real question isn't why they never built one. It's who took the ones they had.

The answer is the empire they served. In the 1830s and 40s a modernizing Ottoman state could no longer tolerate princes who paid no tax and raised their own armies. So it came for the emirates — historians call it a second Ottoman conquest.

Watch how they did it. Muhammad Kor — the prince who minted his own coins — was surrounded in 1836. The Ottoman commander sent him a Quran, sealed with his own seal, swearing safe conduct and a throne to keep. On that promise he surrendered and sailed for Istanbul, where he spent six months as the sultan's guest. And on the voyage home, across the Black Sea, he simply "disappeared under mysterious circumstances." Murdered, almost certainly. And with him, the emirate of Soran.

The last great Kurdish prince, Bedirxan of Bohtan, came closest to something bigger. In 1847 he pulled the neighbouring emirs into a league that Ottoman officers said openly "dreamt of establishing an independent state." The closest the Kurds ever came to unity. And it was crushed — after Bedirxan's own cousin defected and guided the imperial army to his stronghold.

By 1850 the emirates were gone. And here's the consequence the rest of this story hangs on. Destroy the princes and you don't get a modern province — you get a vacuum. Power fell back to the tribes and the religious sheikhs, what one scholar calls a "rapid devolution," society stepping back down the ladder. The Kurds didn't enter the modern age too fractured to build a state. They were fractured because their states had just been demolished.

The first man who tried to put it back together was a sheikh named Ubeydullah, in 1880: "We are a nation apart. We want our affairs to be in our own hands, like other nations." He failed. But he'd named the thing — thirty years before the world carved up his region, at the worst possible moment for a people with no king left to speak for them.

World War I ended the Ottoman Empire, and the winners sat down to redraw the Middle East. This was the Kurds' one chance. And they walked into it with no card to play.

Compare them to the Arabs. The Arabs had the Sharif of Mecca — a descendant of the Prophet, guardian of the holy cities — who revolted against the Ottomans and won a written British promise and a seat at the Paris Peace Conference. The Kurds had fought for the Ottomans, not against — hundreds of thousands died in Ottoman uniform. Their one man in Paris was brushed off by a British official as "a Parisian pamphleteer" with no following back home. The Kurdish case was so faint that Lloyd George, listing the lands to be carved off, simply forgot Kurdistan.

Now, the famous part — the part everyone gets half-right. In 1920, there really was a treaty that promised the Kurds a state. The Treaty of Sèvres. It has a section headed, in capital letters, "KURDISTAN," and an article dangling independence.

But read it carefully. The independence was conditional, ran on a one-year clock, and hinged on a League panel deciding the Kurds were "capable" of it. And the treaty was never ratified — the Turkish nationalists tore it up. Sèvres, one historian writes, was "void before the ink was dry." So Sèvres wasn't a promise betrayed. It was a promise that was never real.

The real document is the one that replaced it. The Treaty of Lausanne, 1923. And here's where it gets interesting — because the erasure is right there in the wording. Britain's Lord Curzon actually argued for the Kurds, calling them a people who "ought to be an autonomous race." Turkey's İsmet İnönü insisted the Kurds and Turks were "a single unit in race, religion and manners" — one people, no minority, nothing to protect.

And look at what the treaty does. It protects "non-Moslem minorities." Greeks, Armenians, Jews — protected by name. The Kurds are Muslims. Which means they fall outside the category completely. Not one line. The erasure wasn't an accident. It was a definition.

Why did Britain let it happen? It wanted oil-rich Mosul folded into its new Iraq, and Turkey's signature was the price. Though, to be fair, the Kirkuk oil wasn't even struck until 1927 — after the deal. A strategic bet on a workable Iraq, with oil as the promising wager, not yet a proven prize.

Either way, the result was the same. A people who'd had kingdoms for three centuries got a treaty that didn't mention them by name.

From there, the twentieth century becomes the story you already know. The Kurds get used and dropped. Armed by America and the Shah in the 1970s, then sold out overnight — Kissinger's own defense was that "covert action should not be confused with missionary work." Called to rise against Saddam in 1991, then left to be slaughtered. "No friends but the mountains," the saying goes.

And it reaches its darkest point in 1988. Saddam's Anfal campaign — chemical and conventional attacks that destroyed around four thousand villages and killed somewhere between one hundred and one hundred eighty thousand people. At Halabja, in a single day, gas killed about five thousand civilians. Survivors said the air smelled of apple and garlic. A Kurdish filmmaker who walked in afterward found "a dead woman holding her son, her arm outstretched as if to beg for help."

And the world stayed quiet — because Saddam was the West's man against Iran. A US sanctions bill died partly because American farmers wanted to keep selling wheat to Baghdad; Britain doubled its credit to Iraq the year after the gassing — having helped supply the gas, a historian writes, "knowingly."

But here's the deeper truth. The Kurds were always the pawn and never the player — not because they wouldn't fight, but because they had no state to fight from. The empires had made sure of that back in 1850. To all of them — Ottoman, British, Iraqi — the Kurds were more useful divided and in place: a buffer, an auxiliary, a borderland on someone else's oil. Never a country.

And if you want to know whether that's history or something still happening — look at January 2026. The last patch of Kurdish self-rule, in northern Syria, was just dismantled and folded back into the state. The Kurds got citizenship, and a law recognizing their language. They did not get a country. And the oil fields went back to the capital. A hundred years after Lausanne, the same deal: rights from above, never a state of their own.

The Kurds gave Islam its greatest king. They built kingdoms that minted their own coins. The one thing they were never allowed to keep was the one thing every empire around them needed them not to have.

A country.
