### What's structurally new (not in v1)

1. **"Everyman Empathy" tone calibration.** The new corpus demonstrates an explicit anti-defensiveness register — phrases like Miano's *"don't think of this video as an attack on him... Graham seems like a very nice guy I have no problem with him personally... this is an analysis of Graham Hancock's rhetorical strategies, not about his character."* This protects the conspiracy-curious viewer from defensiveness while still allowing forensic critique of the ideas. The Shaun model attacks the villain; the Miano model defends the person and dismantles the idea. **For #57, this is the most important single change.** The audience overlap with Hancock fans is too high to use the Shaun register.

2. **"Everyman Proxy" treatment.** Miano uses Joe Rogan as proxy for the viewer: *"Joe Rogan is the Everyman. He represents the average person with the same kinds of feelings, thoughts, and biases that we all have."* This lets him critique Rogan's reactions without attacking the viewer who has had those reactions themselves. For #57: frame the "you might have seen this on JRE in August 2025" viewer as someone whose initial curiosity is reasonable — then walk them through what the document actually says.

3. **"Reaction-Style Forensic Interrogation."** Miano shows Hancock clips with episode number and timestamp on screen, explicitly says *"I will show on screen both the number of the episode and the timestamp so that you can check up on me to make sure"* — building enormous credibility with the Rogan audience by inviting them to verify. For #57: show the JRE August 2025 clip with timestamp + show the Ottoman text with McIntosh page number. Same epistemic move applied to opposite material.

4. **Front-loaded conceptual reframe.** Stefan Milo doesn't list facts first — he introduces the "Problem of Demarcation" (Popper's distinction between science and pseudoscience) in the first 2 minutes as a *mental tool* the viewer uses for the rest of the video. For #57 the equivalent is: front-load "this is how primary-source historiography actually works — single-witness testimony + cartometric corroboration" as the lens. Then the Spanish-prisoner beat and the 20/14/34 beat both land as confirmations of that lens, not as isolated nuances.

5. **Meta-debunk move.** Miano's entire JRE video is NOT about debunking Hancock's science — it's about debunking Hancock's *persuasion tactics*. This is a fascinating choice for #57 to consider: the video could be framed as "here's what the document says AND here's the rhetorical pattern by which the document gets ignored." Higher difficulty but higher payoff if you can execute it.

## Detailed pattern → script mapping for #57

### The cold open

**Decision required:** Choose between two competing opens — both have strong corpus support.

**Option A — Rule 1C (Standard Myth Subversion):**
Open with Hancock's claim narrated straight-faced. ~45 seconds. Then pivot to "Here is what Piri Reis actually wrote on the map." This is the Shaun *Bell Curve* template — read the opponent's strongest framing fairly, THEN demolish.

> "In August 2025, Graham Hancock told 14 million Joe Rogan listeners that this map — drawn in 1513 by an Ottoman admiral — shows Antarctica before its discovery. Charles Hapgood proved it in 1966. The coastline matches sub-glacial Antarctica. The implication: a lost civilization mapped the world before the last ice age. Hancock is one of the most popular history communicators alive. He is wrong. And the document that proves it is the same one he is showing his audience."

Pros: Establishes you as fair referee. Pre-empts the "you're not even taking the claim seriously" objection. The pivot to the document IS the document hook.

Cons: ~45 seconds of myth amplification before the reveal. Anyone who drops in the first 30s gets only Hancock's version.

**Option B — Rule 1D (Primary Source Immersion):**
Open with Piri Reis's voice. The colophon translated.

> "*'This map was drawn by the poor man Piri son of Haji Mehmet and the nephew of Kemal Reis — may Allah have mercy on them both — in the town of Gelibolu, in the month of Muharram of the year 919.'* Piri Reis signed his map in 1513. Then he wrote down — in Ottoman Turkish, directly on the parchment — exactly where every piece of his information came from. Twenty source maps. Named. Numbered. Dated. In the 500 years since, no popular video has read you what he wrote. Today, we do."

Pros: Document-first. Audience meets the protagonist (Piri Reis) before any debunk framing. Zero myth amplification.

Cons: Slower hook. The Hancock/Antarctica through-line has to arrive in Scene 2.

**Recommendation:** Option B is more on-channel-voice. The "Calm Prosecutor" leads with evidence, not with the opponent. Option A is the higher-CTR choice but it borrows Hancock's framing in the first 30 seconds, which is the move the channel has historically avoided. Run /opener to score both empirically before locking.

### The "20 / 14 / 34 sources" beat — structural placement

This beat fits Pattern 8 ("make the gap interesting"). Place it between Scene 2 (source list walkthrough) and Scene 3 (Columbus identification). The set-up is "Piri Reis wrote down twenty maps." The reveal is "Except scholars have spent 90 years arguing about whether 'twenty' was the total or whether he meant twenty additional maps on top of the fourteen he named — making it 34. The honest historians of Ottoman cartography are still arguing this. The pseudohistorians don't even know the question exists."

This is exactly the Atun-Shei "physical archaeology vs Hancock's rhetorical authority" move — the real scholarship has live disagreements about boring questions; the pseudohistory has no questions at all.

### The Spanish-prisoner chain-of-custody beat — Pattern 8 again

Same register. Place inside Scene 3, AFTER the unique place-names corroboration (Porta ghande = Guantanamo Bay). McIntosh's verbatim caveat ("a certain amount of uncertainty and hesitancy must enter into the discussion. Words such as *probably*, *seems likely*, *apparently*, and *plausibly* must be used") is itself the "Calm Prosecutor" voice in published form. Lift it. Cite McIntosh by name and page when delivered.

### The Antarctica debunk — Patterns 4 + 6 + 7

- **Pattern 4 (Perpetrator ID):** Sequence the three names. Mallery (Aug 26, 1956) → Hapgood (1966) → Hancock (2025). Three perpetrators, three documents, three dates. This is the Flat Earth #19 template applied to a 70-year-old pseudoscience claim.
- **Pattern 6 (Evidence display trigger):** "Here's what inscription no. 10 actually says. The text Piri Reis wrote on the supposed Antarctica." Then the McIntosh translation verbatim, on screen.
- **Pattern 7 (Quote-pivot):** Don't let "large serpents... very hot" stand alone. Land with: "Piri Reis described the southern continent as hot, populated, and visited by Portuguese ships. He was right. He was describing Brazil. Hapgood read past it."

### The closer — Pattern 5A (Philosophical Synthesis)

Avoid the "epistemic helplessness" close ("we may never know"). The flat-earth video's closer pattern is documented as working: pivot from past myth to present consequence.

For #57, the channel-fit close is the meta-lesson: **the lost civilization the pseudohistorians keep looking for was never on the map. The actual lost civilization — Columbus's lost Caribbean cartography — is the thing they walked past to chase Antarctica. They were standing in the archive and they couldn't read it.**

This is the inversion close. It returns to the cold open's promise ("today, we read what he wrote"), it reframes the entire genre (lost civilization-hunting as a literacy problem, not an evidence problem), and it doesn't ask the viewer to decide — it tells them what the document said.

## Practical script-writing rules distilled from the corpus

These are translated directly from the Wave 2 corpus answer and the flat-earth template. Apply as a checklist when drafting #57.

1. **Open with Pattern 1C or 1D, never 1E (humor/tonal contrast).** The "Calm Prosecutor" voice dies on the first joke.
2. **Every major quote gets a 1-sentence spoken credential introduction.** Not on-screen tag — *spoken*. "Gregory McIntosh, Professor of History at the University of Georgia, writes..."
3. **No quote stands alone.** Every primary-source reveal lands on a "So what?" sentence in the channel's voice. Decode the jargon. Translate the implication.
4. **Identify the perpetrators by name and date.** Mallery 1956, Hapgood 1966, Hancock 2025. Three documents. Three dates. Three names on screen.
5. **Frame uncertainty as a shared problem, not a knowledge gap.** "Scholars have argued about this for ninety years" is more compelling than "we don't know."
6. **Let the villain speak.** Quote Hapgood's dedication to Mallery verbatim. Let his own book confirm that this is a 1956 invention.
7. **Decoder phrases after primary sources.** "Basically, what this means is..." after Ottoman text. "In other words..." after McIntosh.
8. **Close on philosophical synthesis, not "you decide."** The video has an answer. Deliver it.
