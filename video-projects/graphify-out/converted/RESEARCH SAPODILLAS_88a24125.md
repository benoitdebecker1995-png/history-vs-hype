In the Application Instituting Proceedings filed by Belize against Honduras at the International Court of Justice (ICJ) on November 16, 2022, Belize identifies a critical 1913 communication as evidence of Honduras’s long-standing recognition of British sovereignty over the Sapodilla Cayes.
1. The EXACT QUOTE from the 1913 Document
The Belize Application quotes the Foreign Minister of Honduras explicitly describing the Sapodilla Cayes as:
“Islands belonging to the Colony”
The letter also expresses gratitude to the United Kingdom for placing a ship of war at the disposal of the “Colonial Government at Belize” to patrol these waters.
2. Document Reference
Source: Application Instituting Proceedings: Sovereignty over the Sapodilla Cayes (Belize v. Honduras).
Location: Paragraph 11, Page 7.
Annexed as: Annex 1 (Letter from Mariano Vásquez, Minister of Foreign Affairs, Honduras, to J. P. Armstrong, British Consul General, 8 September 1913).
3. Context and Identification
Author: Mariano Vásquez, the Minister of Foreign Affairs of Honduras, writing on behalf of the President of Honduras.
Recipient: J. P. Armstrong, the British Consul General.
Context: The Honduran government was concerned that "revolutionary movements plotting against it" were using the Sapodilla Cayes as a base of operations.
The Request: Honduras requested that British authorities in Belize take action against these revolutionaries. Honduras thanked the UK for using a "ship of war" to prevent this activity, effectively asking the UK to exercise its sovereign police power over the islands.
4. Other Honduran Acknowledgments
Belize asserts that prior to 1981, Honduras "repeatedly acknowledged British sovereignty" and "never had nor purported to exercise any sovereignty over the Sapodilla Cayes".
Honduras requested British intervention on multiple occasions when threatened by revolutionary groups using the Cayes as a base.
Belize argues that "Honduras had long recognised and accepted the sovereignty of the United Kingdom" for well over a century.
Honduras only made its first formal claim to the islands in March 1981, just as Belize’s independence was imminent.
5. Legal Use of the Letter (Acquiescence and Recognition)
Belize uses this letter to establish a legal foundation of recognition and acquiescence to defeat Honduras's 1982 constitutional claim:
Recognition of Title: Belize argues that the 1913 request “plainly constituted acknowledgement by Honduras of the sovereignty of the United Kingdom over the Sapodilla Cayes”.
Invitation to Exercise Sovereignty: By asking the British to use a warship to stop revolutionaries on the Cayes, Honduras was making a formal “request for the United Kingdom to exercise its sovereignty” over that specific territory.
Estoppel/Stability of Title: By showing that Honduras viewed the islands as "belonging to the Colony" for at least 68 years (1913–1981), Belize intends to demonstrate that the 1982 Honduran Constitution—which claims the Sapodilla Cayes as Honduran territory—has “no basis in international law”.

Based on the provided documents, here is the analysis of the 1859 Wyke-Aycinena Treaty and Guatemala’s subsequent claims regarding the Sapodilla Cayes.
### 1. Does the treaty define ANY maritime boundary?
No. The 1859 Treaty is strictly a boundary treaty defining land and river limits; it does not establish a maritime boundary in the Caribbean Sea. The text of Article 1 begins the line at the "mouth of the River Sarstoon" and proceeds inland. The ICJ has confirmed in parallel jurisprudence that the establishment of land boundaries does not inherently delimit the maritime space.
### 2. Does the treaty mention the Sapodilla Cayes?
No. The 1859 Treaty makes no mention of the Sapodilla Cayes, any offshore islands, or cays in the Gulf of Honduras. Legal experts note that during the 1859 negotiations, it was deemed "unnecessary to describe, in a treaty with Guatemala, the sea-frontier". Guatemala specifically argues today that because the Sapodilla Cayes "were not included in the British-Guatemalan Treaty of 1859," they remained part of the territory it inherited from Spain.
### 3. The "islands" in Article 1 and 6: River or Offshore?
The "islands" mentioned in the treaty are river islands, not offshore cayes. Article 6 refers to "any islands which may be found therein," meaning within the "channels in the waterline of the boundary" described in Article 1 (the Sarstoon River). These islands belong to whichever party is on the side of the main navigable channel.
### 4. Significance of the treaty being land-only
The significance is that the 1859 Treaty failed to provide a "conventional line" for the offshore territory, creating a distinct legal dispute. Because the treaty drew a line on land and stopped at the river mouth, the sovereignty of the Sapodilla Cayes must be determined by other sources of international law, such as uti possidetis juris or effective occupation (effectivités). This omission is the reason the Sapodilla Cayes are currently the subject of a separate ICJ case (Belize v. Honduras) in which Guatemala has intervened.
### 5. How does Guatemala connect the 1859 Treaty to the Sapodillas?
Guatemala argues that the 1859 Treaty was a limited instrument that only addressed the land boundaries of the British "Settlement" and did not extend to the insular territory. Furthermore, Guatemala contends that because Britain breached Article 7 (the road obligation), the entire 1859 Treaty is void, meaning any territory—including the land and the islands—should "revert" to Guatemala.
### 6. Does Guatemala argue the Cayes were part of uti possidetis juris?
Yes. Guatemala contends that the Sapodilla Cayes belonged to the Spanish Crown (specifically within the Province of Verapaz or the Captaincy-General of Guatemala) and that Guatemala succeeded to this sovereignty in 1821 via the principle of uti possidetis juris. It asserts that the Cayes followed the "fate of the adjacent continental coast".
### 7. Is the claim based on the treaty or Spanish sovereignty?
Guatemala’s claim to the Sapodilla Cayes is based on pre-1859 Spanish sovereignty. In its view, the 1859 Treaty did not cede the islands to Britain, and even if it had, the treaty's alleged nullity would cause the islands to revert to the original Spanish/Guatemalan title. As legal experts have summarized, Guatemala argues the islands "were not included in the British-Guatemalan Treaty of 1859" and therefore remained under the rights Guatemala "inherited from Spain".

Strategic Role: It was situated on a "sea trade route between the highland supplies and the lowland consumers" ****.
Regional Hub: It acted as a "near-shore connection in the triad of coastal trading ports" (with Frenchman's Cay and Green Vine Snake) that "controlled travel through the coastal waters of Port Honduras in ancient times" ****.
Economic Reorientation: After the collapse of inland cities like Lubaantún, the port "reoriented their trading with emerging powers in the northern Yucatan, dominated by the Maya at Chichen Itza" ****.

Historical Context: While these specific excerpts summarize the trade, archaeological records of this encounter (often cited by McKillop) describe a large trading canoe, roughly the size of a galley (8 feet wide), carrying a "cargo of cacao, copper axes and bells, obsidian blades, and colorful cotton textiles" (Note: Cargo details are standard archaeological history for the 1502 encounter cited in maritime Maya studies).
5. Duration of Maya Maritime Use
Maya communities were active in these waters for centuries before European contact:
Earliest Evidence: The Butterfly Wing site on the Deep River marks the beginning of coastal trade in the region as early as 300 b.c., during the Late Preclassic ****.

Based on the text of the Convention of London (1786) and the historical analysis provided in the sources, here is the breakdown of the geographic and sovereign limits of that era.
1. Geographic Limits of the 1786 Convention
The 1786 Convention defined the British "Settlement" (not a colony at the time) through specific river boundaries. According to Article II, the line began at the sea and followed the "centre of the river Sibun or Jabon" to its source, then crossed in a straight line to the Wallis (Belize) River, and followed the center of that river to join the limits previously set in 1783. These limits were intended to be "unalterable boundaries" to prevent trespassing into Spanish territories.
2. The Hondo-Sibun Corridor
Correct. The 1783 Treaty of Versailles had originally limited British woodcutting to the area between the Hondo River (northern boundary) and the Belize River. The 1786 Convention extended this southern limit further down the coast to the Sibun River.
3. Offshore Islands and Maritime Areas
The Convention did not establish a general maritime boundary, but it did address specific islands:
St. George’s Caye: Article IV allowed the British to occupy "Casina, St. George’s Key, or Cayo Casina" for "purposes of real utility" (health/recreation) but strictly prohibited any fortifications or military presence.
Southern Triangle: Article V granted the liberty to refit merchant ships in a specific cluster of small islands known as the "southern triangle".
Fishing Rights: Article VI allowed the British to "peaceably catch fish" on the coast of the assigned territory and opposite islands, provided they did not establish permanent settlements on those islands.
Silence on the Sapodillas: The 1786 text does not mention the Sapodilla Cayes, which are located significantly further south of the Sibun River limits.
4. Status as "Concessionaires"
This is accurate. The 1786 Convention was explicit that Britain did not have sovereignty over the territory.
Nominal Sovereignty: Article VII states that all lands in question were "indisputably acknowledged to belong of right to the Crown of Spain".
Restricted Rights: The British were granted only the "liberty of cutting all other wood" and gathering "produce of the earth, purely natural and uncultivated".
Prohibitions: They were expressly forbidden from establishing a "system of government, either military or civil," engaging in agriculture, or building fortifications.
5. Extension of Control South to the Sarstoon
The Sapodilla Cayes and the Sarstoon River are far south of the 1786 Sibun limit. British control extended south through a process of de facto expansion and military victory:
1798 Battle of St. George’s Caye: This was the last Spanish military attempt to dislodge the settlers. The Spanish defeat marked the end of effective Spanish power in the area.
1799–1800: British woodcutters had already pushed south to Deep River.
1802–1806: Settlers reached Stann Creek and the Rio Grande.
1814–1816: Possession extended to the Moho River, just north of the Sarstoon.
1825: The Sarstoon River was officially regarded by the British Superintendent as the southern boundary of the settlement.
1859 Treaty: This expansion was finally formalized in the Wyke-Aycinena Treaty, which for the first time defined the international boundary as beginning at the mouth of the River Sarstoon.
This timeline confirms that while the 1786 Convention confined the British to the Hondo-Sibun area as "concessionaires," the current southern boundaries (and control over the Sapodilla Cayes) were established through 19th-century occupation that ignored the earlier Spanish treaty limits.
The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) established a legal framework that turned tiny maritime features into high-stakes economic assets. Based on the Convention text and its application in landmark cases like Nicaragua v. Colombia, here is the breakdown of the "island vs. rock" distinction and its implications for the Sapodilla Cayes.
1. The Distinction under Article 121
According to Article 121 of UNCLOS, the distinction is based on two criteria: physical form and the ability to support life.
Island (Paragraph 1): Defined as "a naturally formed area of land, surrounded by water, which is above water at high tide".
Rock (Paragraph 3): These are a specific sub-category of islands. The text defines them as "rocks which cannot sustain human habitation or economic life of their own".
2. Maritime Entitlements: Correct
Your assessment is correct. The legal status of a feature determines the "reach" of its sovereign rights into the sea:
Islands that can sustain habitation or economic life generate a full suite of maritime zones, including a 12nm territorial sea, a 24nm contiguous zone, a 200nm Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ), and a continental shelf.
Rocks that fall under Article 121(3) are explicitly denied an EEZ and a continental shelf. They are limited to a 12nm territorial sea.
3. Are the Sapodilla Cayes "Islands" or "Rocks"?
The Sapodilla Cayes (the largest being roughly 300 meters across) sit at the threshold of this definition.
Physical Status: Because they are naturally formed and remain above water at high tide, they technically meet the Paragraph 1 definition of an island.
Functional Status: Whether they are "rocks" depends on their ability to sustain habitation. In Nicaragua v. Colombia, the ICJ examined cays of almost identical size—such as Roncador (550m x 300m) and Low Cay (300m x 40m). While the Court held these were islands capable of appropriation, it often avoided a final ruling on their 121(3) status if their maritime effect could be limited through other means.
Practical Application: In the Belize dispute, the presence of permanent structures (like the Hunting Caye base) and historical residency (like the Cabral family on Lime Caye) would be Belize's primary evidence to argue they are not "rocks" and should generate a full EEZ.
4. The Economic Difference
The economic gap between an "island" and a "rock" is immense:
Rock (Territorial Sea only): A 12nm radius covers approximately 450 square nautical miles of ocean.
Island (Full EEZ): A 200nm EEZ can cover up to 125,000 square nautical miles.
Value: This area includes exclusive rights to all "living and non-living resources," including fisheries, minerals, and potential hydrocarbon (oil and gas) deposits. For the Sapodilla Cayes, an EEZ would grant the owner control over the lucrative fishing grounds of the Gulf of Honduras and any subsoil resources in the continental shelf.
5. Relevant Tribunal Rulings
The ICJ and other tribunals have ruled on this specific distinction to prevent "tiny features" from unfairly "distorting" maritime boundaries:
Nicaragua v. Colombia (2012): The Court ruled that QS 32 (a tiny feature at Quitasueño) was a rock that generated no EEZ. Furthermore, the Court enclaved larger cays (Roncador and Serrana) with only 12nm territorial seas, even without formally labeling them rocks, because giving them a full EEZ would have an "out of all proportion" effect on the maritime boundary.
South China Sea Arbitration (2016): An Annex VII tribunal famously ruled that none of the features in the Spratly Islands were capable of sustaining human habitation or economic life, classifying them as "rocks" under Article 121(3) and denying them an EEZ.
Conclusion for your script: UNCLOS created a "lottery" for coastal states. If the 14 Sapodilla Cayes are judged as "islands," they project a 200-mile economic empire; if they are "rocks," they are merely 12-mile bubbles of sovereignty. The difference is billions of dollars in resources.
