Three Accounts of the Three Hundred: The Thermopylae Perspectives
1. AUTHOR PROFILES
Herodotus of Halicarnassus was born c. 484 BCE in a Greek-Carian city under Persian rule, writing his Histories approximately 40 to 50 years after the battle [1, 2]. He is known as the "Father of History" for his pioneering "inquiry" (historiē), though critics like Plutarch labeled him the "Father of Lies" due to his perceived bias towards Athens and his interest in "marvels" [3-5]. Herodotus obtained his information primarily through oral testimony, interviewing veterans in their 60s and 70s to preserve the kleos (fame) of great deeds [1, 6, 7].
Diodorus Siculus, a Sicilian Greek writing in the mid-1st century BCE, composed his Library of History roughly 430 years after Thermopylae [8, 9]. His account is a "composite work" that relies heavily on earlier, now-lost sources, most notably the 4th-century BCE historian Ephorus [8, 10]. Diodorus aims for moral didacticism, framing the Spartan stand as an intentional, noble sacrifice for Panhellenic freedom, and he provides the most famous "alternative" version of the battle—the suicidal night attack on the Persian camp [11-13].
Ctesias of Cnidus was a Greek physician who served at the Persian court of Artaxerxes II for seventeen years, finishing his Persica c. 398/7 BCE, about 80 years after the battle [14-17]. He positioned his work as a direct, "insider" correction to Herodotus, whom he frequently calls a liar and "writer of fables" [18, 19]. Although Ctesias claimed to access "royal records" written on leather, modern scholars believe he primarily used oral traditions from the Persian nobility, resulting in a narrative that is often salacious, muddled, and oddly pro-Persian [20-22].

4. WHAT ALL SOURCES AGREE ON
King Leonidas was the supreme Greek commander and died in the battle [50, 51].
An elite core of 300 Spartans formed the backbone of the defense [26, 51].
The battle took place in the narrow pass of Thermopylae (the "Hot Gates") [52, 53].
The Persians held massive numerical superiority, regardless of whether it was thousands or millions [54, 55].
The Greeks were only defeated after a flanking movement or encirclement (though Ctesias omits the traitor's name) [26, 38, 56].
The Greek rearguard was completely wiped out [36, 50].
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. MAJOR CONTRADICTIONS & WHAT THEY REVEAL
The Final Stand (Day vs. Night): Herodotus describes a daytime defense on a hillock; Diodorus describes a suicidal night attack [35, 36]. This reveals how later sources (like Ephorus) likely embellished the narrative to fit the 4th-century BCE rhetorical tradition of the "heroic suicide" [13, 57].
The Traitor’s Identity: Herodotus names Ephialtes; Diodorus refers to him only as a "Trachinian"; Ctesias ignores the betrayal entirely [29, 37, 39]. This reveals the fragmentary nature of Persian-court memory and the likely focus on royal failure rather than local Greek details in Ctesias’ sources [58].
Casualty Numbers: Ctesias reports "two or three" Spartans died in waves of 10,000 Persians [26]. These "absurd fantasies" show Ctesias’ agenda to prioritize narrative sensation over historical probability to discredit Herodotus [18, 59].
The "Silent" Persian Account: There is no surviving Persian narrative account of Thermopylae [60]. Persian "historiography" was limited to bureaucratic ration tablets (Fortification Archive) and royal proclamations that focused on "Truth" vs. "The Lie" rather than detailed military history [61, 62].
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. HISTORICAL SYNTHESIS
Modern scholars like Paul Cartledge and Rupert Matthew reject the multi-million figures but accept a Persian force of 150,000 to 250,000 [63-65]. They conclude that Thermopylae was part of a coordinated land-sea strategy; the Greek fleet at Artemisium held the straits while Leonidas held the pass to prevent an amphibious landing in the Greek rear [66, 67]. The "Last Stand" was not merely suicidal but a strategic rearguard action necessary to allow the retreating Greek allies to escape the Persian cavalry [68, 69]. As Cartledge argues, the battle provided the "shining cornerstone of freedom" that kept the Greek coalition from collapsing through the moral example of Spartan devotio [51, 70].

The Rhetoric and Authenticity of Thermopylae’s Legendary Sayings
This analysis evaluates the most famous quotes of the Battle of Thermopylae by comparing their earliest recorded appearances, their historical plausibility, and their literary functions in ancient historiography.
1. "Molon labe" (Come and take them)
First Ancient Source: Plutarch (Apophthegmata Laconica / Sayings of Spartans) [1, 2].
Timeline: Recorded roughly 600 years after the battle (1st–2nd century CE) [3].
Confirmation: Not confirmed by the earliest sources. Herodotus, the primary source for the battle, omits this specific phrase entirely [4]. Diodorus Siculus (writing ~430 years after) records a similar rhetorical exchange but phrases Leonidas' response much longer: "The Greeks have learned from their fathers to gain lands, not by cowardice, but by valour" [5-7].
Scholarly View: Modern historians generally consider it a later rhetorical invention intended to exemplify the "Spartan Legend" rather than a verbatim historical record [8, 9].
Literary/Political Function: To demonstrate "Laconic" brevity and the psychological superiority of the Spartans over the "barbarian" King [9].
2. "Then we shall fight in the shade"
First Ancient Source: Herodotus (The Histories, 7.226) [10].
Timeline: Written approximately 40 to 50 years after the battle (~440–425 BCE) [11, 12].
Confirmation: Recorded by Herodotus and later echoed by Plutarch and Valerius Maximus. Notably, Herodotus does not attribute this to Leonidas, but to a Spartan soldier named Dienekes [10, 13].
Scholarly View: Regarded as more plausible than other quotes because it appears in the earliest continuous narrative and is attributed to a specific, non-royal individual, which suggests it may have been part of an actual oral tradition from veterans [10, 13].
Literary/Political Function: It captures the Spartan indifference to overwhelming numbers and their professional focus on the tactical reality of the battle rather than fear [10, 14].
3. "Go tell the Spartans, passerby..." (The Epitaph)
First Ancient Source: Herodotus (The Histories, 7.228), though the poem itself is attributed to the contemporary lyric poet Simonides of Ceos [15-18].
Timeline: Simonides was a contemporary of the battle; Herodotus recorded the inscription roughly 40–50 years later [15, 18].
Confirmation: Well-attested. This quote was a physical inscription on a stone pillar at Thermopylae and is quoted by multiple ancient authors, including Lycurgus, Diodorus, and the Palatine Anthology [15, 16, 19, 20].
Scholarly View: Authentic. Scholars accept this as a genuine historical inscription commissioned by the Amphictyonic League of Delphi to honor the fallen [15-17].
Literary/Political Function: To memorialize "obedience to Spartan law" (nomos) as the supreme virtue that justified their self-sacrificial suicide [8, 21-24].
4. "Eat a good breakfast, for tonight we dine in Hades"
First Ancient Source: Diodorus Siculus (Library of History, 11.9.4) [25].
Timeline: Recorded approximately 430 years after the battle (mid-1st century BCE) [26].
Confirmation: Also found in Plutarch’s late collection of sayings [13, 27]. It is completely absent in Herodotus [28].
Scholarly View: Highly likely to be a rhetorical invention. Scholars note it belongs to a later, more "cinematic" tradition (likely from the lost historian Ephorus) that sought to heighten the drama of the final stand [28, 29].
Literary/Political Function: To emphasize the intentionality of the suicide mission and frame the final stand as a Homeric "beautiful death" [27, 29, 30].

Thermopylae: Modern Historical Perspectives and Strategic Realities
Modern scholarship views the Battle of Thermopylae as a sophisticated tactical operation within a larger amphibious strategy, which was ultimately transformed into a powerful moral victory despite the military defeat.
1. Modern Scholarly Consensus
The consensus among modern historians is that Thermopylae was not a standalone heroic gesture but part of a coordinated land-sea strategy designed to protect the "Hellenic League." The goal was to hold the pass while the Greek fleet engaged the Persians at Artemisium to prevent the outflanking of the land force [1, 2].
Academic Quote on Reconstruction:
> "The actual events which Herodotus records — events known to all — are the best, and in fact the only, remaining evidences of what was planned and why. The Greeks could have had no expectation of defeating the Persian army at Thermopylae. They expected only to delay the Persian army, in the hope that their fleet might defeat the Persians." [1]
2. Specific Claims Rejected by Historians
The Numbers: Herodotus’ claim of over 5 million Persians is universally rejected as logistically impossible [3, 4].
Synchronicity: Historians generally reject Herodotus' claim that the land and sea battles occurred on the exact same three days as a "chronological error" intended to simplify the narrative [5, 6].
Persian Motivation: The claim that Persian troops were driven solely by whips is viewed as Greek propaganda designed to contrast "slavish barbarians" with "free Greeks" [7, 8].
Secret Meetings: Detailed accounts of private conversations between Xerxes and his advisors are dismissed as dramatic reconstructions [9, 10].
3. Specific Claims Accepted by Historians
Topography: Archaeological and geological investigations have confirmed Herodotus’ description of the "Hot Gates," the Middle Gate, and the existence of the Phocian Wall [11-13].
Tactical Superiority: Scholars accept that the disparity in equipment (heavy Greek bronze vs. light Persian wicker) made the Greeks nearly invincible in a frontal assault within a narrow defile [14, 15].
The Path: The existence of the Anopaea mountain path and its role in the Greek defeat is accepted as historical fact [16, 17].
4. Best Modern Estimates for Xerxes' Army
While Herodotus claims 1.7 million infantry [18], modern historians estimate the total land force was between 75,000 and 300,000 [19, 20].
Academic Quote on Logistics:
> "If Xerxes’ army really was as large as Herodotus says, then the column of march would have been 2,000 miles long, with the head of the column reaching Thermopylae in Greece at the same time as the end of the column was marching out of Susa in Iran." [3]
5. Suicidal vs. Strategic Purpose
Scholars argue the "last stand" was both a strategic rearguard action and an intentional act of religious/political devotio.
Strategic: It allowed the retreating allies to escape Xerxes’ cavalry, which would have "cut the retreating Greek army to ribbons" in open country [21, 22].
Symbolic: It provided a necessary "shining cornerstone of freedom" to keep the wavering Greek coalition from collapsing [23, 24].
Academic Quote on Strategic Impact:
> "The Spartans’ heroically suicidal stand at Thermopylae showed that the Persians both should and could usefully be resisted, and gave the small, wavering and uncohesive force of patriotic Greeks the nerve to imagine that they might one day defeat the invaders." [24]
6. Universal Agreement Across Sources
All three ancient sources and modern historians agree on three central pillars of the event:
Leadership: King Leonidas of Sparta was the supreme commander [2, 11.4.2, 7.204].
The Elite Core: An elite force of 300 Spartans formed the backbone of the defense [27, 11.4.5, 7.202].
Encirclement: The Greeks were only defeated after a flanking movement allowed the Persians to attack from the rear [11.10.4, 7.225, 146].

The Silent Empire: Persian Historiography and the Greek Wars
The absence of a Persian narrative account for the Battle of Thermopylae is a foundational problem in Achaemenid studies. While the Greeks developed history as a "reflexive, introspective, cross-culturally comparativist literature," the Persians utilized writing primarily for administrative record-keeping and royal ideological proclamations [1].
1. Persian Sources from Xerxes’ Reign
The surviving evidence from the reign of Xerxes is significant but entirely non-narrative. It consists of:
Royal Inscriptions: Formal declarations carved in stone or on objects, often in trilingual script (Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian). Examples include the Daiva Inscription and inscriptions on calcite jars [2, 3].
Administrative Records: Thousands of clay tablets, such as the Persepolis Fortification Archive and Treasury Texts, which document the "bureaucratic efficiency" of the empire, tracking food rations, travel supplies, and silver disbursements [4].
Art and Reliefs: Monumental architecture and carvings at Persepolis and Susa that depict the King of Kings receiving tribute from "a rainbow of subject nations" [5, 6].
2. Mentions of Thermopylae in Persian Sources
No surviving Persian source mentions the Battle of Thermopylae, either directly or indirectly [7]. Modern historians note that "the general lineaments" of Persian history are reliable, but "surviving non-Greek sources tend to be either non-narrative royal inscriptions or administrative or business documents—quite different in nature and purpose from Herodotus’ narrative history" [8]. Even Ctesias, a Greek who claimed to have used "royal records," provided a version of Thermopylae that omitted key elements like the traitor Ephialtes [9, 10].
3. The Daiva Inscription
The Daiva Inscription is one of the most famous documents from Xerxes' reign. It does not mention the Greek campaign by name but provides the ideological framework Xerxes used to justify his wars as a struggle for religious and imperial order.
Exact Quote: "Among these countries there was a place where previously demons [daiva] had been worshipped. Afterwards . . . there I worshipped Ahura Mazda in accordance with Truth reverently." (Xerxes, Daiva Inscription [2]).
Significance: This inscription portrays the King as the "embodiment of the Truth," engaged in a divinely ordained battle against "the Lie" [11]. It suggests that Xerxes viewed the suppression of rebellious regions (which likely included Greece) not as a matter of historical record, but as a religious imperative to uphold the "moral precepts of Ahura Mazda" [12].
4. Development of Historiography: Persian vs. Greek
The development of historiography in Greece was an intellectual innovation of the 5th century BCE that found no equivalent in Persia.
Academic Quote: "Achaemenid Persia had scribes, many of them, but not one historian. Scribes, paradoxically, made any less restricted form of literacy unnecessary as well as, probably, unwelcome." (Cartledge, p. 32 [13, 14]).
Persian Method: The Persians "comprehended their past in terms of their myths... and the grand tales of gods, heroes, and kings" [15]. Their records were intended to "convey eternal verities about the Great King" rather than "record individual political events" [11].
Greek Method: Herodotus’ "inquiry" (historiē) was a "new, entirely human-based search for knowledge" that relied on critical investigation of causes, a concept alien to the "top-down Persian world" [13, 16].
5. Scholarly Views on the Asymmetry of Sources
Modern scholars describe the lack of a Persian side to the story as an "incurable paradox" that forces us to view a world empire through the eyes of its enemies [17].
Academic Quote on Asymmetry: "In our evidence for the mighty Persian empire and its dealings with Greeks lies an incurable paradox... unfortunately, the Persians have not left us the same kind of reflexive, introspective, cross-culturally comparativist literature that the Greeks, famously, did." (Cartledge, p. xi-xii ).
On the "Silence" of the Persians: "The Persica can be used as a history because it records the way in which the Persians remembered or imagined their past... [but] Ctesias’ omissions and confusions... can be explained perhaps by the idea that his internal sources did not chronicle the events in detail (or at all)." (Robson and Llewellyn-Jones, p. 28 [18, 19]).

Classical Chroniclers of the Persian Wars
HERODOTUS OF HALICARNASSUS
Identity and Origin: Herodotus was born in Halicarnassus, a city in Asia Minor, around 484 BCE [1, 2]. He was later associated with the Athenian colony of Thurii in southern Italy [1]. He published his Histories roughly 40 to 45 years after the Battle of Thermopylae, likely in the 420s BCE [2, 3].
Sources and Travel: Herodotus claimed his work was the result of an "inquiry" (historié) [4, 5]. He traveled extensively through Asia Minor, Egypt, Phoenicia, and North Africa to gather information [1]. For the Persian Wars, he relied on oral testimony from veterans who were in their 60s or 70s when he interviewed them [3, 6].
Agenda and Audience: His goal was to preserve the "great and wonderful deeds" of both Greeks and barbarians so they would not lose their glory [7, 8]. His narrative often highlights the ideological struggle between Greek freedom and oriental despotism [9, 10]. His audience consisted of the general Greek public, and it is believed he performed his work aloud [11].
Modern Reliability: Known as both the "Father of History" and the "Father of Lies" [12, 13]. While scholars appreciate his cultural sensitivity and general narrative arc, his numerical claims (millions of Persians) are viewed as "impossibly high" [14, 15].
Quotable Line: "Herodotus of Halicarnassus here presents his research so that human events do not fade with time." [8]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
DIODORUS SICULUS
Identity and Origin: Born in Agyrium, Sicily, Diodorus lived in the mid-1st century BCE (c. 90–30 BCE) [16, 17]. He wrote his Library of History approximately 430 years after Thermopylae [18].
Sources and Ephorus: Diodorus assembled a "composite work" from many lost sources, most critically the historian Ephorus [19, 20]. Ephorus (c. 405–330 BCE) wrote a 30-book universal history and is noted for being better than many other ancient writers at examining the foundations and kinship of peoples [20, 21].
Divergence from Herodotus: Diodorus clearly diverges from Herodotus by describing the climax of the battle as a suicidal night attack by the Greeks on the Persian camp, intended to assassinate Xerxes [22]. This suggests his source (Ephorus) drew on a different, possibly later rhetorical tradition that sought to further heroize the Spartan stand [23].
Quotable Line: "Commendable deeds performed by exemplary individuals are the hallmark of the Library." [24]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CTESIAS OF CNIDUS
Identity and Origin: Ctesias was from Cnidus, an important medical center in Greek-speaking Caria [25, 26]. He served as a Greek physician at the Persian court of Artaxerxes II for seventeen years, starting around 413 BCE [27, 28]. He finished his Persica around 398/7 BCE, roughly 80 to 90 years after the battle [29-31].
Persian Court Context: His position allowed him special insight into court life, harem politics, and secret plots [32]. His account is "pro-Persian" compared to others, aiming to tell a "history of Persia from the inside" [32, 33].
Persian Records: Ctesias claimed to have consulted the "royal records" (basilikai diphtherai) written on leather [34, 35]. Modern scholars generally doubt he consulted official archives directly, suggesting he likely relied on oral reports from the Persian nobility or Queen Parysatis herself [36-38].
Survival of Fragments: Only fragments survive because his work was later summarized into epitomes by writers like Photius [25, 39]. His reputation for sensationalism, "absurd fantasies," and muddled chronology made his full work less likely to be preserved than that of Herodotus [40-42].
Quotable Line: "He says that for the majority of things that he recounts, he was an eyewitness or... he heard it straight from the Persians themselves." [43]

Classical Testaments to the Battle of Thermopylae
Beyond Herodotus, Diodorus Siculus, and Ctesias, several other ancient sources provide significant information, critiques, or contemporary perspectives on the Battle of Thermopylae.
Simonides of Ceos
As a contemporary lyric poet (c. 556–468 BCE), Simonides is the source of the famous epitaphs and encomiums that immortalized the fallen [1, 2]. While he did not write a continuous narrative, his verses are considered a primary source of the "Thermopylae spirit" and were used by later historians to confirm details [3, 4]. One surviving fragment explicitly identifies the numerical disparity, claiming "four thousand of the Peloponnese once fought with three million" [5].
Plutarch
Writing in the 1st and 2nd centuries CE, Plutarch provides critical supplementary material in several works:
Life of Themistocles: Though focused on the Athenian leader, it discusses the strategic decision to defend the pass [6].
Life of Leonidas: This specific biography is lost, but its existence is noted by modern scholars as a major missing link in the tradition [7].
On the Malice of Herodotus: Plutarch explicitly critiques Herodotus, accusing him of bias and claiming he was less than generous to certain Greek states, particularly the Thebans and Corinthians [8].
Laconic Mottoes: This work preserves famous "apophthegms" or sayings attributed to Leonidas and his wife Gorgo, such as his command to "Marry good men and give birth to good children" before departing for battle [9, 10].
Pausanias the Geographer
Writing in the 2nd century CE, his work Description of Greece provides a detailed topographical and historical account of the site [11, 12]. He records later local traditions, such as the specific list of names of the 300 Spartans that he claimed to have seen on a memorial in Sparta centuries after the battle [13, 14]. He also offers commentary on the betrayal by Ephialtes and the tactical significance of the pass [11].
Ephorus of Cyme
Although his Universal History is lost, Ephorus (c. 405–330 BCE) is arguably the most important "missing" source because he was the primary source for Diodorus Siculus [15, 16]. Modern scholars believe Ephorus provided a more detailed, possibly rhetorical account of the night attack on Xerxes' camp, a detail entirely absent from Herodotus [17-19].
Other Minor or Fragmentary Sources
Pindar: A contemporary poet who wrote a celebratory epic poem about the campaign, now mostly lost, except for a line praising the battle as the "shining cornerstone of freedom" [20, 21].
Aeschylus: While his play The Persians focuses on the Battle of Salamis, it provides the earliest contemporary ideological context for the war as a struggle between Greek freedom and Persian despotism [22-24].
Strabo: The geographer describes the physical "Hot Gates" in detail, including the location of the Phocian Wall and the surrounding mountain forts like Nikaia and Teichious [11, 25, 26].
Lucian: A later writer who adopted an "opposite view," devaluing the heroic ideals of Thermopylae as part of his rhetorical exercises [27].

Comparative Perspectives on the Battle of Thermopylae
This comparison of the accounts of the Battle of Thermopylae provided by Herodotus, Diodorus Siculus, and Ctesias highlights the significant variations in their narrative structures and details across five key moments.
1. The Greek Decision to Defend at Thermopylae
Herodotus (7.175): "The opinion that prevailed over all the others was to defend the pass at Thermopylae, since they saw that it was narrower than the pass into Thessaly and closer to their own territory." [1]
Diodorus Siculus (11.4.1): "The Greeks who were in assembly, when word came to them that the Persian forces were near, took action to dispatch... a considerable body of hoplites to Thermopylae to forestall them in occupying the passes at the narrowest part of the defile and to prevent the barbarians from advancing against Greece" [2]
Ctesias (Fragment 27, 27): "Through Artapanus, who had 10,000 men, Xerxes attacked the Spartan general, Leonidas, at Thermopylae." [3]
Agreement: All sources agree that the decision involved placing a force at Thermopylae to physically block the Persian advance into Greece. Contradiction: Herodotus frames the decision as a strategic pivot following the abandonment of the Tempe line [1, 4]. Diodorus portrays it as a direct assembly decision motivated by the imminent arrival of the enemy and includes a specific dialogue between Leonidas and the Ephors about the small size of the force [2]. Present/Absent: Herodotus notes that the Greeks were initially unaware of the mountain path when they arrived [1]. Ctesias omits the Greek deliberation entirely, jumping directly to the Persian assault [3].
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. The First Days of Fighting
Herodotus (7.210-211): "The Medes charged headlong into the Hellenes, and great numbers of them fell... the Hellenes made it clear to everyone... that although there were many in his army, there were few real men... The Lacedaemonians fought remarkably well... especially whenever they would turn their backs and feign flight all together, and the barbarians, seeing this, would pursue them with much clatter and shouting; the Lacedaemonians would allow the barbarians to catch up with them and then suddenly turn around to face them, at which point they would slay countless numbers of them." [5, 6]
Diodorus Siculus (11.7.2): "For since the men stood shoulder to shoulder in the fighting and the blows were struck in close combat, and the lines were densely packed, for a considerable time the battle was equally balanced. But since the Greeks were superior in valour and in the great size of their shields, the Medes gradually gave way" [7]
Ctesias (Fragment 27, 27): "And the great Persian horde was cut down, while only two or three Spartans were killed. Then Xerxes ordered them to attack with 20,000 men: and these were defeated, too." [3]
Agreement: All three accounts describe a massive slaughter of the Persian vanguard (Medes) while the Greeks suffered very few losses in the initial days. Contradiction: Herodotus highlights the tactic of feigned retreat as the key to Spartan success [6]. Diodorus describes the Greek force as a dense "wall" or phalanx that simply held its ground through superior armor [7]. Ctesias claims absurdly low casualties for the Spartans ("two or three") despite the scale of the Persian waves [3]. Present/Absent: Diodorus mentions Xerxes threatening his own soldiers with death if they fled [8]. Herodotus notes the king leapt from his throne three times in fear for his army [9].
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. The Betrayal by Ephialtes (Mountain Path)
Herodotus (7.213): "The King was at a loss about how to deal with this impasse, but just then Ephialtes of Malis, son of Eurydemos, came to speak with him, expecting to win some great reward for telling the King of the path that led through the mountain to Thermopylae." [10]
Diodorus Siculus (11.8.4): "there came to the king a certain Trachinian, a native of the region, who was familiar with the mountainous area. This man was brought into the presence of Xerxes and promised to lead the Persians through a narrow and precipitous path" [8]
Ctesias: Absent. Modern commentary notes that Ctesias' account "caused the complete omission of the traitor Ephialtes" from the narrative [11].
Agreement: Herodotus and Diodorus agree that a local man enabled the Persians to outflank the Greek position via a secret mountain path. Contradiction: Herodotus identifies the traitor specifically as Ephialtes of Malis [10]. Diodorus refers to him only as "a certain Trachinian" [8]. Present/Absent: Ctesias omits the betrayal entirely, stating instead that Xerxes simply "put an end to the battle" after his frontal assaults failed [3, 11]. Herodotus provides extensive detail on the Phocian guards stationed on the path who were bypassed by the Persians [12].
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. The Final Stand
Herodotus (7.225): "On this spot they tried to defend themselves with their daggers if they still had them, or if not, with their hands and their teeth. The barbarians pelted them with missiles, some running up to face the Hellenes directly and demolishing the defensive wall, and others coming to surround them on all sides." [13]
Diodorus Siculus (11.10.4): "the Persians observing that the Greeks were few in number, viewed them with contempt; the Persians did not, however, join battle with them face to face, fearing their valour, but they formed on their flanks and rear, and shooting arrows and hurling javelins at them from every direction they slew them to a man." [14]
Ctesias: Absent. Ctesias' summary in Fragment 27 does not describe a final stand or the complete destruction of the Greek rearguard [3].
Agreement: Herodotus and Diodorus agree that the remaining Greeks were encircled and eventually killed by Persian missiles rather than in hand-to-hand combat. Contradiction: Diodorus describes the final act as a suicidal night attack by the Greeks on the Persian camp intended to assassinate Xerxes, where they broke into the royal pavilion [15, 16]. Herodotus describes the final stand as a defensive retreat to a hillock during the day [13]. Present/Absent: Diodorus includes the desperate night-time pavilion attack [14, 16]. Herodotus records the specific detail of Greeks fighting with "hands and their teeth" [13]. Ctesias omits the final defeat entirely [3].
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. The Death of Leonidas
Herodotus (7.224): "And it was during this struggle that Leonidas fell, the man who had proved himself the most valiant of all... the Persians and Lacedaemonians engaged in a violent struggle over the corpse until the Hellenes... managed... to drag it out and away from the crowd." [13, 17]
Diodorus Siculus (11.10.4): "Now as for the soldiers of Leonidas who guarded the passes of Thermopylae, such was the end of life they met." [14]
Ctesias: Absent. Ctesias' summary mentions Leonidas as the general but does not detail the circumstances of his death [3].
Agreement: All sources (explicitly or implicitly) confirm Leonidas perished at the battle. Contradiction: Herodotus places Leonidas’ death at the height of the main battle, followed by a fierce struggle to recover his body [13, 17]. Diodorus implies his death occurred as part of the general massacre following the night attack [14]. Present/Absent: Herodotus details the retrieval of the body and the subsequent mutilation—decapitation and impalement—ordered by Xerxes [13, 18]. Diodorus mentions the mutilation later as a point of contention between Pausanias and an Aeginetan [19, 20]. Ctesias omits specific details of the death [3].

Ancient Historiography of Thermopylae Force Estimates and Casualties
The historical accounts of the Battle of Thermopylae (480 BCE) by Herodotus, Diodorus Siculus, and Ctesias differ significantly in their numerical claims, reflecting their respective historical methods, distances from the event, and potential biases.
1. Persian Force Numbers
Herodotus: Claims the total Persian land army from Asia was 1,700,000 infantry, plus cavalry and other units, totaling 2,317,610 fighting men [1, 2]. Adding European recruits and non-combatants, he brings the total to over 5 million [3].
Exact Quote: "the number of troops in the whole land army added together was found to be 1,700,000" (7.60) [1].
Timeline: Writing approximately 40 to 55 years after the battle (~440–425 BCE) [4, 5].
Modern View: Modern historians consider these figures "grossly exaggerated" and "impossibly high" [6, 7]. Estimates for the actual force range from 75,000 to 100,000 [8] to approximately 200,000 to 300,000 [9, 10].
Diodorus Siculus: Reports a significantly lower but still massive Persian land force.
Exact Quote: "the number of his land forces was over eight hundred thousand men" (11.5.7) [11].
Timeline: Writing approximately 430 years after the battle (mid-1st century BCE) [12, 13].
Modern View: Historians view 800,000 as still being physically impossible to supply or march through the pass [14].
Ctesias: Agrees with Diodorus on the overall figure but adds details on the ships.
Exact Quote: "Xerxes assembled a Persian army of 800,000 (not including chariots), and 1,000 triremes" (Fragment 27, 20) [15].
Timeline: Writing approximately 80 to 100 years after the battle (early 4th century BCE) [16, 17].
Modern View: Ctesias is generally viewed by modern scholars as less reliable, with his work being described as "absurd fantasies" or "scandal history" [17, 18].
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. Greek and Spartan Force Numbers
Herodotus: Provides a detailed breakdown totaling roughly 4,000 Peloponnesians (including the 300 Spartans) plus several thousand allies, totaling about 7,000 [19, 20].
Exact Quote: "The Hellenes who were waiting for the Persian in this region were 300 Spartan hoplites... altogether, they added up to [about 7,000]" (7.202-203) [19, 20].
Timeline: ~40–55 years after.
Modern View: This figure of roughly 7,000 Greeks is generally accepted by modern historians as plausible for a holding action [21, 22].
Diodorus Siculus: Gives a similar total but divides the Lacedaemonian contingent differently.
Exact Quote: "Leonidas... announced that only one thousand men should follow him... There were, then, of the Lacedaemonians one thousand, and with them three hundred Spartiates, while the rest of the Greeks... were three thousand" (11.4.2-5) [23]. Totaling 4,000 initially, with another 3,400 joining later [24].
Timeline: ~430 years after.
Modern View: The total of ~7,400 is consistent with the strategic requirement of holding the pass [22].
Ctesias: Focuses specifically on the Spartans in his summary.
Exact Quote: Ctesias does not provide a total Greek army sum in the surviving fragment but identifies Leonidas as the commander [15].
Timeline: ~80–100 years after.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. Reported Casualties
Herodotus: Reports that Xerxes lost 20,000 men over the three days [25]. He claims the Greeks recovered Leonidas' body after routing the Persians four times in a final struggle [26], though eventually, all who stayed (the 300 Spartans and 700 Thespians) were killed [27, 28].
Exact Quote: "Of all the corpses from his own army... (and there were actually about 20,000 of them), he left about 1,000 on the battlefield and buried the rest" (8.24) [25].
Modern View: While the 20,000 figure for Persians is a guess, historians agree Persian losses were significantly higher than Greek ones due to equipment disparity [29, 30].
Diodorus Siculus: Describes a heroic night attack on the Persian camp that resulted in a "great slaughter" of Persians before the Greeks were eventually overwhelmed [31].
Exact Quote: Diodorus notes the Persians "slew them [the Greeks] to a man" at the end, while the Persians "lost great numbers of his own troops" (11.10.4, 11.12.1) [31, 32]. He notes only 500 Greeks participated in the final suicidal night attack [33].
Modern View: Most historians consider the night attack described by Diodorus to be a later rhetorical invention, not a historical fact [34].
Ctesias: Claims massive, round numbers for Persian casualties across different assault waves.
Exact Quote: Artapanus attacked with 10,000 and was cut down; then 20,000 were defeated; finally, Xerxes ordered 50,000 to fight [15]. He reports that in early fighting "only two or three Spartans were killed" [15].
Modern View: These numbers are viewed as unrealistic exaggerations intended to discredit Herodotus [16, 17].
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. Areas of Agreement across Sources
The 300 Spartans: All sources agree that 300 elite Spartans formed the core of the defense under King Leonidas [19, 23, 35].
Betrayal via the Path: All sources (though Ctesias is summarized briefly) agree that the Greeks were only defeated after being encircled via a mountain path [15, 26, 36].
Persian Numerical Superiority: All sources agree the Persians massively outnumbered the Greeks, regardless of the specific millions or thousands claimed [15, 37, 38].
5. Areas of Contradiction across Sources
Persian Army Size: Herodotus claims 1.7 million+ [1], while Diodorus and Ctesias claim 800,000 [11, 15].
Total Greek Force: Herodotus and Diodorus provide detailed totals around 7,000–7,400 [19, 24], while Ctesias focuses almost exclusively on the Spartans [15].
The Final Stand: Herodotus describes the final stand as a desperate defense on a hillock during the day [26]. Diodorus describes it as a night attack on the Persian camp intended to assassinate Xerxes [31, 36].
Greek Casualties: Herodotus reports "only a few" fell until the final day [39]. Ctesias claims "only two or three Spartans" died in the first massive wave [15].
