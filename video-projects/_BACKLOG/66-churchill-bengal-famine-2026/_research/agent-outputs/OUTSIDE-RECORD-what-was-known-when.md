## 0. THE HEADLINE

**Three things this layer establishes.**

1. **London ran out of deniability on 23 September 1943 — the day before the 24 September War Cabinet.**
   Amery made the government's first official Commons statement on Bengal that day and conceded a
   "menace of famine," blaming profiteering and hoarding. He did it four days after the first British
   press report and one day after a Communist daily ran the story with a cartoon. **The next afternoon
   the War Cabinet, with Churchill in the chair, weighed Bengal against Balkan grain stocks.** [INSPECTED]
   The *earlier* boundary — when it stopped being *possible* to not know — is **23 August 1943**
   (Statesman photographs, 22 Aug) or, on a documentary reading, **28 January 1943** (Amery denies
   famine conditions in the Commons; Swaraj House prints 5,000 pamphlets rebutting him within weeks).

2. **The Americans were told the hoarding diagnosis, in Washington, as the reason not to allocate.**
   FRUS 1943 IV, Doc. 345 (Hull to New Delhi, 9 Oct 1943, 9 p.m.): Indian food requirements were left
   to the UK "at the insistence of representatives of the United Kingdom"; when the Combined Food Board
   Committee on Rice received urgent advice of a serious Indian shortage, the British member had it
   "checked in London," then reported "there appeared to be a speculative movement of rice into stocks
   and that the Government of India was coping with the situation." **E2's diagnosis was not an internal
   Cabinet opinion. It was Britain's operative position in an Allied allocation body.** [INSPECTED]

3. **⚠ Bowbrick's myth list hits three things this project currently relies on** — the *magnitude* of
   the food shortfall (and therefore the "bridgeable gap" argument), the *malaria* share of excess
   mortality, and, obliquely, the F2 "Sen's thesis stated by the Commission" line. Details in §4.3.
   **None of them destroys the spine of the video. Two of them change how a number can be said aloud.**

**And one citation error found in the task premise itself:** the "Ghosh" Ó Gráda cites is **Tushar Kanti
Ghosh, *The Bengal Tragedy* (Lahore: Hero Publications, 1944)** — a different man and a different book
from **Kali Charan Ghosh, *Famines in Bengal 1770–1943* (Calcutta: National Council of Education,
1944)**, the one in our library. See §3.1. [INSPECTED — Ó Gráda 2008 WP, bibliography, and n.10, n.33]

### 1.2 What the government said to them

- **Denial, then blame-shift.** Amery denies famine conditions (28 Jan 1943); concedes a "menace of
  famine" only after the press broke it (23 Sep 1943), and then attributes it to profiteering and
  hoarding — **the same diagnosis the War Cabinet minuted on 4 August (E2), delivered publicly seven
  weeks later.** [CORROBORATED — Simonow art. p.12 + n.94 ✕ Source E2]
- **Provincial-autonomy defence.** "Initially the Government diluted its responsibility by pointing to
  the tokenistic autonomy of the provincial government of Bengal" (art. p.15, n.119) — the argument
  our A4 / E6 / PROVINCIAL work covers. Simonow's judgement is that it was *tokenistic* because Delhi
  retained and used the power to dismiss provincial ministries by invoking Emergency.
- **Press management, not information.** Amery, 27 Sep 1943, wanting the story *out* of the forefront
  (n.123). The pamphleteer Gangulee was classified by Scotland Yard in 1940 as "holding strong
  anti-British and pro-Nazi sentiments" — claims Simonow says "had little evidence" (art. p.8, n.49).
  His famine pamphlet was deemed by the authorities "unfortunate" and unlikely to be "an honest or
  sincere document" (n.52).
- **Then benevolence theatre.** Wavell's RAF/army relief from late Oct, with the press running "heroic
  accounts" (*Daily Mirror* 30 Oct and 15 Nov; *Evening Telegraph* 13 Nov; *Illustrated London News*
  20 Nov 1943), and Major-General D. Stuart announcing success in **early December** — followed by
  Wavell declaring the famine "a matter of the past" (art. p.16, nn.125–126). Simonow: "a generosity
  that appears cynical in light of the severe delay."

### 1.3 The answer to the video's question

**When did it stop being possible for London to say it did not know?**

Three defensible answers, in rising order of strictness. Pick by how hostile a referee you want to
survive.

| Standard | Date | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **When the government was told** | **28 Jan 1943** | Amery denies famine conditions *in answer to Commons questions*. Within weeks a 5,000-copy pamphlet by a Royal Commission on Agriculture member, using Indian press evidence, is circulating in London to rebut him. The government simultaneously ran its *own* public appeal for Bengal's cyclone districts (early Jan 1943) — so it was publicising Bengal distress and denying Bengal famine in the same month. |
| **When the evidence became unignorable** | **22–26 Aug / 19–26 Sep 1943** | Statesman photographs 22 Aug; first British press report 19 Sep; photographs reprinted in Britain 26 Sep. Simonow: "It was with the publication of photographs that the official strategy of denying the existence of the crisis began to fail" (art. p.3). |
| **When the government itself conceded** | **⭐ 23 Sep 1943** | Amery's "menace of famine" in the Commons. **This is the operative date for the script**, because it is the government's own admission and it sits **one day before the 24 September War Cabinet** at which Bengal was weighed against Balkan stocks (E7). |

**The line for the script:** on 23 September 1943 the Secretary of State for India told the House of
Commons there was a menace of famine in Bengal, and blamed hoarding. The following afternoon the War
Cabinet met and decided that not more than 50,000 tons could be taken from grain stockpiled for
irregular forces in the Balkans. **Six weeks later, on 3 November, a British actress was quoted in the
Indian press saying six ships of food would do more good than all the guns in the world; on 10 November
the War Cabinet asked the Prime Minister to write to Canada discouraging one ship.** [INSPECTED —
each element separately sourced; the juxtaposition is ours]

### 2.3 What the American record does for the video — and what it does against it

**FOR the prosecution:**
1. **Doc 345** proves the hoarding diagnosis was Britain's *operating* position in an Allied allocation
   body, not just a Cabinet minute — and that Britain kept India's own representative from putting the
   case directly. It converts E2 from an opinion into a mechanism with an international footprint.
2. **Doc 343** independently confirms the **200,000-ton** figure ten days after the 24 Sep Cabinet set
   it (E7), *in the Government of India's own words*, and phrased as a complaint: "**only** 200,000 tons."
3. **Doc 336** is a senior Government of India official, in August 1943, telling an American diplomat
   that **six shiploads** would fix it and that wheat imports had been the obvious answer for a year.
4. **Doc 283** is a British minister telling the US Secretary of State, in writing, that a public
   Viceregal number was constructed to look as favourable as possible.

**AGAINST it — and this is the balance the script needs:**
1. **The Americans refused too.** JCS 29 May 1944; Roosevelt 1 June 1944 (Docs. 282). The State
   Department wanted to say yes and was overruled on military grounds. Any framing in which shipping
   refusal is uniquely British does not survive Doc. 282.
2. **The US had nothing to send.** Doc. 344: no significant Western-Hemisphere rice source; both US
   coasts wheat-deficient; Australia is the short haul. Doc. 338: US rice production ~1.75bn lb against
   an Indian cereal deficit of ~2bn lb, half the US crop needed at home and by the military.
3. **Ships, not grain, was the binding constraint — and both governments said so independently.**
   Churchill: "We have the wheat (in Australia) but we lack the ships" (Doc. 281). Hull: "There are
   larger supplies of wheat in Australia than there are boats to move this grain" (Doc. 344). A neutral
   party reached the same conclusion. **That does not exculpate the 4 August diagnosis** — the Cabinet
   said the shortage was *not physical*, which is a different claim from *we lack ships* — but it does
   mean the shipping constraint was real, and the video must say so.
4. **The 1944 counterfactual is documented.** Doc. 283 shows that when the military accepted the
   strategic case, **ships were found by cancelling military maintenance sailings in every theatre for
   three months.** The same instrument existed in 1943. It was used in 1944 and not in 1943. That is
   the cleanest available "it was possible" exhibit in the project, and it comes from a British minister.

**On Lend-Lease:** the searches returned **no** document in either volume tying Lend-Lease to Bengal
relief. FRUS 1943 IV Doc. 342 mentions OLLA (Office of Lend-Lease Administration) only to complain that
"piecemeal and uninformed statements attributed to OFFR and OLLA are having unfortunate effect" in the
Indian press. **Searched terms:** `famine`, `Famine` across both full-text extractions;
plus the whole 845.5018 / 845.50 / 845.48 document runs read in full. **Absence claim scope:** within

### 3.5 Ghosh — where he overreaches

- **Verdict-first structure.** His own Preface says the chapters were polemical newspaper articles,
  revised. His conclusion on Parliament — that the debates were "mere farces," "hollow," "a perfect
  camouflage or a smokescreen for deceiving the people of the entire civilized world" (printed p.121)
  — is **inference from one attendance claim**. It is quotable *as 1944 Indian opinion*; it is not a
  finding.
- **He treats Kunzru's 50,000–100,000/week as established.** Kunzru's own method is stated and is
  frankly an extrapolation: "one or two officials he met agreed that one death per week per village
  was a fair estimate," applied to half of 90,000 villages. **Kunzru's figures are useful as evidence
  of what informed Indians believed in November 1943 — and as a measure of the gap between that and
  Amery's 1,000/week — not as a mortality estimate.** Handle exactly as our MORTALITY file already
  handles the Calcutta University press release of 21 Feb 1944 (which Ghosh also reprints, App. G).

### 3.8 ⚠ The wartime-censorship caveat, applied

Both books were published in India in 1944 under the **Defence of India Rules** press regime. Two
consequences for how they are used:

1. **They are evidence of what could be printed as much as of what was true.** Ghosh's own Preface
   thanks an editor for running his series at "the almost certain risk of incurring the displeasure of
   the authorities" — i.e. he tells you the constraint was live.
2. **Their silences are not evidence.** Neither book's failure to mention something is a finding.
   Conversely, **what they *do* print is strong evidence that the information was publicly available** —
   which is exactly the question this file exists to answer. A district cholera figure printed in
   Calcutta in September 1943 is a figure London could have read.

**⚠ What kind of document this is.** It is not a survey of the literature. It is a **point-by-point

### 4.1 The myth list, with his evidence

| # | The myth Bowbrick says is false | His evidence | Printed p. |
|---|---|---|---|
| **M1** | **"There was enough food in Bengal in 1943"** / "11% more food available than 1941" (Sen; Daoud) | The claim requires the availability statistics to be ~100% accurate. Desai (1953) found survey estimates ran **47%–153%** of official estimates. | **M2** | **Speculation/hoarding withheld enough grain to cause the famine** | To cause it, a cartel had to remove 1–2M tons (28–56M bags) and destroy, export or store it a year. | **M3** | **Traders can raise prices independent of supply/demand** ("wicked speculators") | Thousands of competing traders cannot act in perfect concert; an individual defector profits by selling first. **How to say it without losing the argument.** Do **not** lead with a percentage. Lead with what is
uncontested and documentary: **India asked for 500,000 tons for September 1943 – February 1944 and the
War Cabinet authorised a token 50,000 tons to Colombo, unearmarked, on a diagnosis that the shortage
was not physical (E1, E2, E3).** The diagnosis was wrong. Whether the residual gap was one million tons
or two and a half is genuinely unknown, **and that uncertainty runs against the Cabinet, not for it** —
because it means London acted decisively on a number nobody had. Bowbrick's own statisticians said so
at the time: "disbelieved by the very government that produced them."

⚠ There is also a live tension inside our own file: the HINGE verdict says the shortfall was "modest,
which is exactly why shipping grain would have worked," while the mortality work needs a shock large
enough to kill 1.5–2.4 million. **Bowbrick's larger-shortfall reading resolves that tension in the
other direction.** Worth an explicit decision before scripting.

#### ⚠ HIT 2 — the **malaria share of excess mortality**

**Our current claim** (`01-VERIFIED-RESEARCH.md`, and the MORTALITY file): **Maharatna 1994, Table 1,
printed p.181 — malaria accounts for 43.06% of 1943 excess deaths.**

**Bowbrick M14** (printed p.2533): about half of Bengal's quinine allocation, **43,000 lb — enough for
more than 2 million five-day treatments — was never distributed by the end of 1943**; the Director of
Public Health said publicly in December 1943 that a vast quantity had gone to the black market; and
there is a contemporary claim of a virulent strain imported by Burma refugees. His conclusion: *"It
cannot, therefore, be assumed that any of the excess mortality from malaria was due to the famine."*

**Assessment.** Bowbrick's inference is **over-strong** — "cannot be assumed that *any*" is not
supported by the quinine facts, since a starving population is more susceptible regardless of quinine
supply, and Maharatna's excess-death methodology measures deviation from baseline, not attribution.
**But the underlying facts are real and are new to this project, and they cut two ways:**

- **Against a simple famine→malaria causal chain:** part of the malaria excess is attributable to an
  **administrative and criminal failure in quinine distribution**, not to hunger.
- **⭐ FOR the prosecution, and more usefully:** a government that could not distribute the quinine it
  already held is a government whose "we could not have shipped grain in time" defence weakens. **The
  quinine story is a controlled experiment in Bengali/Indian distribution capacity, and it fails.**
  It also converges with the FIC's finding (via Bowbrick) and with Dutt's contemporaneous "paucity of
  quinine" (§3.7) — three independent lines.

**Action:** the 43.06% figure is safe as *a decomposition of recorded excess deaths by cause*. It is
**not** safe as "43% of the famine's victims died of malaria caused by the famine." Fix the phrasing.

#### ⚠ HIT 3 — "**This is Sen's entitlement thesis, stated by the Commission in 1945**" (our F2)

Our F2 note reads: *"This is Sen's entitlement thesis, stated by the Commission in 1945 — thirty-six
years before *Poverty and Famines*."*

**Bowbrick's reading of the same Commission** (printed pp. 2514, 2517) is the exact inverse: the FIC
*set up* "Bengal had plenty of food" and "speculation and hoarding took vast amounts off the market"
**as hypotheses to test, tested them rigorously, and showed them to be false.** On his account Sen
revived refuted myths thirty years later and presented them as his own.

**Assessment.** Our F2 is not *wrong* on the words — the Commission does say that in 1928/1936/1941,
when purchasing power was provided, "no deaths from starvation occurred." **But our gloss overreaches**:
the Commission said it about *earlier, milder* shortfalls, not about 1943, and it is the *opposite* of
what the Commission concluded about 1943. Our file already carries the correction ("✅ with correction —
1928/36/41 had carry-over stock 1943 lacked"). **Bowbrick's point is that the framing sentence should
go entirely**, because it hands a hostile referee a real hit for no analytical gain. **Recommend
retiring the "this is Sen's thesis, stated in 1945" line.** [CONTESTED]
