## 1. Document identification

**Not a telegram — a private weekly letter.** Ó Gráda's citation ("Linlithgow to Amery, January 26th

- **Mansergh Vol. III, Document No. 362**
- **"The Marquess of Linlithgow to Mr Amery"**
- **MSS. EUR. F. 125/12** (Linlithgow's private-correspondence file, India Office Private Papers)

- **Printed pp. 541–548** (pdf pages 634–641, 0-indexed `d[634]`–`d[641]`)

## 2. The quote, char-exact off the rendered page image, printed p.544

> *"9. Mindful of our difficulties about food I told him that he simply* must ***produce some more rice
> out of Bengal for Ceylon even if Bengal itself went short! He was by no means unsympathetic, and it
> is possible that I may in the result screw a little out of them. The Chief continues to press me
> most strongly about both rice and labour for Ceylon. I have every sympathy with him, and will give
> him all the help I can in Council, particularly over rice."***

("*must*" is italicised in the original — Linlithgow's own emphasis.)

**Who "him" is (para 8, same page, immediately prior):**

> *"8. I had a friendly talk with* **Fazlul Huq** *of Bengal yesterday and found him in very good
> form..."*

So the antecedent of "him" in para 9 is confirmed on the page: **A. K. Fazlul Huq**, then Premier of
Bengal (he would be forced out in the last week of March 1943, per Ó Gráda). Ó Gráda's bracketed
`[Fazlul Huq]` is a correct, non-distorting editorial insertion.

**What prompted it — the letter's own throughline:**
- Para 2 (p.541): the week's headline development was the Government of India's new **wheat price
  control** and a public communiqué on the food position, issued that morning.
- Paras 3–6: the Gandhi-fast crisis and the Indian Army's post-war future — unrelated to food.
- Para 7: Congress/Rajagopalachari correspondence.
- Para 8: the Huq conversation begins as a routine item — Huq wants a face-saving overseas posting;
  Linlithgow is non-committal.
- **Para 9 pivots on "Mindful of our difficulties about food"** — i.e. Linlithgow uses the same
  meeting to press Huq on rice, immediately after (in the same conversation, on the same day) declining
  Huq's personal request. The letter frames it as incidental, almost an aside, not a formal directive.
- Para 9 continues: **"The Chief continues to press me most strongly about both rice and labour for
  Ceylon."** Within this same letter (para 6, "I consulted the Chief... about that" — footnote 5 = Doc.
  360, Wavell's 25 January letter) "the Chief" is **Field Marshal Wavell, Commander-in-Chief, India** —
  not the Governor of Ceylon. So the pressure Linlithgow is passing on to Huq originates with the
  **military command**, not (only) the civil administration. Para 9's last sentence turns to the labour
  side (footnote 8: **Telegram 184-S of 23 January 1943**, GoI negotiating terms for 20,000 Indian
  labourers to Ceylon's rubber plantations — L/P&J/8/205) — confirming the Ceylon demand was twofold:
  rice **and** rubber-plantation labour, both tied to Ceylon's wartime role after Malaya and Burma fell.
- **What happened next, within the letter:** nothing — paras 10 onward move to unrelated administrative
  matters (a misunderstanding over a Commons statement, a stalled constitutional-discussion follow-up,
  the Gandhi-fast press coverage, service Indianisation, lascars' pay). **The letter does not report an
  outcome of the Huq conversation** beyond Linlithgow's own hedge: "it is possible that I may in the
  result screw a little out of them." No tonnage, no date, no confirmation Huq acted.

**No footnote is attached to para 9 itself** — the whole letter carries one archival reference (MSS.
EUR. F. 125/12) at its head; there is no separate citation for the rice remark to chase further within
this document.

## 3. Verdict on Ó Gráda's epigraph

**Accurate in substance; one word silently dropped.**

| Ó Gráda's epigraph | Mansergh Doc. 362, p.544 (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| "he simply must produce **more** rice out of Bengal for Ceylon" | "he simply must produce **some more** rice out of Bengal for Ceylon" |
| "even if Bengal itself went short!" | "even if Bengal itself went short!" — exact |
| "Linlithgow to Amery, January 26th 1943" | Document dated 26 January 1943, Linlithgow to Amery — exact |

The dropped "some" softens nothing substantively (Ó Gráda's version if anything reads slightly
*stronger*, not weaker, than the original — "must produce more rice" vs "must produce some more
rice"). This is a minor transcription slip in a working paper, not a distortion of meaning or
attribution. **Not truncated, not altered in sense — one word missing.** If this quote goes on screen,
use the primary's exact wording ("some more rice"), not Ó Gráda's.

## 4. The wider evidence trail — is this one remark isolated, or part of a documented pattern?

Searched the full volume for "Ceylon" (53 hits reviewed for date/context) and traced the surrounding
administrative correspondence. **The 26 January remark is not an isolated aside — it sits inside a
five-month documented thread**, both before and after:

### Before (the demand's origin is London, and Burma's fall is the proximate cause)
- **Doc. 181** — Amery to Linlithgow, 14 Nov 1942 (Telegram, printed p.259): *"I have been much
  exercised over your food problems, both as regards wheat for the Army and rice for Ceylon, about
  which as you know I have been very much pressed by the Secretary of State for the Colonies."*
  **The chain of pressure is Colonial Office (Ceylon) → India Office (Amery) → Viceroy (Linlithgow) →
  Bengal (Huq)** — the 26 January remark is the last link in a chain that starts in Whitehall.
- **Doc. 330** — Amery's own memorandum, "THE INDIAN DEMAND FOR IMPORTED FOOD GRAINS" (undated,
  printed pp.473–474; L/E/8/3297: ff 223–6), explains *why* Ceylon depended on India at all: *"there is
  a constant import of rice from Burma... while Burma also fed Ceylon and other colonies. Loss of the
  Burma rice crop has been one of the chief disturbing factors."* Ceylon's claim on India(/Bengal) rice
  is a direct downstream consequence of Burma's fall (March 1942), not a standing colonial extraction
  arrangement.
- **Doc. 242** — Linlithgow to Amery, 3 Dec 1942 (Telegram No. 3402-G, printed pp.333–334): reports a
  worsening statistical food position and states plainly: *"I think it probable that until our own
  position becomes clearer we shall have to stop all exports of foodgrains from India... I realise
  that this will mean serious difficulty in Ceylon and Arabia... but export of food-grains from India
  has already excited considerable public criticism. If it continues despite a threatening internal
  shortage it may become a political issue of considerable gravity."*
  **⚠ Correction to the existing filing:** `01-VERIFIED-RESEARCH.md` E10 attributes the *"has already
  excited considerable public criticism"* line to **Doc. 243**. It is not there — **Doc. 243 is an
  unrelated telegram about Jinnah, Usman and the Indianisation of Council** (confirmed by rendered page
  image, `TOP3-p334-doc242-public-criticism.png`, which shows Doc. 242 ending and Doc. 243 beginning on
  the same printed page — an easy mis-copy). **The quote belongs to Doc. 242, 3 December 1942, printed
  pp.333–334.** Fix the citation in the ledger/research file.
- **Doc. 297** — Linlithgow to Amery, 26 Dec 1942 (Telegram No. 3599-S, printed pp.421–422): despite
  a stated **1.4-million-ton rice deficit** for India as a whole, states *"Twelve thousand tons of rice
  a month are being supplied to Ceylon Government and we shall endeavour to continue this supply until
  alternative arrangements for Ceylon can be made, say for a period of two months (January...)."* So the
  contemplated export freeze in Doc. 242 (3 Dec) was **not implemented** for Ceylon — supply continued
  at 12,000 t/month through the turn of the year, one month before Linlithgow leaned on Huq.
  A companion footnote (p.427, on Doc. 242's page) records that between 1 November and end-1942,
  *"Ceylon was likely to receive 43,000 tons of rice plus anything additional that **Bengal and
  Madras** could spare"* — Bengal is named, alongside Madras, as one of the provinces GoI hoped could
  supply the margin. This is the clearest documentary link between "Ceylon rice" and Bengal specifically
  *before* the 26 January conversation.

### After (the pressure did not let up, and Bengal's position kept worsening)
- **Doc. 681 (extract)** — Linlithgow to Amery, 2–4 May 1943 (printed pp.937–942; MSS. EUR. F. 125/12,
  same file as Doc. 362), para 24: *"I am still very uneasy about the rice position, and the Bengal
  position in particular is most unsatisfactory. I do not think that it has been at all well handled
  by their Civil servants or, for that matter... by their Ministers... That is all of course directly
  relevant to the extent to which we can help Ceylon and Saudi Arabia."* By this point Huq's ministry
  had already fallen (late March 1943) and Nazimuddin's had replaced it — so the same linkage (Bengal's
  worsening internal rice position weighed directly against continuing Ceylon/Arabia obligations)
  persisted under the *next* Bengal government too, three months after the Huq conversation and three
  months before the famine's peak became visible to London (E2, 4 Aug 1943). Same letter: Linlithgow
  records he has just told the Food Member *"I would run the Food Department myself, though not
  publicly, for the time being"* — the Viceroy took direct personal control of Food at the Centre in
  early May 1943, worth noting for context.

**What this trail does and doesn't establish.** It establishes, with primary documents at five
different dates (Nov 1942 – May 1943), that (a) the demand for Bengal rice to go to Ceylon was real,
sustained, and understood by Linlithgow himself to come at Bengal's expense; (b) it originated as a
consequence of Burma's loss and was pushed on Delhi by London and by the military C-in-C; (c)
Linlithgow twice explicitly weighed cutting exports because of India's own shortage and both times the
Ceylon obligation won out, at least in part. **It does not establish** a tonnage figure for rice that
actually left Bengal specifically for Ceylon in this window, nor does it show Huq complying, nor does
it connect this rice stream causally to excess deaths — the letter itself reports intent and pressure,
not an executed shipment.

## 6. E10 "psychological effect" — verification (task item 5, part A)

**Confirmed exact, and pinned to a page for the first time.** Doc. 265 (printed p.358, para 3):

> *"To produce the desired psychological effect this should be consigned to Food Department with no
> allocation for military and civil... If this wheat could be despatched not in regular monthly quotas
> but with an initial consignment of substantial proportions **the psychological effect in releasing
> stocks in India would be enhanced**."*

This is the Government of India's **own** Food Department language (not Linlithgow's, not London's) —
confirms E10's point that Delhi supplied this framing itself, now with an exact locator: **Doc. 265,
printed p.358**.

## 8. Provenance verdict on R1 (task item 3 / the project's request)

**R1 as phrased ("Britain shipped Bengal's food away," implying systematic wartime extraction to
England as a driver of the famine) stays REFUTED at the aggregate level — A8 is untouched.** Bengal
was a net rice importer on the pre-war average, the volume here contains **zero** hits for foodgrain
moving to England/Britain, and the actual destination named throughout this thread is Ceylon (and
Arabia, and the Army) — not the metropole.

**But R1 is not simply false, and the flat "❌ REFUTED" framing needs the qualification the project
already flagged.** This document, now fully read in context and corroborated by four other documents
across five months (Docs. 181, 242, 265, 297, 330, 681 — plus the primary itself, Doc. 362), establishes
a real, sourced, named event: **on 26 January 1943 the Viceroy of India told Bengal's own Premier that
he "simply must produce some more rice out of Bengal for Ceylon even if Bengal itself went short," and
said so as a considered position, not a slip** — Linlithgow had weighed cutting all foodgrain exports
seven weeks earlier (Doc. 242) specifically *because* of a worsening internal shortage, and chose
instead to keep pressing for more from Bengal. The demand did not originate with Bengal or with
London's malice toward Bengal specifically; it traces to the Colonial Office's need to keep Ceylon fed
and provisioned with rubber-plantation labour after Burma's fall removed Ceylon's normal supplier.

**Restated verdict for the ledger/script (recommended wording):**

> R1 is wrong about the destination (Ceylon, not England) and wrong about the aggregate (Bengal was a
> net importer, not an exporter, of rice) — but it is not simply false. On 26 January 1943 the Viceroy
> himself recorded pressuring Bengal's premier to supply more rice for Ceylon "even if Bengal itself
> went short," a documented instance of London/Delhi choosing an imperial wartime supply obligation
> over Bengal's own food security, months before the famine's peak. This is CORROBORATED (primary
> document, read in full context, triangulated against four related documents) as a real policy
> position — not corroborated as an executed large-scale shipment, and not the mechanism that explains
> the famine's scale (A8's net-import fact still carries that argument).

**Claim-status grading:** G1 moves from ⏳ (lead) to **CORROBORATED** (primary read in full, cross-dated
against Docs. 181/242/265/297/330/681, char-exact verbatim confirmed off the rendered page). Verdict
words are used here because the bar is met.
