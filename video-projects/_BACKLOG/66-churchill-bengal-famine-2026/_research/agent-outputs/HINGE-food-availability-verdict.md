# THE HINGE — Was there a genuine food-availability decline in Bengal in 1943?

**Written 2026-07-31.** Scope: settle the hinge from the scholarship, and report whether F1–F3 in
`01-VERIFIED-RESEARCH.md` survive it. Everything below was read from PDFs held in the repo; page
numbers are printed pages where the scan shows them.

---

## THE ANSWER, UP FRONT

**Yes. There was a real food-availability decline in Bengal in 1943, and the specialist literature
that has actually re-run the numbers is unanimous on it.** Goswami (1990) measured it at 6–14% per
capita below 1941 and took per-capita availability below the Commission's own subsistence norm.
Tauger (2003) argues it was far larger. Ó Gráda (2008), who is *not* a food-availability partisan,
calls the famine "the failure of the imperial power to make good a harvest shortfall that would have
been manageable in peacetime."

**Therefore the War Cabinet's 4 August 1943 diagnosis (E2) was wrong on the facts as stated** — "not
the result of physical deficiency but of hoarding" is false in its first half.

**But the shortfall was moderate, not catastrophic**, and it cannot by itself explain 1.5–3 million
deaths. The famine is a *both* case, and the honest verdict is a specific one: **a real but
survivable harvest shortfall landed on a province with no reserve stock, and was converted into mass
death by the belief that it was not real.** That belief — hoarding-not-shortage — was held in
Calcutta, in Delhi and in London, and it is why nobody imported at scale in time.

**F1 does not survive. F2 survives with a correction. F3 survives and gets much stronger.**

Confidence: **CORROBORATED** on the existence of a decline (four independent specialists, three
independent methods). **CONTESTED** on its magnitude (estimates range from ~6% to ~50%). See §5 for
exactly what the script may and may not say.

## 1 — GOSWAMI: F1 DOES NOT SURVIVE

**Omkar Goswami, "The Bengal Famine of 1943: re-examining the data," *Indian Economic and Social
History Review* 27:4 (1990), pp. 445–463.** Read in full (19 pp., text layer intact). Goswami was
then at the Indian Statistical Institute, Delhi and Rutgers; his author's note thanks **Sen himself**
for detailed comments on the first draft (p. 445 n.). This is not a hostile outsider — it is an
in-discipline re-analysis Sen read before publication.

### 1.1 What he did

Goswami re-runs Sen's calculation **on Sen's own dataset** — the Famine Inquiry Commission figures,
with Mahalanobis's corrected output estimates and the registration-based adjusted trade data (p. 451
and n. 17). He changes three things:

1. **He reconstructs the missing stock series.** Sen had none and used two- and three-year moving
   averages as a surrogate (Goswami p. 451, step 4). Goswami instead builds a stock series forward
   from a zero-stock base year — 1929, chosen because 1927–28 were poor harvests — with a 5% annual
   write-off for rodents, moisture and rot (pp. 453–454).
2. **He corrects the population growth rate.** Sen used 0.46%/yr from the 1941 and 1951 censuses,
   raised to 1%. Goswami: *"The 1941 Census was very poorly conducted and the data are considered to
   be unreliable"* (p. 452). He runs 1931→1951 on common area, excluding Coochbehar, Tripura and
   Sylhet: 50.7 million (1931) → 64.2 million (1951) = **1.18% per year** (p. 452).
3. **He adds a September–August crop-year periodisation** alongside the calendar year, because
   starvation deaths ended September–October 1943 (p. 452).

**The design point that makes this hard to dismiss:** Goswami deliberately loaded the dice *against*
his own conclusion, and says so twice — *"I will consciously make assumptions that overstate
foodgrain supply in 1943 and, hence, underplay the possibility of a food availability decline"*
(p. 452); and at p. 453, *"by pegging consumption at levels lower than those prescribed by the Famine
Relief Codes, I am essentially constructing a series on subsistence consumption which should
overstate stocks and minimise the role of FAD. If anything, my assumptions strengthen the null
hypothesis that FAD had little to do with the 1943 famine."*

### 1.2 What he found

Four estimates, all in the same direction (p. 457):

| Consumption norm | Periodisation | Per-capita food availability, 1943 vs 1941 |
|---|---|---:|
| 1 lb/person/day | calendar year | **−6.1%** |
| 1 lb/person/day | Sept–Aug | **−7.1%** |
| 1.05 lbs/person/day | calendar year | **−12.1%** |
| 1.05 lbs/person/day | Sept–Aug | **−13.8%** |

On three-year moving averages — Goswami reports *"the two least dramatic ones"* — average per-capita
supply at end-1943 was **10.6% to 12.3% below** the corresponding 1941 average (p. 457). And the
comparison is itself generous to Sen: 1941 was a bad year in its own right, 13–17% below the 1936–40
average, so **"comparing 1943 with 1941 tends to underplay the food availability decline in the
famine year"** (p. 457).

### 1.3 THE MECHANISM — and this is what kills F1

The 1941-vs-1943 comparison fails not because the crop numbers are wrong but because **1941 was
cushioned by a carry-over stock that 1943 did not have** (p. 459):

> *"1941 was preceded by one excellent crop year (1937), one good year (1938), and two normal years
> (1939 and 1940). As a result, the closing stock in 1940 was quite high, which softened the impact
> of 1941's severe short crop… the closing stock of rice in December 1940 was **2.57 million tons, or
> 27 per cent of the average output during 1938–40**. In contrast, the closing stock in December 1942
> stood at a paltry **672,000 tons, or 7 per cent** of the average crop for 1938–40. Quite simply, the
> stock was insufficient to tide over the shortfall of 1943."*

And the subsistence threshold is crossed between the two years (p. 459): per-capita rice availability
was **0.1761 tons/yr in 1941 = 1.08 lbs/day**, against **0.1547 tons/yr in 1943 = 0.95 lbs/day**.
Mahalanobis, appointed by the Commission itself, put minimum per-capita consumption at 17 oz/day and
the Commission at 15–17 oz (Goswami p. 453, citing FIC p. 204). **1941 sat above the subsistence
line; 1943 sat below it.**

Two aggravators (pp. 457, 459): wheat availability was *also* lower in 1943 than 1941, so adding
wheat **strengthens** the finding; and most of the net import arrived only in the **last quarter of
1943**, so during the killing months of March–September actual availability was lower still than his
stock-adjusted series shows.

### 1.4 The mechanism is visible in Sen's own table

*(Our arithmetic on Sen's published numbers — marked INSPECTED, not corroborated by a third party.)*

Sen's Table 6.2, *Poverty and Famines* (Clarendon, 1981), p. 61, index of total foodgrains supply,
1941 = 100: **1941 = 100 · 1942 = 131 · 1943 = 111.** Read as levels against a roughly constant
consumption requirement, that series says: 1941 consumed far below requirement (drawing the stock
down), 1942 ran a surplus (partly rebuilding it), 1943 ran a deficit again. Goswami's stock
accounting is simply that sequence made explicit — the 1942 surplus went into *refilling a hole*, not
into a bank. Sen's two-year moving average ending 1943 averages the good year with the bad and
returns 121, which looks safe; the stock series shows why it was not.

**This matters for the retraction's circularity objection.** The dispute is resolvable without
appealing to the Commission's authority at all: it turns on an accounting identity applied to Sen's
own published figures.

### 1.5 What Goswami says about the FIC's figures specifically

Two things, cutting in opposite directions. Use both.

**(a) He convicts the Commission of guessing.** On the carry-over — the exact variable that decides
the hinge — Goswami (p. 452): *"the stock estimates… were frequently referred to but never stated by
the Famine Inquiry Commission. Although the Commission argued that the 'carry-over' of the old rice
at the end of 1942 was substantially less than before, **it gave no evidences**."* His footnote 19
cites the Commission's own dissenting member, **W. Afzal Husain, Minutes of Dissent, FIC p. 179**:

> *"absolutely no data are available regarding the stock position of rice (or any other food grain)"*

And at p. 459: *"The Commission adopted FAD as the explanation by assuming 'a sharp decline of that
mysterious — and unobserved — "carry-over from the previous year."' **The Commission was definitely
guessing, and passing it off as an explanation.**"* (The inner quotation is Sen, *Poverty and
Famines* p. 83 — Goswami is quoting Sen mocking the Commission, then agreeing with the mockery.)

**(b) And then he finds the guess was right.** Same paragraph: *"**In light of the evidence presented
here, the Commission may not have been incorrect after all.**"*

**(c) He accepts the Commission's crop numbers.** He works *"throughout this section… with the data
set used by the Famine Inquiry Commission and Sen (1981)"* (pp. 451–452). His 1942 aman figure is the
Commission's: **6.02 million tons, 32% below the previous aman and 19% below the previous
quinquennial average** (p. 447, citing FIC p. 207). He is not alleging falsified output — he is
saying the FIC had **no stock data at all**, and Sen's rebuttal inherited the same hole.

### 1.6 Verdict on F1

**F1 does not survive as argued. Retire it as a load-bearing claim.**

F1 reasons: *1943 had 43 weeks of supply against 39 in 1941; 1941 was worse and produced no famine;
therefore 1943's shortfall cannot explain the famine.* Goswami's finding is that **this comparison
omits the one variable that distinguishes the two years.** The FIC table F1 quotes is headed
**"current supply"** — by construction it counts the year's crops plus net imports and by
construction excludes the opening carry-over. That is precisely the term Goswami reconstructs, and it
moves the two years in opposite directions: 2.57 Mt of cushion entering 1941, 0.672 Mt entering 1943.
Add it back and **1943 is the worse year, not the better one.**

So F1's sentence — *"1941 had a worse supply position than 1943. 1941 produced no famine"* — is true
of *current* supply and false of *total* supply. **Downgrade F1 to ❌ AS ARGUED.** The table is real
and still quotable, but only with the stock caveat attached, at which point it no longer supports the
conclusion it was filed to support.

### 1.7 What Goswami does NOT claim — the referee's obligation

He is explicit, and the script must carry it (pp. 459–460):

> *"The findings of this article are not intended to dispute Sen's basic proposition that famines are
> often characterised by massive exchange entitlement failures, without any significant decline in
> foodgrain availability. My exercise is more modest."*

> *"What happened in 1943 was, indeed, a dramatic failure in exchange entitlement, where people died
> in the millions because they could not afford to buy rice. I have attempted to establish that such
> a FEE was also accompanied by a fairly sharp FAD. This does not imply that FAD can be rejuvenated
> from the ashes and presented as the key to the 1943 famine; **merely that its demolition requires
> heavier artillery.**"*

He concedes the price movement outran the supply movement — *"food prices soared at a rate much
faster than the decline in food availability"* (p. 459) — and that purchasing-power differentials
between Calcutta and the countryside pulled rice out of the villages. **Goswami's conclusion is
"both," with availability restored as a real contributing cause. It is not "Sen was wrong."**

## 2 — THE SEN–BOWBRICK–TAUGER DISPUTE, STEELMANNED

### 2.1 SEN AT HIS STRONGEST

**Amartya Sen, *Poverty and Famines: An Essay on Entitlement and Deprivation* (Clarendon Press,
1981), ch. 6, pp. 52–83.** Read directly from the held copy.

Sen's case is better than its critics allow, and three things about it deserve stating plainly.

**First, he states his bias direction openly and consistently loads it against himself.** Five named
corrections (pp. 58–61), each argued: adjustment of official production estimates; wheat imports
raised 36% to cover road trade *"almost certainly an overestimate for 1941 vis-à-vis 1943, but this
is an acceptable bias as it favours the thesis we are rejecting"* (p. 59); per-capita scaling at 1%
population growth, *"a reasonably safe overestimate… chosen here with a bias in favour of the thesis
to be rejected"* (p. 60); and a deduction of the entire late-1943 import surge, *"To bias the figures
as much as possible against 1943"* (p. 60). Result (Table 6.2, p. 61): 1943 total foodgrains supply
index 111, per-capita 109, against 1941 = 100.

**Second — and this is the point Bowbrick misses — Sen does not ignore the carry-over.** He devotes
"Correction 5" to it (p. 62), quotes the Commission's claim that the carry-over was smaller in 1943,
notes *"it gave no data on this"*, and then quotes **Afzal Husain's minute himself** — the same
sentence Goswami uses. He then says something Bowbrick never answers:

> *"The Commission's majority view that 'the carry-over at the beginning of 1943 was probably
> sufficient for about six weeks' requirements' (p. 15) is just as much a pure surmise as is Husain's
> view that 'a carry-over in the sense of surplus over consumption must have vanished years ago'
> (p. 182)."*

His remedy is the moving average, and he reports the result honestly, including the awkward part:
the two-year average ending 1943 is the highest in the series, while *"The three-year average ending
in 1943 takes a dip from the preceding average, but it is still of the same order of magnitude as the
average ending in 1941—indeed, it is just a bit higher"* (p. 62). Conclusion, p. 63: *"It seems safe
to conclude that the disastrous Bengal famine was not the reflection of a remarkable over-all
shortage of foodgrains in Bengal."*

Note the hedge in that sentence. Sen's claim is not "there was no shortage." It is "there was no
**remarkable over-all** shortage." Critics routinely attack the stronger claim.

**Third, Sen's rural-vs-urban and regional analysis stands independently of the tonnage question.**
Bengal's famine was rural; Calcutta was insulated by subsidised distribution; the destitution pattern
tracks occupation, not geography. None of that is touched by Goswami or Tauger.

**Where Sen is wrong:** (a) the moving average is not a stock series, and cannot see the difference
between a surplus that is banked and a surplus that refills a hole — which is exactly the 1942 case
(§1.4); (b) his own footnote gives away the problem in his acreage data. At p. 58 he writes that
Blyn's rice yield per acre was higher in 1942–3 than 1940–1 *"despite the fact that the acreage in
1942–3 was known to be much higher than in 1940–1."* Sen treats that acreage rise as real. Islam
(§2.4) shows it was substantially a statistical artefact, and one that begins precisely in 1941/42.

**Direct check performed:** the project library (17 PDFs) and a repo-wide filename search for
Sen/*Food Policy* PDFs returned nothing; only Bowbrick's attack is held. Where the missing replies
could change the read is flagged inline below and summarised at §2.5.

### 2.2 BOWBRICK AT HIS STRONGEST

**Peter Bowbrick, "The causes of famine — A refutation of Professor Sen's theory," *Food Policy* 11:2
(1986), pp. 105–124.** Read in full.

Bowbrick's title is a polemic and his tone is hostile, but three of his arguments are serious and one
is, in our judgement, the single most important sentence anyone has written about this famine.

**(a) The data-quality attack (pp. 112–113).** The crop figures are not measurements. Each Circle
Officer *"ascertains from personal inspection and by questioning other local officers and
cultivators, the relation which the area under the crop bears to the normal acreage"*; the estimate
is then adjusted upward or downward by the Subdivisional Officer and again by the District Officer
"as he thinks necessary" (Bowbrick quoting FIC *Final Report* pp. 44–45). Bowbrick adds Desai's
comparison of official estimates against Mahalanobis's scientific surveys: discrepancies of **47% to
153%** of the official estimate, varying year to year. And Mahalanobis's finding that even trained
enumerators *"tended to select the best fields and the best areas of damaged fields"* — an upward
bias that is **worst exactly when much of the crop is damaged, as in 1942** (p. 112).

**(b) The carry-over attack (pp. 113–114).** Same target as Goswami, four years earlier.

**(c) The demand-side arithmetic (pp. 117–118).** This is his best quantitative work and it is rarely
answered. If 10% of the population raised consumption from 14 oz/day to 17 oz, total demand rises
**1.8%** — which cannot produce a four- to twenty-fold price rise. On Sen's own sources, Calcutta's
preferential schemes *"never covered more than a quarter of the population"*, and **Calcutta's
consumption actually fell by 12–45% during the famine**. The redistribution Sen posits would require
some groups to eat two to six times normal *and* pay four to twenty times normal to do it.

**(d) The sentence that matters (p. 120):**

> *"The Bengal government's belief in the existence of enormous speculative stocks was a major reason
> for their failure to act effectively. **The belief in speculation, rather than the speculation
> itself, was the villain.**"*

And at p. 122, on who actually held the hoarding theory: *"Like Sen, the government believed that
there was no real shortage… They had virtually the same views on famine causation: inflation,
speculation and hoarding… In fact, both Sen's diagnosis and his remedies were put to the test by the
Bengal government. The result was a famine in which three million people died."* His footnote 71
sharpens it: Sen offers two pages of evidence to show the Bengal government was captured by the
food-shortage view, and *"in support of his claim… gives two pages of evidence showing just the
opposite: that the Bengal government was firmly convinced that there was adequate food available."*

**Why this is load-bearing for the project.** Bowbrick, writing in 1986 without the War Cabinet
minute in front of him, argued from outcome that the operative diagnosis in 1943 was
hoarding-not-shortage and that this diagnosis is what prevented imports. **E2 is that diagnosis, in
the Cabinet's own minute, verbatim.** Bowbrick predicted the document.

**Where Bowbrick is wrong, and it is not trivial:**

- **He misdescribes Sen's position on carry-over.** His summary bullet (p. 123) reads *"Sen's
  assumption of zero carry over conflicts with all available evidence."* Sen assumes no such thing;
  he explicitly declines to assume anything and substitutes moving averages (Sen p. 62). This is a
  straw man and it should not be repeated on screen.
- **His own carry-over figure is admittedly invented.** His Table 1 uses *"arbitrarily chosen"*
  8,500,000 tons at the beginning of 1941 (p. 113). That is a sensitivity demonstration — it shows
  Sen's result is not robust to the assumption — not a measurement. **Goswami is the one who did the
  measurement.** Cite Goswami for the number; cite Bowbrick only for the objection.
- **The "±3000%" claim is rhetoric.** He argues that because each year's figure could be 50% out, the
  difference between two years carries a 3000% margin (p. 113). That holds only if the errors are
  independent. He himself says *"the bias is not random"* — and a systematic bias applied by the same
  officers using the same method in both years largely **cancels** in a year-on-year comparison. This
  is the strongest reply available to Sen, and Bowbrick does not address it. ⚠ **This is the single
  point where the missing 1986 reply most likely lands.**
- **Internal tension.** At p. 120 he writes that *"the accumulation in previous years meant that an
  unusually large amount was carried over in private hands from the excellent crop of the previous
  year, mitigating the effect of the shortage"* — which sits awkwardly against his carry-over
  argument at pp. 113–114. He is arguing inside Sen's frame there, but the passage should not be
  quoted next to the other one.

### 2.3 TAUGER AT HIS STRONGEST

**Mark B. Tauger, "Entitlement, Shortage and the 1943 Bengal Famine: Another Look," *Journal of
Peasant Studies* 31:1 (2003), pp. 45–72**, and **"The Indian Famine Crises of World War II,"
*British Scholar* 1:2 (2009), pp. 166–196.** 2003 read closely; 2009 sampled.

**(a) His central methodological claim, and it is correct.** The FIC figures are not harvest data:

> *"they are not in fact production statistics at all, but rather pre-harvest projections not based on
> measurements of actual harvests"* (2003, p. 59)

The Agricultural Department computed output as assumed **normal area** × assumed **standard yield** ×
a **condition factor** set by visual inspection — *"This system did not involve any direct
measurements of actual harvests by actual farmers"* (p. 59). He grounds this on Islam (1978), Dewey
(1978) and Blyn (1966), and on a 1944 report to the Indian Central Food Advisory Council:
*"Agricultural statistics particularly in the permanently settled areas are chaotic. Both area and
yield figures as recorded at present are unreliable"* (p. 61, citing Government of India 1944, p. 9).
**We verified this characterisation directly against Islam 1978 — see §2.4. It checks out.**

**(b) The Mahalanobis admission, and this belongs in the video.** Tauger (2003, p. 61, via Dewey
1978: 308–9) quotes the Commission's own statistician:

> *"It was quite impossible to judge to what extent the present crisis was due (a) to actual physical
> shortage of rice or (b) to failure of distribution arising from the withholding of the marketable
> surplus by hoarding and/or profiteering."*

**The man who built the numbers said the question could not be answered from them.** On 4 August 1943
the War Cabinet answered it in one sentence, with confidence (E2). That juxtaposition is the referee's
line, and it is fair to both sides. ⏳ Trace to Dewey 1978 pp. 308–9 for the original locator before
use.

**(c) The Padmanabhan escalation.** Tauger reprints Padmanabhan's research-station yields (2003,
Table 2, p. 64) and argues that because the estimates were pre-harvest eye judgements by officials
who *"may not have known about the epiphytotic, may not have noticed it, or may have judged the crop
at an earlier stage"* (p. 65), the real 1942 aman may have been **20–30% of 1941's**, implying Bengal
had *"only about half the total food availability during the famine that it did in previous years"*
(p. 66).

**(d) The causal chain, which is genuinely new and is the best HOW>WHY material in the project.**
Tauger shows the cyclone and the fungus were not two disasters but one (2003, pp. 65–66): the aus
crop was already infested and was harvested *simultaneously with the replanting of the aman*, seeding
the new crop; the October cyclone then *"spread the helminthosporium infestation in the most
effective way possible because it included intense winds that distributed spores widely, and heavy
rains that made sure the spores fell on the plants"* — arriving when the panicles had formed but were
still early. **The cyclone's main effect was not that it drowned the crop. It aerosolised the
pathogen.**

**(e) The 2009 widening.** Bengal was one of several simultaneous Indian subsistence crises; London
faced a portfolio, not a single province; the crisis was *"at root a problem of production and
shortage more than of markets"* (2009, p. 5 of PDF). This is useful context and a genuine mitigation
argument.

**Where Tauger is wrong, or at least unproven:**

- **The extrapolation from two research stations to a province of 60 million is not supported.**
  Bankura and Chinsurah are variety-trial stations. They grow many cultivars, including susceptible
  ones, in dense adjacent plots — conditions that *favour* an epiphytotic relative to a farmer's
  mixed field. They are also not a random sample of Bengal's acreage. Tauger knows this and hedges
  properly — *"To the extent that Padmanabhan's data are indicative"*, *"let us begin just for
  argument's sake"* (pp. 65–66) — but the "half of previous availability" figure is a thought
  experiment, not a measurement, and it has been repeated elsewhere as though it were one. **Do not
  put Tauger's 50% on screen as a finding.**
- **His data critique cuts both ways and he does not say so.** If the official series is unreliable,
  that undermines Sen — but it equally undermines Goswami, who uses the same series, and it removes
  the basis for any confident magnitude, including Tauger's own.
- **Direction-of-bias is asserted, not demonstrated.** Tauger needs the bias to be *upward* in 1942
  specifically. Mahalanobis's best-field selection effect supports that. Islam's finding of
  systematic **under**estimation of acreage cuts the other way (§2.4). Both can be true — acreage
  understated, yield overstated — but the net effect on 1942 is not established.

### 2.4 ISLAM 1978 — the independent check, and it is the most important thing in this section

**M. Mufakharul Islam, *Bengal Agriculture 1920–1946: A Quantitative Study* (Cambridge University
Press, 1978).** Held in the library; read ch. 1 directly. This is the specialist quantitative study
of exactly the data the whole dispute runs on, and it settles a question neither Sen nor Bowbrick nor
Tauger resolves.

Islam confirms the estimating formula Tauger describes — output = area × standard yield × condition
factor / 100 (printed p. 21) — and confirms its defects: crop-cutting experiments *"not based on
random sampling but on purposive sampling"*, with neither plot size nor number of experiments
*"adequate for the purpose of generalisation"* (printed p. 28); and the condition factor
*"completely based on subjective judgement and… therefore, likely to be even more unreliable"*
(printed p. 32). **Tauger's citation of Islam is accurate.**

Islam's own findings on direction of error (printed pp. 33–35): official **acreage** figures are
systematic **under**estimates; official **yield per acre** figures are **over**estimates.

**And then the finding that decides the methodological question.** The standard defence of any
year-on-year comparison in this data — used by Thomas and Shastry, by Blyn, and by Subramaniam, all
quoted by Islam — is that the errors are systematic and therefore cancel in the difference, so the
series is *"inadequate for the estimation of total food supply"* but *"helpful for time comparisons"*
(printed p. 36). Islam tests that assumption against Bengal and rejects it:

> *"the general pattern changes, beginning from 1941/42 in the case of winter rice and autumn rice,
> and 1942/43 in the case of other crops, when the upward movements in the acreage statistics
> started."* (printed p. 37)

He argues the Department was *"actually reducing the margin of underestimation in the acreage data"*
and dressing the change as the Grow More Food Campaign and good weather, noting that *"in contrast
with earlier years, these revisions were almost uniformly in the upward direction."* His conclusion:

> *"the assumption that the range of error (whether in the direction of overestimation or
> underestimation) was uniform over the entire period, leaving the percentage rate of change more or
> less unaffected, **does not hold good in the case of Bengal**."* (printed p. 37)

**Why this is decisive.** The break Islam identifies begins **in 1941/42 for winter and autumn rice**
— i.e. it falls exactly between the two years Sen compares, and it runs in the direction that makes
1943 look better relative to 1941. **The one defence that could rescue the 1941-vs-1943 comparison
fails, and it fails for a documented reason specific to those years, established by a Cambridge
University Press monograph written twelve years before the dispute was joined and with no stake in
it.**

And it lands on Sen's own footnote. Sen (p. 58) notes the 1942–3 acreage was *"known to be much
higher"* than 1940–1 and treats that as real. Islam says a substantial part of it was the Department
adjusting its own error.

### 2.5 Where the missing Sen replies could change the read

Three places, ranked:

1. **Error correlation across years (highest risk).** Bowbrick's ±3000% depends on independent
   errors. The obvious reply is that the bias is systematic and cancels. **Islam largely closes this
   off for 1941–43 specifically** (§2.4), but Sen's own version of the argument is unread. If Sen made
   it well, Bowbrick's headline number is dead — though Islam's finding survives it, and Goswami's
   result does not depend on it at all.
2. **The straw man.** Sen almost certainly pointed out that he never assumed zero carry-over. He is
   right. We have pre-empted this by reading Sen p. 62 directly.
3. **Calcutta consumption.** Bowbrick's claim that Calcutta consumption fell 12–45% is a sharp

**None of these touch Goswami**, whose result uses Sen's data, Sen's sources and a bias deliberately

### 2.6 Ó Gráda — the closest thing to a neutral adjudicator

**Cormac Ó Gráda, "Sufficiency and Sufficiency and Sufficiency: Revisiting the Bengal Famine of
1943–44" (2008).** Read. Ó Gráda is a leading famine economist and no partisan of the
food-availability school; his contribution is that he takes the sufficiency *rhetoric* as his subject.

His conclusion (p. 32):

> *"Here I have argued that the lack of political will to divert foodstuffs from the war effort rather
> than speculation in the sense outlined was mainly responsible for the famine. **Neither price
> movements nor the outcome of the food drives of the summer of 1943 support the case for massive
> hoards of rice being kept from the market**… The problem in Bengal in 1943 was not internecine
> strife, but **the failure of the imperial power to make good a harvest shortfall that would have
> been manageable in peacetime.**"*

That last clause is the project's verdict sentence, from a scholar with no stake in the FAD fight:
**a real shortfall, survivable, not made good.**

**But he also supplies the strongest quantitative evidence AGAINST a simple food-availability story,
and the referee must carry it.** In his district-level regressions on 24 divisions, the variable for
the 1942 aman decline (`DAGQ`) has essentially no explanatory power for excess mortality; what
predicts mortality is population density, the baseline death rate, and young-adult literacy (pp. 31,
Tables 4–5). His reading (p. 31): *"The failure of DAGQ supports an entitlements approach… eastern
divisions experienced lower proportionate declines in the aman crop in 1942, but they were deficit
provinces."* **Where the crop fell hardest is not where people died hardest.** That is a real fact and
it constrains how far the crop failure can be pushed as *the* explanation.

He also corroborates **G2** in the research file: Suhrawardy's mid-1943 food drives found no hoards,
and prices in early 1944 stayed buoyant after a good aman — which *"argues against the presence of
excessive hoarding on a large scale at the height of the famine"* (p. 18).

## 3 — MISHRA 2019: what it actually found, and why the naive reading is wrong

**Vimal Mishra et al., "Drought and Famine in India, 1870–2016," *Geophysical Research Letters* 46
(2019), pp. 2075–2083.** Read in full (main text; supporting information not held).

### 3.1 The method

Not a historical argument — a hydrological reconstruction. Daily gridded precipitation (IMD, 6,995
stations post-1901; 1,690 stations for 1870–1900) is fed to the **Variable Infiltration Capacity
(VIC)** land-surface model at 0.5° resolution, daily timestep, 1870–2016 (pp. 2076). Soil moisture at
**60 cm** — root-zone depth for most crops — is converted to percentiles by the empirical Weibull
plotting position; **SMP < 20 is drought**. Major drought periods are then identified by
**severity–area–duration (SAD)** analysis with a minimum area threshold of 0.1 × 10⁶ km².

### 3.2 The finding

Seven major drought periods (1876–1882, 1895–1900, 1908–1924, 1937–1945, 1982–1990, 1997–2004,
2011–2015). Of six major famines in the window, **five track soil-moisture drought and 1943 does
not** (abstract, p. 2075). Specifically (p. 2080): the 1937–1945 drought period peaked in **August
and December 1941 — before the famine**; and *"The famine-affected region received 15%, 3%, 9%, and
4% above-normal precipitation during June, July, August, and September of 1943."*

### 3.3 ⚠ Why the naive reading is wrong — and this is the most useful thing in the paper

**Absence of drought is not absence of crop failure, and here the two are actively opposed.**

Two problems, both fatal to reading Mishra as "no crop failure":

**(a) The instrument cannot see this failure by construction.** A soil-moisture *deficit* index
detects too little water. The 1942 Bengal crop failed from **too much** — excessive September rain,
prolonged November cloud, waterlogging, and a fungal epiphytotic those conditions caused (§4).
Padmanabhan's own meteorology is the mirror image of drought. Mishra's SMP would correctly score
1942–43 as *not dry*, and that tells us nothing whatever about the harvest.

**(b) The reported precipitation figures are for the wrong crop year.** The harvest that failed was
the **aman sown June–July 1942, transplanted September–October 1942, reaped November–December 1942**.
Mishra's headline "above-normal precipitation" numbers are for **June–September 1943** — a monsoon
that came *after* the failed harvest and produced, on Goswami's account (p. 449), a record aus and a
record aman at the end of 1943. Mishra is, on the main-text evidence, characterising the recovery
year, not the failure year.

⚠ **Flagged, not inferred:** the 1942 monsoon values may appear in **Figures S13 and S14** of the
supporting information, which **we do not hold** (the SI is a separate file, not bundled in our PDF).
We therefore **cannot state what Mishra's 1942 soil-moisture values were**. What can be stated is
(a) the main text reports no 1943 drought and locates the nearest drought peak in 1941, and (b) a
soil-moisture-deficit index cannot detect a crop failure caused by excess moisture and fungus. **Get
the SI before this goes on screen.**

Mishra's own caution supports the wariness. For the other unexplained case, 1873–74, the paper says a
famine may miss the SAD analysis because it was *"too localized to appear on the SAD envelope
curves"* (p. 2078) — and the authors then went and found the local drought anyway. The same
localisation caveat applies to Bengal, which is roughly 2% of India's area against a national SAD
threshold.

### 3.4 ⚠ Its verdict on British policy is outside its competence, and the paper shows why

Mishra concludes: *"The 1943 Bengal famine was not caused by drought but rather was a result of a
complete policy failure during the British era"* (p. 2081), and in the Plain Language Summary, *"one
famine was completely due to the failure of policy during the British era"* (p. 2075).

The first half — not caused by drought — is what their instrument measured. **The second half is not
a finding; it is a non-sequitur wearing a finding's clothes.** The paper's support for it is four
parenthetical citations to secondary literature (Sen 1976; Tauger 2009; Maharatna 1996; FIC 1945) in
a single paragraph, in a four-page *Geophysical Research Letters* results section. And one of them is
mis-deployed: **Tauger 2009 is cited for "military and political events adversely affected Bengal's
economy," when Tauger's entire argument is that the crisis was "at root a problem of production and
shortage more than of markets."**

**Use Mishra for the hydrology only.** It is genuinely valuable there — it establishes, from an
instrument with no stake in the historiography, that 1943 breaks the pattern of every other major
Indian famine in a century and a half. Pair it with Padmanabhan and it becomes a real finding: **1943
is the outlier because the mechanism was the opposite of the usual one.** Do not cite its policy
sentence, and if a competing video cites it, that is the correction to make.

## 4 — PADMANABHAN: F3 SURVIVES AND GETS MUCH STRONGER

**S. Y. Padmanabhan, "The Great Bengal Famine," *Annual Review of Phytopathology* 11 (1973),
pp. 11–24.** Read in full. Padmanabhan was appointed **Mycologist in Bengal in October 1943, at the
height of the famine**, later became head of rice research for all India, and describes travelling to
his post on **18 October 1943** past *"dead bodies and starving and dying persons all along the way
from Bahudurabad Ghat on the Brahmaputra to Dacca"* (p. 11). He is an eyewitness and the ranking
technical authority.

### 4.1 The magnitude

His central claim (p. 11):

> *"the principal cause of the short crop production in 1942 was the epidemic of helminthosporium
> disease which attacked the rice crop in that year… **Nothing as devastating as the Bengal
> epiphytotic of 1942 has been recorded in plant pathological literature.** The only other instance
> that bears comparison in loss sustained by a food crop and the human calamity that followed in its
> wake is the Irish potato famine of 1845."*

Table 1 (p. 12) gives 1941-vs-1942 yields at the **Bankura and Chinsurah Rice Research Stations** for
21 varieties:

| Crop group | Range of yield loss, 1942 vs 1941 |
|---|---|
| Aus (early) | **6.8% – 59.2%** |
| Aman (medium-late) | **39.5% – 91.2%** |

The aman losses — the crop that is three-quarters of Bengal's rice — cluster in the **74–91%** band.

### 4.2 The mechanism, which is the part the video should actually use

Padmanabhan does not just assert the epidemic; he reconstructs the weather that caused it and shows
1942 was anomalous in three specific, measured ways against 1941, 1943 and 1944 (pp. 13–16, 24):

1. **Exceptionally heavy September 1942 rainfall** — 40.6–65 cm at the three stations, against
   8.9–33.8 cm in the other three years.
2. **Prolonged November cloud with occasional rain.** November 1942 recorded **63% of possible
   sunshine over 208.8 hours — the lowest November on record in the series**; there were 2–6 rainy
   days in November 1942 against **none at all in November 1943 or 1944**.
3. **Higher minimum temperatures.** November 1942 minima of 19.4–21.1 °C against a normal range of
   16.7–20.0 °C, compressing the daily temperature range.

He then ties each to the pathogen's biology from his own 218 weeks of spore-trapping at Cuttack
(pp. 20–21): spore release peaks October–December and is triggered by exactly this combination —
*"a sudden rise in relative humidity followed by a few dry days… fall in the daily temperature
range… low solar radiation and cloudiness, and… intermittent light showers."* Because those
conditions ran **continuously through November 1942**, *"it is probable that an unprecedented spore
release occurred continuously during November 1942"* (p. 21). Infection requires leaf wetness — 4
hours at 25–30 °C (p. 21) — which the drizzle supplied; susceptibility peaks at flowering and
maturity (p. 23), which is where the aman was; and heavy-rain nutrient leaching and waterlogging
predisposed the plants (pp. 23–24). His summary (p. 24): *"The scales were apparently heavily loaded
against the host and in favor of the pathogen."*

### 4.3 Does it support F3?

**Yes, comprehensively, and it upgrades F3 from a witness statement to a measured agronomic finding.**

F3 currently rests on the FIC's report that *"We have been told by several witnesses about the damage
caused by fungus and 'root-rot'"* and that Bengal's government thought the effects *"even more
serious to the outturn"* than the cyclone. Padmanabhan supplies the identification (*Helminthosporium
oryzae* / brown spot, teleomorph *Cochliobolus miyabeanus*), the measured yield losses, the
meteorological anomaly, and the biological mechanism connecting them. **The Commission's hearsay
becomes an experiment.**

It also **converts the cyclone from a competing explanation into part of the same one** (with Tauger
2003 pp. 65–66): the cyclone's winds dispersed spores and its rain deposited them, at panicle
formation. F3's two clauses are one causal chain.

### 4.4 ⚠ The limit, and it must be stated

**Research-station variety trials are not a sample of Bengal's fields.** Bankura and Chinsurah grow
many cultivars in adjacent plots — including highly susceptible ones — which favours epidemic spread
relative to a farmer's field. The direction of the resulting bias is **unknown**: stations are better
managed (arguing for understatement of field losses) but denser and more varietally diverse (arguing
for overstatement). Note the sanity check: the province-wide aman shortfall on the official series was
**19% below the quinquennial average** (Goswami p. 447), against station losses of 74–91%. Those two
numbers cannot both describe the same thing. Either the official series badly understates the failure
(Tauger's position), or the stations badly overstate it, or both. **We cannot resolve this, and the
script must not pretend to.**

**What the video may say:** the fungus was real, it was identified and measured by the government's
own mycologist, it destroyed 74–91% of the aman yield *at the two research stations where it was
measured*, and it was described by the man who ran India's rice research as the worst rice epidemic
in the recorded literature. **What it may not say:** that Bengal's harvest fell by 74–91%.

## 5 — THE VERDICT THE SCRIPT CAN BE BUILT ON

### 5.1 Verdict

**SETTLED (CORROBORATED, four independent specialists, three independent methods): there was a
genuine food-availability decline in Bengal in 1943.**

- Goswami (1990): −6.1% to −13.8% per capita against 1941, crossing below the Commission's own
  subsistence norm, using Sen's data with the bias set in Sen's favour.
- Islam (1978): the one methodological defence that could rescue the 1941-vs-1943 comparison —
  cancelling systematic error — is shown not to hold for Bengal, with the break beginning in 1941/42.
- Tauger (2003) + Padmanabhan (1973): an independently measured agronomic failure mechanism, with
  yield data that were actually harvested rather than estimated.
- Ó Gráda (2008): *"a harvest shortfall that would have been manageable in peacetime."*

**CONTESTED: its magnitude.** Goswami says ~6–14%. Tauger argues for something near 50%. The
Commission's own series says the 1942 aman was 19% below the quinquennial average. These are not
reconcilable, and the underlying statistics are not good enough to reconcile them.

**CORROBORATED: the decline is far too small to be the whole story.** Prices rose four- to
twenty-fold against a supply decline in the low tens of percent (Bowbrick p. 117; Goswami p. 459). Ó
Gráda's district regressions find the 1942 crop decline does not predict where people died. The
transport collapse, provincial export bans, the inflation, the failure of the Famine Code to trigger,
and the collapse of rural purchasing power do the rest of the work.

### 5.2 What this does to the two-outcome table in `01-VERIFIED-RESEARCH.md`

Neither branch as written. The table offered "Sen is right → the Cabinet diagnosed it correctly" or
"the FAD school is right → grain shipments were the answer." The literature returns a third option,
and it is better television than either:

**The Cabinet was wrong on the facts, and grain shipments were the answer — but the shortfall they
were denying was modest, which is exactly why shipping grain would have worked.** A 10% gap on a
population of 60 million is roughly a million tons. India asked for 500,000 tons in August 1943 and
the Cabinet approved a token 50,000 to Colombo, unearmarked (E1, E3). Ó Gráda's "manageable in
peacetime" is the load-bearing phrase: **this was a shortfall of a size that food aid closes.** The
tragedy is not that the gap was unbridgeable. It is that it was bridgeable and was not bridged,
because three governments had agreed the gap was imaginary.

### 5.3 The closing the evidence actually supports

Three governments held the same theory, in sequence, and it is documented at each level:

- **Calcutta, May–June 1943.** Suhrawardy: *"The doctrine of sufficiency and sufficiency and
  sufficiency must be preached ad nauseam"*; the house-to-house drive from 7 June to *"drag out the
  hoards"* found little rice (Ó Gráda; project G2). ⚠ working-paper sourcing, see §2.6.
- **Delhi, December 1942.** The Government of India's own telegram argued a large initial consignment
  would enhance *"the psychological effect in releasing stocks in India"* (project E10).
- **London, 4 August 1943.** *"the shortage of grain in India was not the result of physical
  deficiency but of hoarding, due to inflationary conditions"* (project E2).

And the man who built the numbers had already said the question could not be answered from them —
Mahalanobis: *"It was quite impossible to judge to what extent the present crisis was due (a) to
actual physical shortage of rice or (b) to failure of distribution"* (Tauger 2003, p. 61).

**Bowbrick's sentence is the closing (p. 120): "The belief in speculation, rather than the
speculation itself, was the villain."** Written in 1986, from the outcome. E2 is that belief, minuted,
in the Cabinet's own hand, with Churchill in the chair.

### 5.4 What the video must NOT claim

- ❌ "1941 was worse and nobody starved" — **retired.** (§1.6)
- ❌ Bengal's harvest fell by half, or by 74–91%. Tauger's extrapolation is a thought experiment and
  Padmanabhan's figures are station plots. (§4.4)
- ❌ "The War Cabinet was broadly right that it wasn't physical deficiency." **Reverse of the current
  F-section table.** They were wrong on the facts; the shortfall was real and below subsistence.
- ❌ Mishra 2019's policy verdict. Hydrology only. (§3.4)
- ❌ "Sen was refuted." He was corrected on the magnitude of the supply shock. His entitlement
  analysis of *who* died and *why those people* is untouched by any of this, and Goswami says so
  explicitly.
- ❌ Any Ó Gráda quotation from the held file, until the published version replaces it. (§2.6)
- ❌ Any Bowbrick quotation, until checked against the published *Food Policy* text — our copy carries
  a 2011 author insertion. (§2.2)

### 5.5 Do specialists still disagree?

**On whether there was a decline: no, not among those who have re-run the data.** Goswami, Bowbrick,
Tauger, Ó Gráda and Islam all accept a real shortfall. Sen's position — *"no remarkable over-all
shortage"* — is compatible with a moderate one, and Goswami, who corresponded with Sen, frames his
own result as a modification rather than a refutation.

**On magnitude, and on how much of the mortality it explains: yes, sharply, and the video should say
so.** That disagreement is *itself* the most interesting thing here, and it is the channel's lane:
the reason nobody can settle the magnitude is that **the underlying numbers were guesses produced by
a method that never measured a harvest.** Islam and Tauger both demonstrate it; Mahalanobis admitted
it at the time. **The video's honest, distinctive claim is not "how much food was there" but "nobody
knew, the Cabinet decided anyway, and the deciding went one way for reasons that had nothing to do
with evidence."**

## 6 — ANSWER TO THE RETRACTION'S FOUR OBJECTIONS

### Objection 1 — "The resolution is circular." **RESOLVED, and the circle broke against F1.**

Three independent exits were found:

- **Goswami** re-derives the answer from the same data but adds the missing stock term. That is not
  circular: it changes the model, not the source, and it inverts the result.
- **Islam 1978** is fully independent of the FIC — a CUP quantitative monograph on Bengal agriculture
  written before the dispute existed — and it invalidates the specific comparison F1 rests on, for a
  documented reason located in 1941/42.
- **Padmanabhan 1973** is independent of the entire administrative statistical apparatus: actual
  harvested yields from research stations, plus meteorological records from Poona and Alipore.

The answer that emerges is the opposite of F1's. Circularity was a real defect and correcting it
changed the verdict — which is the best possible demonstration that the objection was right.

### Objection 2 — "The FIC is not a neutral source." **RESOLVED as to the supply tables; the underlying worry is upheld and is now sharper.**

The literature does not find the FIC's supply tables self-serving. It finds something more damaging
and more useful: **they are not measurements.** Output = assumed normal area × assumed standard yield
× a condition factor set by eye (Tauger 2003 p. 59; Islam 1978 pp. 21, 28, 32). Crop-cutting
experiments used purposive rather than random sampling with inadequate plot sizes (Islam p. 28).
Enumerators selected best fields, biasing upward exactly when crops were damaged (Bowbrick p. 112,
citing Mahalanobis). The 1944 Central Food Advisory Council report called Bengal's statistics
*"chaotic"* (Tauger p. 61).

**Afzal Husain's minute is now fully mapped**, which the retraction specifically flagged. His
sentence — *"absolutely no data are available regarding the stock position of rice (or any other food
grain)"* (FIC p. 179) — is quoted by **Sen (1981 p. 62), Goswami (1990 p. 452 n. 19) and Bowbrick
(1986 p. 113 n. 28)**. All three sides of the dispute rest on the same dissenting commissioner. He is
the most-cited single sentence in this literature and he is a document we hold. ⭐ **He should be on
screen.** Caption him as a dissenting member, per D5.

Note also (Sen p. 62 n. 100) that Husain's scepticism about carry-over was deployed *to argue the
1943 shortage was large* — the opposite of the majority's use of the same uncertainty.

### Objection 3 — "We know one of its figures is superseded." **UPHELD, and now generalised.**

The worry was that if the 1.5M death figure was revised, other FIC figures might be too. Correct, and
it goes further than expected: **the supply figures were never measurements to begin with.** The
death estimate at least rested on registration data the Commission described as inaccurate; the crop
figures rested on visual impressions of a "condition factor." Islam then shows the error structure
*changed* in 1941/42, so the series is not even internally consistent across the years in dispute.

**Practical rule for the script:** FIC tables may be shown as *what the Commission believed and
published*, never as *what was true*. That is a stronger position anyway — it is the video's actual
subject.

### Objection 4 — "Search bias — regexes for terms we already expected." **RESOLVED. The scholarship surfaced four things the primary-document sweep could not have.**

1. **The carry-over.** No keyword search for cyclone/hoarding/exports/denial reaches it. It is
   invisible in the FIC because the FIC had no data on it — the decisive variable is the one the
   primary source is silent about. Only Goswami's reconstruction makes it visible, and it inverts F1.
2. **The nature of the crop statistics.** The FIC prints the numbers; it does not print how they were
   made. Islam and Dewey do.
3. **The fungus as a measured event with a mechanism.** The FIC has "we have been told by several
   witnesses." Padmanabhan has 21 varieties, two stations, four years of meteorology and 218 weeks of
   spore counts.
4. **The negative finding.** Ó Gráda's regressions showing the 1942 crop decline does *not* predict
   district mortality is evidence against our own emerging thesis, arrived at by a method we would
   never have run. This is the clearest vindication of the objection: **scholarship is what tells you
   what your source is not saying.**
