## 1. CAMP A AT ITS STRONGEST — responsibility runs upward

*Stated in the scholars' own terms and, wherever possible, their own words. No adjudication in this
section.*

### 1.1 Janam Mukherjee — famine as the by-product of a policy of priorities

Mukherjee's argument is not that London willed a famine. It is that a chain of decisions, each
defensible in its own frame, had a single organising principle — **keep industrial Calcutta fed and
working, and let the rest of the province take the residual** — and that the province's residual was
zero.

The chain, as he documents it:

1. **Denial (1942)** removes rice and boats from the coastal districts as an anti-invasion measure.
2. **Priority feeding (Aug 1942 onward)** builds a parallel food system for Calcutta's war industry.
   *"'Civil Supplies,' it was well understood by now, meant only supply to 'essential' industrial
   labor in and around Calcutta. **The rest would be left to fate.**"* (JM p.75)
3. **Requisition (27 Dec 1942)** seizes two-thirds of Calcutta's visible stocks and destroys the
   trade's willingness to hold declared stock — driving grain into the black market, which then
   *increases* the apparent hoarding the government is fighting.
4. **De-control (11 March 1943)** abolishes the statutory maximum price. Within a fortnight the price
   of rice, already at twice its December 1942 level, **doubles again** (JM p.103).
5. **The Food Drive (June 1943)** puts 8,000 government employees into the districts to find hoards.
   *"Calcutta itself was excluded."* (JM p.117)

His summary of the ideology: *"Rhetoric about the welfare of the industrial labour-force in Calcutta
was, in fact, central to an ideology that justified various schemes of appropriation and differential
distribution of rice, which, in turn, contributed significantly to the acute impoverishment of the
Bengal countryside, and ultimately famine."* (JM p.148)

And his closing formulation — note that it names capital and colonialism, not one man:
*"the intimately entwined ideologies of war, colonialism and capital exacerbated long-standing
inequalities, resulting in a multifaceted breakdown of the economic, political, and moral order of
the province. It is a massive affair to kill off 3–5 million people in the period of a few short
years, but… it is not a mystery as to how or why that happened."* (JM p.251)

**Mukherjee is not soft on the Bengal politicians.** On the opposition's May 1943 resolution demanding
rationing, an export ban and release of stocks: *"Why these same proposals were not advanced while
the petitioners themselves were in control of the Ministry is not a mystery… Concern for the poor of
Bengal only seemed to ever emerge as an issue — for either party — when it was recognized as
politically expedient."* (JM p.111) **This sentence should be in the video.** It is Camp A's most
credible witness refusing the easy version of his own case.

### 1.2 Greenough — the priority was a *decision*, and it is minuted

Greenough's version of the same mechanism is colder and better quantified, and he states the strategic
logic without flinching:

> *"The priority given to Calcutta in 1943 is hardly surprising. Economic and political strategy since
> early 1942 had concentrated upon the defense of the capital… Thus the coastal districts might be
> conceded to the enemy — as the 'denial schemes' implied — but Calcutta would have to be defended."*
> (GR pp.109–110)

And the consequence, in one sentence: *"The needs of the districts, no matter how urgently pressed
(as the examples of Chittagong and Midnapur revealed), were **relegated to second place**."* (GR p.108)

His judgement on the December 1942 seizure is not the prosecution's: *"It is difficult in retrospect
to agree that the air-raids in December 1942 portended the collapse of Calcutta; they were militarily
ineffective and petered out in early 1943… the government's decision to seize privately held [rice]
had been taken in panic."* (GR p.109) — i.e. **not malice, panic**, and a panic that then made supply
worse: *"the movement of rice into the city slowed to a trickle."*

### 1.3 ⛔ THE BRIEF'S OWN PREMISE IS PARTLY WRONG — Greenough is not in Camp A on causation

**Correction to my own first pass, and to the spawn brief.** I initially wrote that Greenough's
famine-policy chapters do not run through London. **That check came back the other way when I ran it
properly.** Greenough cites Wavell nine times, cites *Transfer of Power* Vol. IV documents 199, 213,
216–218, 221–222, 234, 326–327, 339, 353, 377 and 412, and cites a War Cabinet Paper of 28 January
1944. He is not innocent of the imperial record; he simply concludes something different about it.

**And what he concludes is closer to Roy than to Mukherjee.** His own summary of causation, printed
p.138:

> *"…an unperceived but gradual decline in the per capita availability of rice, along with an
> inefficient grain-marketing system upon which millions of rural consumers depended for subsistence,
> were the underlying conditions which made a famine in Bengal [more likely] than anyone could have
> expected. The disruptions arising from a full-tilt war effort, when combined with local disasters
> and political struggle, and clumsy market interventions, triggered the onset of a lurking food
> crisis. **A search for the man or men who made the Bengal famine thus comes to little now when a
> wider perspective is adopted**, while the strong political feelings at the time made suspect every
> contemporary exercise in assigning blame, including the *Report on Bengal*."*

**Read that beside Roy p.131 — *"The culpability of either Nature, or Administration, or London, or
Market has not been proven"* — and the two camps are not where the brief placed them.** The sense in
which Greenough will call the famine "man-made" is a *social* one: the abandonment of dependants by
*"resource-controlling males — landlords, cultivators, heads of households"* (GR p.138). That is the
"man-made" of chapters 4 and 5, not of the War Cabinet.

**Consequence for the video.** The clean two-camp frame does not survive the reading. What survives is
sharper: **the disagreement is not between historians who blame London and historians who don't. It is
between historians who think the question "who did it" is answerable at all.** Mukherjee thinks it is;
Greenough, Roy and Bose all, in different registers, think it is the wrong question. **Only one of
these four historians is making the argument the video is built on** — which is exactly what a
referee needs to know before scripting.

Note also the nuance in Greenough's own supply position, which is finer than either camp's slogan:
he accepts *"an unperceived but gradual decline in the per capita availability of rice"* (p.138) while
endorsing Sen's demonstration that there was no *sudden* 1943 crash — *"It seems safe to conclude that
the disastrous Bengal famine was not the reflection of a remarkable overall shortage of food grains in
Bengal"* (GR p.290). He also repeats the argument this project has **retired**: 1943's current supply
*"was, in fact, 13 percent higher than in 1941, and there was, of course, no famine in 1941"*
(GR p.286). **Slow decline plus a broken marketing system, not a harvest cliff** — that is Greenough's
actual position, and it is compatible with both Goswami's carry-over finding and Bose's household-level
argument.

## 2. THE CALCUTTA PRIORITY-FEEDING MECHANISM — how the city ate while the districts did not

*This is the section the project's title has promised and never documented. It is quantified below in
four independent registers: who was enrolled, how much they drew, what the government moved, and what
was left for everyone else.*

### 2.1 The scheme, named and dated

- **August 1942** — official rice stocks in Calcutta are *"exceedingly low"* and government begins
  *"worrying in earnest about feeding labor in war-production factories."* **About 100 privately
  owned, government-sanctioned and government-supplied "control shops"** are set up in the city to
  feed industrial employees, and a **Directorate of Civil Supplies** is created under L. G. Pinnell —
  with, on Pinnell's own admission, *"never even… 'the staff to ask for the staff' for months"*
  (JM pp.74–75).
- **Late August 1942** — the **Bengal National Chamber of Commerce launches its "Chambers Foodstuff
  Scheme"**, buying rice and paddy in the districts and supplying member firms directly. The Labour
  Department simultaneously notifies employers that **expenditure on foodstuffs for "essential"
  employees can be written off against the Excess Profits Tax** (JM p.75).
- The scheme's formal name in Greenough is the **"priority classes scheme"** (GR pp.109–111), run by
  *"close consultation between the Chamber and the Civil Supplies Department"* — allocations worked
  out on the size of each firm's workforce, delivered to firm warehouses, sold to employees from
  shops on factory premises.

government offices, and a miscellany of other influential organizations joined the priority classes
scheme until there were **77 participants employing more than a million workers**."*

### 2.3 QUANTIFIED — what they drew

- **5 to 7 kilograms per week** of rice, flour etc. per worker from the priority shops, *"intended to
  subsist both the worker and his family"* (GR p.110).
- **The railways maintained "exceptionally high allotments of 10 kg per week"** (GR p.110 n.62).
- Priority-shop prices stayed at the **August 1942 retail rates — Rs. 7/4 for coarse rice, Rs. 7/12
  for medium — throughout 1943**, while the open-market price rose four- to twenty-fold (GR p.110
  n.62). Greenough's dry note: *"Such amounts had not seemed excessive in mid-1942, but a year later
  with famine raging they seemed very generous indeed."*
- And they could not be cut: *"Once established, the workers' portions could not be reduced without
  disorder, and workers watched over foodstuffs entering factory premises with a jealous sense of
  possession."* (GR p.110)
- Pinnell's own testimony to the Famine Commission on what enforcement looked like: one Howrah mill
  *"succeeded in running into its own siding some trucks of rice intended for the District
  Magistrate, Howrah. He could not get them away in daylight; he tried to move them but the attitude
  of labour was so menacing that it looked as though there was going to be a riot."* (GR p.110 n.63,
  citing *Nanavati Papers*, testimony of L. G. Pinnell, pp.581–82)

### 2.4 QUANTIFIED — the requirement, and the shortfall

- Calcutta's requirement, as the Civil Supplies Department worked it: **~40,000 tons of rice a month
  (and a similar amount of wheat)** (GR p.108 — ⚠ this figure is mangled in the OCR as "'1000 tons";
  it is reconciled by the next page, which contrasts *"the 40,000 tons of rice necessary for
  Calcutta"* with what actually arrived).
- **Against that: only 14,000 tons entered the city before March 1943** (GR p.112).
- *"During these early months of 1943 no more than a two-week supply of grain was ever on hand, and
  when the civil supplies officials sat down with representatives of the priority classes scheme to
  work out allocations, **the deliberations were kept secret to prevent panic**."* (GR p.112)
- *"upon arrival, every boatload and boxcar of rice was destined for a jute mill or control shop.
  Policemen and plainclothes agents were stationed at every road and landing to monitor arrivals."*
  (GR p.112)
- **Only 17,000 tons entered Bengal from all other provinces in the first quarter of 1943** despite
  promises (GR p.112, citing *Report on Bengal* p.54, para. 37c).

### 2.6 The rest of Calcutta, and everyone else

- For the non-priority poor of Calcutta the Bengal government opened controlled-rate shops in
  September 1942, selling **2 seers (about 2 kg) per transaction**, intended to be *"a trickle of rice
  under conditions deliberately made uncomfortable enough to debar any but the very poor"* — by
  mid-1943 there were **120 "control shops"** (GR p.111).
- From **27 May 1943** control-shop hours were cut (afternoon closing, on the stated ground of
  hardship in the queues) and sales were limited to **one seer per head per day** (JM p.112).
- Days later the Bengal government announced **one control shop in each approved market reserved for
  "local people"**, residence to be proved by an **A.R.P. enumeration slip** (JM p.112). ⚠ **Handle
  with care and attribute**: Mukherjee reports that both the A.R.P. and the control shops *"were
  thought by many, including Muslim League Ministers, to be Hindu enterprises,"* and that Fazlul Huq
  had protested the A.R.P. was *"at least 95 per cent Hindu."* **That is a contemporaneous political
  allegation reported by Mukherjee, not a finding — never state it in our own voice.**
- **Rationing did not begin in Calcutta until 31 January 1944** (JM p.180) — after the mortality peak.
  A year later *"Calcutta itself was now fully rationed, but the rest of Bengal, with the exception of
  Dacca and Narayangunge, were without any rationing scheme at all"* (JM p.198).
- The destitutes who walked to the city: **between 80,000 and 150,000** on the pavements after July
  1943, wholly dependent on free relief. Greenough gives five separate contemporary estimates in a
  single footnote (GR p.146 and n.17) — 82,000 (government, early September), *"less than 100,000"*
  (Bengal Revenue Minister, 27 September), 150,000 (Food Member, Government of India, 3 October),
  *"nearly 100,000"* (*Report on Bengal* p.70 para.21), 190,000 (Narayan, September–November).
  **Present the range and the disagreement; that is the honest form.**

### 2.7 The mechanism's sting — the priority firms were the hoarders

**This is the finding that converts §2 from an atrocity list into an argument.** Greenough, quoting
B. K. Acharya, an officer of the Bengal Civil Supplies Department:

> *"One may naturally ask, 'to whom did the traders and speculators sell?' The answer in my opinion is
> that most of them sold to the Chamber of Commerce and other Priorities (several such cases are in my
> knowledge) who were at this time offering fairly high prices for the rice they were stocking…"*
> (GR p.112, citing *Pinnell Papers*, "Memo. on Speculation")

Greenough's own reading of it: the Chambers had struck a *"gentleman's agreement"* to stay out of the
wholesale market during official procurement and had broken it by January 1943; government knew the
large firms were *"dangerous"* because they enjoyed *"unlimited buying capacity"* and could write
food purchases off against Excess Profits Tax; *"It seems entirely probable that such [firms] bid up
the price of rice relentlessly to get what they required; government, bound to stay reasonably close
to its unofficial price levels, and often unable to meet its commitments to the priority classes,
turned a blind eye on these extravagances."* (GR p.113)

Mukherjee documents the same thing from the other end (JM pp.89–90): jute and cotton mills, paper
factories, the Calcutta Port Trust, the big railways, the military — *"and even the Revenue
Department of the Government of Bengal itself"* — continued heavy bulk purchasing in the countryside
during the government's own procurement drive, expenditure written off against the Excess Profits
Tax.

**So: the War Cabinet on 4 August 1943 diagnosed hoarding, and there was hoarding — by a
tax-subsidised, government-chartered, 77-member scheme feeding a million war workers in the city the
Cabinet was defending.** That is the mechanism, it is documented from two archives by two university
presses, and it is exactly the channel's lane.

### 2.8 ⭐⭐ THE MECHANISM'S END — Calcutta was taken off the Bengal market in 1944, and it worked

**This is the counterfactual, and it is not a counterfactual. It is a thing that was done, one year
late.** Greenough, printed p.140:

> *"To allay the fear that Calcutta once again would try to seize the crop, **the viceroy, Lord Wavell,
> publicly pledged in December [1943] that all of Calcutta's wheat and rice needs for 1944 would be
> supplied from outside Bengal**. In January 1944 the Bengal government introduced modified rationing
> in Calcutta. Shortly after this Bengal and other provincial governments were advised to abandon both
> uninhibited 'free-trade' and the requisitioning of cultivators' stocks as unacceptable methods for
> routine food procurement. **These steps… had the desired effect. Prices stabilized below Rs. 20 per
> maund in the new year**, and the continued presence of army officers and troops in the districts
> assured [distribution]."*

**The scale, and the archival locator, from his note 2 on the same page:**

> *"The removal of Calcutta from the Bengal market had been mooted in October, in the first week of
> Wavell's vice-royalty, when he made a three-day trip to Calcutta and Midnapur… See also **Transfer of
> Power, 4: doc. 347, War Cabinet Paper, January 28, 1944, p. 679. Approximately 650,000 tons of rice
> and wheat were to be supplied to Calcutta in 1944**."*

⚠ The printed year in that note reads "1941" in the OCR; the surrounding text and the document date
(28 January 1944) make **1944** the only possible reading. **Verify off the page image and, better,
read TOP Vol. IV doc. 347, p.679 directly — the project holds that volume.**

**Why this is the most important paragraph in the sweep after §5.4b.**

1. **It names the remedy.** Not "ship more grain" in the abstract — *stop Calcutta buying in Bengal,
   supply the city from outside the province, and ration it.* That is a policy with a name, a date, a
   tonnage and a War Cabinet paper.
2. **It was proposed in October 1943 and executed from December 1943 / January 1944** — after roughly
   a million people were already dead.
3. **It worked**, on Greenough's own account and on the Commission's.
4. **It reframes the shipping argument.** The 650,000 tons for Calcutta in 1944 is a comparable order
   of magnitude to the 500,000 tons India asked London for in August 1943 and did not get. **The
   question the video can now ask is not "could Britain have shipped grain?" but "why was the thing
   that ended the famine in 1944 not done in 1943?"** — and the answer, on our own E-series, is that
   until late 1943 all three governments believed there was nothing to fix.

**Greenough also supplies the sentence that makes the priority system's logic visible in one line.**
On what the 18 May 1943 abolition of the inter-provincial barriers achieved:

> *"**The unleashing of the unrestrained purchasing power of a great city on rural Bengal and nearby
> provinces succeeded in supplying Calcutta with adequate food supplies after mid-May 1943.**"*
> (GR p.136)

**Calcutta's supply problem was solved in mid-May 1943. The famine ran until the end of the year.**
That is the whole thesis of the video in two sentences from an Oxford University Press book, and it
is not a rhetorical construction — it is Greenough's own summary, immediately before he describes the
grain finally moving the other way in November and December: *"a strange phenomenon occurred, in
defiance, it seemed, of the generalizations of economic science: **the City began to feed the
Countryside**."* (GR p.136)

### 2.9 The provincial half, from inside the Viceroy's own correspondence

For the apportionment section, and it is verifiable in a volume we hold. Greenough, printed p.137:

> *"The viceroy had been confidentially advised by the Bengal Relief Commissioner in November 1943
> that **the Bengal administration was itself 'a mass of corruption and dishonesty from top to bottom,
> and that pilfering and misappropriation were … on such a scale as to make relief measures largely
> ineffective.'**"*

Wavell *"considered setting [up] special tribunals to try those involved. Nothing came of this

## 3. GREENOUGH ON WHAT THE FAMINE DID SOCIALLY — and what he actually says about mortality

### 3.1 Destitution and abandonment

Greenough's contribution is not the death toll; it is the anatomy of how households broke. His
governing claim, stated in the preface: *"there were **implicit rules for crisis conduct**, rules
which prescribed **who was to be saved** when the social, moral, and economic orders were perceived to
be in collapse."* (GR p.10) He argues these rules favoured adult men and women aged roughly 20–40 —
the reproductively and socially load-bearing — over children, the aged and dependants, and that this
shows up in the age-and-sex mortality data.

The concrete findings, all from the Indian Statistical Institute survey:

- **925,000 families — about 14 per cent of Bengal's land-owning families — sold some paddy land**
  between mid-April 1943 and mid-April 1944. **267,000 disposed of all of it**; 658,000 sold part.
  Total acreage sold: **711,000 acres** (GR p.202).
- **About 700,000 families** suffered "economic deterioration" — a fall to a lower-earning occupation
  — of which perhaps **400,000** were the shift from independent cultivation to agricultural labour
  after selling land, implements and cattle (GR p.206).
- **331,000 persons were found destituted** by the ISI survey, of whom **140,000 had previously
  belonged to cultivating families** (GR p.214).
- On abandonment: *"the abandonment or exclusion of familial dependents constituted the greatest
  social cost of the famine, excepting famine deaths. It exposed women and children to exploitation
  at best and starvation at worst."* (GR p.222)
- On the migration to Calcutta and the collapse of alms: by April 1943 *"the citizens of Calcutta were
  practically being waylaid in the streets by destitutes"* and residential areas *"were overrun by
  in-migrant destitutes begging from door to door"* (GR p.226). Mukherjee gives the Bengali word for
  it — ***durbhikkho*, literally "scarcity of alms"** (JM pp.91–92), which is the better on-screen
  line because it is the language the victims used.
- On what actually killed: *"most famine deaths in 1943 seem to have come about not from starvation
  per se but from starvation-induced diseases of the digestive tract"* (GR p.232) — consistent with
  our H4, and the same caution applies to the phrasing.

### 3.2 ⚠ Bose contests Greenough's central social thesis

**Do not present Greenough's "rules for who is saved" as settled.** Sugata Bose, in the same
literature, calls it overreach: Greenough's point about the Bengali *"cultural ideal of prosperity"*
being used as an ideology of abandonment in a crisis by authoritative male patrons *"is much more
acceptable. **But it is stretching the point to claim that the same ideal constituted an 'active
adaptation' to the crisis by dying victims**."* (BO p.218 n.139, citing Greenough pp.271–2)

**This is a real intra-Camp-A disagreement about the most quotable idea in Greenough's book.** If the
script uses the "who gets saved" frame, it has to carry Bose's objection.

### 3.3 ⭐ THE MORTALITY QUESTION — H2 confirmed from Greenough's own page

**Our filed claim (H2):** *"Greenough's 3.5–3.8M is derived from Sen (D1 p.282 n.6) — not independent
corroboration. Anyone citing Sen and Greenough as two sources is citing one."*

**Verified. Greenough says it himself, in the derivation, in one sentence.** Appendix C, "Famine
Mortality, 1943–46", printed **pp.308–309**:

> *"…the sum of all three classes in all subdivisions amounts to **3,123,000 deaths**. (Mahalanobis
> indicated that a sampling fluctuation or error of 300,000 should be kept in mind with this figure.)
> To this we need to add an **18 per cent correction for the infants under one year** who were
> excluded. The resulting figure is **3,685,140 or about 3.7 million deaths**. From this 3.7 million
> the 'normal' mortality must be subtracted… Mahalanobis employed for this purpose a figure calculated
> from the 1931 census. This seems unnecessarily cautious and is bound to underestimate famine
> mortality. **A better figure is the average 1941–42 mortality, as given by the civil registration
> data and suitably corrected in the fashion indicated by Amartya Sen.** This 1941–42 average is
> approximately 1.7 million. Thus from the 3.7 million we subtract 1.7 million to obtain an overall
> 1943 mortality of about **2.0 million deaths**. This is more than 800,000 above Sen's calculation of
> 1.17 million deaths for 1943. **If we add these 0.8 million to Sen's estimated 2.7 to 3.0 million for
> 1943–46, we get a total of between 3.5 and 3.8 million as the excess mortality due to the famine.**"*

**So the structure of Greenough's number is: Sen's total, plus a 0.8M top-up for 1943 derived from the
unpublished ISI survey.** Between 77 and 86 per cent of it *is* Sen's figure. **H2 goes to SETTLED.**

Three further things on this page that matter:

1. **Greenough's independent contribution is real but narrow.** The 0.8M increment comes from data
   Sen did not use — the **unpublished ISI mortality data**, a stratified sample of **13,358
   households in 40 of Bengal's 86 subdivisions**, submitted to the Famine Commission and never
   published. Greenough notes the Commission's failure to use them is *"curious"* (GR p.304). That is
   a genuine archival finding and should be credited to him.
2. **Greenough explicitly endorses Sen's total as sound**: *"Sen's method seems the best that can be
   applied to registration data which are inherently incomplete. **His total figure of 3 million is
   entirely plausible.**"* (GR p.302)
3. **He is candid that his own figure is an outlier**: it is *"more than twice the figure given by the
   famine commission, and it is larger, by at least 500,000 deaths, than the highest reputable figure
   previously calculated."* (GR p.236)

**Chattopadhyay, separately.** Greenough is also the source for the caution our H2 records about the
3.5M "Calcutta University" figure: Chattopadhyay's *"sample was poorly selected and the projection of
the sample to the entire rural population was improper"*, and he *"had released his figure at a time
of political debate on the famine, when the larger the total famine mortality, the bigger the stick to
beat one's opponents"* (GR p.300). ⚠ That page is heavily OCR-degraded; treat as INSPECTED and verify

## 4. ROY AT HIS STRONGEST — and a fair test of our framing of him

### 4.1 What Roy actually argues

Roy's case has three layers, and only the third is about Churchill.

**Layer 1 — the structural claim (RN, 2012).** Colonial-era famine response was limited less by will
than by fiscal and administrative capacity, and the standard nationalist and entitlement framings
both obscure this:

> *"famines did not happen because the government was fattening itself at the expense of the poor
> peasant; **famines happened because the poverty of the peasant left the government too poor to deal
> with the effects of crop failure**. British India was one of the poorest states in the world. State
> capacity was constrained by limited fiscal resources."* (RN p.26)

With the numbers: *"Hardly 5 per cent of the national income of India at the time Dutt wrote his
treatise was available to the state to spend. Corresponding proportions for Japan and the major
Western European states were in the range of 15–20 per cent."* And in the early nineteenth century
average tax per person in India was *"less than one-tenth of that in England, half of that in Ireland,
a quarter of that in Australia… and a quarter of that in Mauritius"* (RN p.26).

And on the epistemic limit, which is the part that transfers to 1943: *"The capacity would include
being able to know where the rains had failed, and where food stocks were running low. That kind of
weather-related economic intelligence no pre-modern state had the means to collect."* (RN p.28)

**⚠ Honest scope note, direct check performed.** *Natural Disasters and Indian History* **does not
treat the 1943 famine.** Searched the full text for `1943`, `Bengal famine of 1943`, `Suhrawardy`:
**two hits**, one a passing methodological remark (RN p.28) and one an index entry ("famine, Bengal
1943, 28"). Its chapters are 1770, 1783, 1837, 1866 and the Deccan. **It is background for Camp B's
theory, not evidence about 1943, and it should not be cited as the latter.**

**Layer 2 — the 1943 argument (RP, 2019, pp.128–131).** Three moves:

- *(a) The evidence cannot settle the availability question, as a matter of method.* *"Sen's claim is
  difficult to test, because of the way crop statistics were collected in British India. Between one
  year and the next, the statistical office would measure changes in cropped areas and then apply a
  standard or average productivity in order to estimate the total output… it was not good at recording
  change if there was, say, flood damage or disease."* Then, fairly: *"This problem has led several
  scholars to revise the crop output data, including Mark Tauger, Peter Bowbrick, Omkar Goswami and
  Mufakharul Islam. **Their work shows that the harvest size probably fell**."* (RP p.129)
- *(b) Bengal was not a despotism, and the food system was not a command system.* *"Bengal did not
  have a despotic rule in this time… provinces were ruled by elected governments. The administration
  was a government formed by a democratic process. **Food was distributed by the free market and was
  not subject to command and control. The little command and control the government did try to impose
  were not very effective because the government had few resources.**"* (RP p.129)
- *(c) The operative failure was provincial political dysfunction.* *"Democracy as Bengal had did not
  help. Within the government, between ministers, and between the ministers and bureaucrats, there
  was fierce conflict, rivalry, and misinformation about the famine. The most powerful opinion was
  that there was no shortage of food in Bengal… In 1943, the key decision-maker in the government was
  the Minister of Civil Supplies, Hussein Suhrawardy. Throughout the famine, he was an aggressive
  campaigner for the no-shortage theory, and throughout, his colleagues, critics, and officers
  contradicted him. **This quarrelsome bunch of relief-givers weakened and delayed response.**"*
  (RP pp.129–130)

**Layer 3 — Churchill (RP p.130).** Roy's target is Madhusree Mukerjee. His argument is not that the
Cabinet behaved well; it is that **Churchill's personal racism is the wrong variable**:

> *"As political history, the argument is naive. **There is little evidence that Churchill's personal
> views about Indians influenced the policies of the War Cabinet.**… Churchill's reactionary views on
> the empire notwithstanding, the context for almost everything he said about Indians and the empire
> was related to the Indian nationalist movement… Racist or not, no Prime Minister would be willing to
> fight a war and negotiate with the nationalists at the same time."*

And the causal claim: *"What has any of that to do with the famine? Very little. **The War Cabinet did
not divert enough ships from the theatres of war to Bengal or order India to divert army rations to
feeding people because the Cabinet believed what the Bengalis told it: there was no shortage of food
in Bengal.** The Cabinet took decisions in the knowledge that the axis powers were sinking one ship
every day and had sunk around a million tons of shipping in 1942. The regions where rice might be
available were the most dangerous waters to enter. Army rations were already reduced. Further cuts
could risk a mutiny."* (RP p.130)

His closing, which is the strongest single defence sentence in the university-press literature and
which the video is obliged to show:

> *"**The Bengal Famine of 1943 has never been explained. It offers no definite lesson. The
> culpability of either Nature, or Administration, or London, or Market has not been proven.**"*
> (RP p.131)

### 4.2 Is our framing of Roy accurate or dismissive?

**Our filed framing:** Roy's Churchill remarks are *"economic-historical interpretation, not a
Cabinet-file audit."*

**Verdict: accurate as a description, and it must not be used as a dismissal — because Roy never
claimed to be doing a Cabinet-file audit, and his argument does not depend on one.**

The evidentiary facts, checked:

- **Roy cites no archival source for any claim in the Churchill passage.** The chapter's notes (RP
  p.132) run to ten items: Mahalanobis & Bhattacharya on population; Patell on plague; Bhatia; Loveday;
  Ambirajan; the 1880 Indian Famine Commission; his own 2012 book; Bayly and Marks on information;
  Tagore; and a tenth note that is a list of twenty-eight scholars' names with no page references.
  **There is no citation to CAB 65, CAB 66, Mansergh, the India Office records, or any 1943 Cabinet
  document anywhere in the chapter.** The shipping figures (*"one ship every day"*, *"a million tons
  of shipping in 1942"*) are likewise unsourced in the text and notes.
- **So on the narrow question — "did the War Cabinet's own minutes support the claim that Churchill's
  views were irrelevant?" — Roy offers assertion, not evidence.** That is a fair and checkable
  statement, and it is the honest form of our framing.
- **But the claim he is making is a negative one about causal relevance**, and our own E-series
  agrees with it more than the project has noticed. Our filed verdict is *"Not 'Churchill deliberately
  starved Bengal'"*; the E-documents show a **collective Cabinet** acting on a **transport minister's
  precedent-based advice** (E3/E9), against its **Secretary of State's** objection (E4), on a
  **factual diagnosis** (E2). None of that requires Churchill's opinions about Indians, and none of it
  is refuted by Roy. **Roy and this project converge on the negative claim and diverge on the
  positive one: whether the Cabinet's decisions were culpable given what it had been told.**
- **Where Roy is vulnerable, and it is not where the project has been aiming.** His causal sentence —
  the Cabinet *"believed what the Bengalis told it"* — is a claim about the transmission of a belief,
  and it is testable against documents we hold. E10 shows **Delhi** supplying the *"psychological
  effect in releasing stocks"* framing in December 1942; E11e shows the India Office telling the
  Cabinet on **31 July 1943** that *"famine conditions are already reported as having begun to
  appear"*; E11f shows Amery warning in writing of *"a most serious risk"*; E7 shows the Chiefs of
  Staff and the incoming Viceroy pressing in September. **The Cabinet was not only hearing the
  no-shortage line.** Roy's sentence is defensible as an account of what the Cabinet found convenient
  to believe; it is not defensible as an account of what it was told. **That is the specific,
  document-backed rebuttal — and it is a much better one than "he didn't read the files."**

Two of Roy's own findings cut against the Cabinet, and a referee has to use them:

1. **On hoarding — the exculpation collapses in the defence's own text.** *"The administration acted

2. **On the harvest** — *"Their work shows that the harvest size probably fell"* (RP p.129) — Camp B's

### 5.2 A4 (corrected) — provincial export bans, Bihar under Governor's rule

**Status: SUPPORTED, but our account stops halfway and the second half is stronger.**

- **The bans are confirmed** by Bose's archival trail and by Mukherjee, and Bose adds the mechanism
  behind Bengal's own: government procurement in the coastal districts under denial required
  Rs. 1.5 crore from the Government of India *"as the principal merchants refused to move the stocks
  unless it had the financial backing of the government"* (BO p.95 n.74, citing Gov. to Gov. Gen.
  telegrams, L/P&J/5/149, IOR). **That is a new primary-document trail we do not have.**
- **⛔ The half we are missing: the barriers came DOWN, and it made things worse.** On **18 May 1943**,
  at the demand of Bengal's own Director of Civil Supplies, *"the Government of India… abrogated the
  'Basic Plan' and all trade barriers between Bengal and its neighboring provinces of Bihar, Orissa,
  and Assam were abolished overnight."* The Famine Inquiry Commission's finding, quoted by Mukherjee:
  *"The introduction of free trade led immediately to the invasion of the provinces of Bihar, Orissa
  and Assam by a large army of purchasers from Bengal."* Bihar's government protested that *"Bengal
  merchants or their agents went into the interior villages and offered fantastic prices, as a result
  of which the arrivals of supplies in local markets were extremely poor [and] prices fluctuated
  almost from hour to hour due to wild speculation."* Orissa called it *"undoubtedly the greatest
  factor in causing high prices, hoarding and the un-availability of food grains to consumers in the
  latter part of 1943."* Prices in Bihar and Orissa rose immediately; **in Calcutta only a transitory
  dip was registered** (JM pp.111–112). **[INSPECTED — JM pp.111–112, quoting the FIC and the two
  provincial governments]**

  **Why this matters to the script.** Our current line is *"three provinces sealed their borders
  against Bengal."* True — and then the Centre opened them, and the result was that Bengal's buying
  power wrecked its neighbours' markets while barely touching Calcutta's price. **The barrier was not
  the binding constraint. Purchasing power was.** That is a mechanism, it is HOW not WHY, and it is
  the channel's exact register.
- **⚠ Precision point on Bengal's constitutional status.** Mukherjee dates **Section 93 emergency rule
  in Bengal to 31 March 1943**, after Governor Herbert engineered Fazlul Huq's resignation; Nazimuddin's
  ministry took office **24 April 1943** (JM pp.105–107). So **Bengal had no ministry for roughly
  three and a half weeks at the start of the famine year** — and the new budget was signed into law by
  the Governor alone on 1 April (JM p.106). Our A4 correction says Bengal had a ministry. That is right
  for the famine's peak and wrong for the spring. Say "from late April" or cite the gap.
- **⚠ Roy contradicts the framing, not the fact.** *"provinces were ruled by elected governments. The
  administration was a government formed by a democratic process"* (RP p.129). For Bengal after
  24 April 1943 that is correct; for Bihar in 1943 it is not (our A4c, Transfer of Power III, Doc. 331);
  for Bengal in April 1943 it is not. **Roy's generalisation is too broad, and we can show the
  documents.**

### 5.3 ⛔ G2 (corrected) — did the "no shortage" doctrine come down from Delhi or up from Calcutta?

**Status: CONTRADICTED as a one-way arrow. This is the most consequential correction in the sweep.**

**Our filed position:** the doctrine came *down* from Delhi rather than *up* from Calcutta.

**Roy says the opposite, explicitly and as his central causal claim:** Suhrawardy was *"the key
decision-maker"* and *"an aggressive campaigner for the no-shortage theory"*; the Cabinet failed to
act *"because the Cabinet believed what the Bengalis told it"* (RP pp.130).

**Mukherjee says both, and gives dates for each.** He puts Suhrawardy's press note — *"The high price
of rice was the result of hoarding by stockists and cultivators alike, and had no relation to any
actual shortage"* — in late April 1943, and then writes:

> *"Suhrawardy's position was, in fact, **conspicuously resonant with that of the Government of
> India**."* (JM p.109)

And the evidence he gives for the resonance runs **downward**:

- **December 1942** — Linlithgow to Amery: *"building up of large reserves by middle-class consumers
  and the tendency on the part of the small subsistence farmer to keep back more of his grain than
  usual for his own consumption"* are the primary factors inhibiting supply to Calcutta (JM p.109).
- **April 1943** — **the Government of India's Regional Commissioner for Civil Supplies, Mr Justice
  Braund, broadcasts on All-India Radio**: the crisis is consumers withholding rice; *"there is
  nervousness and panic which makes a man produce the very scarcity that he dreads."* Mukherjee's
  observation: *"**No mention was made of industrial concerns.**"* (JM p.109)
- **Second week of May 1943** — **Azizul Haque (Food Member of the Viceroy's Executive Council) and
  Major-General E. Wood (Secretary of the Food Department) travel to Calcutta**, meet Suhrawardy at
  Writers' Building, hold a joint press conference citing *"psychological factors"*, and
  **Major-General Wood produces figures showing that Bengal had more foodgrains at its command than
  it had in 1939** (JM p.110). **Delhi supplied Calcutta with the statistics for the no-shortage
  line, in person, three weeks before the deaths began to be counted.**

**The honest restatement.** [CONTESTED — Roy 2019 p.130 against Mukherjee 2015 pp.109–110]

> The no-shortage doctrine was not handed down and it was not sent up. **It was held simultaneously in
> Calcutta, Delhi and London, each reinforcing the others, and each able to cite the others as
> corroboration.** Delhi's version is the earliest in the record (December 1942) and Delhi supplied the
> arithmetic (May 1943); Bengal's minister was its loudest and most consequential advocate; London
> made it the basis of a decision and then used it in Washington (our K2).

**Script consequence: cut any line that assigns the doctrine a direction of travel.** The stronger and
safer claim is the one all four scholars support — that three governments held the same convenient
theory at once, and that this is *why* nobody's disagreement stopped anybody.

### 5.4 ⛔ K3 HIT 1 — the magnitude, and whether "the gap was bridgeable" survives

**Status: it does not survive. Retire the sentence. But the sweep hands us something better.**

**Why it does not survive.** Roy's objection is stronger than Bowbrick's because it is about the
instrument rather than the answer: the colonial crop-reporting system measured *acreage* change and
applied a *standard yield*, so it was structurally incapable of registering loss from flood, storm or
fungus — the exact three mechanisms operating in 1942–43 (RP p.129). **A dataset that cannot see the
shock cannot be adjusted into a number precise enough to divide into 500,000 tons.** Roy's own summary
is *"The evidence on this episode… does not support any claim conclusively"* (RP p.129).

**Bose does something more interesting than picking a side — he dissolves the question.** In a
footnote that is the best single sentence in the literature on this dispute:

> *"the lines of academic battle as at present drawn appear to subsume a variety of elements, not
> unconnected with one another, in any explanation of famine, under the rival categories of 'food
> availability decline'… or 'decline in exchange entitlements'… The predicament of the small paddy
> cultivators in the Bengal famine would indicate that **it is important to show how fluctuations in
> food-grain availability for peasant family economies (and this may happen without macro-level
> disasters) relate to their shifting exchange entitlements in the food market**, and what bearing this
> has on the phenomenon of starvation."* (BO p.95 n.74)

Bose's own reconstruction of the mechanism, which is Camp-neutral and entirely quotable:

- The Paddy and Rice Enquiry Committee had found in the late 1930s that *"The great majority of the
  paddy-growers have neither a saleable surplus nor sufficient food to last them throughout the year.
  It is this third class that forms the bulk of the agriculturists of this province."* (BO p.95,
  quoting *Rice Cmt. Report* vol.1 p.27)
- Therefore Sen's assumption that rice producers could not suffer a deterioration *vis-à-vis* rice is
  *"potentially misleading"*: **most rice growers were net buyers.** *"In 1942–3 the diminished
  harvest reduced the direct entitlement of many rice-growers, especially in west Bengal, **turning
  would-be net sellers into net buyers and would-be small buyers into large buyers**. Moreover, many
  small peasants had sold immediately after the harvest and were taken unawares by the unprecedented
  rise in prices."* (BO p.95)
- And Sen's blind spot: *"the more important question of the exchange rate of rice in relation to
  **jute** is left out of account… The jute-growing, grain-deficit districts of east Bengal were the
  areas hardest hit by the famine."* (BO p.94)
- Corroborated at the time by the Government of Bengal's own Director of Rural Reconstruction,
  H. S. M. Ishaque, in a March–April 1943 note: *"More jute means less paddy and the price of paddy at
  the moment is about one and a half times that of jute. Principal jute-growing districts cannot,
  therefore, be considered well-off."* (BO p.91, from the West Bengal State Archives)

| Calcutta, rice and paddy in terms of rice (tons) | 1941 | 1942 |
|---|---:|---:|

### 5.5 H1 — the 1.8–2.4M range

**Status: COMPLICATED, not contradicted — and the complication is specific and checkable.**

None of these four books engages Dyson & Maharatna (1991); three of them predate it. Where they land:

| Source | Figure | What it rests on |
|---|---|---|
| Greenough 1982 | **3.5–3.8M**, of which ~**2.0M in 1943 alone** | Sen's 2.7–3.0M **plus** 0.8M from the unpublished ISI survey (GR p.309) |
| Bose 1986 | *"Some 3 million died"*; the FIC's 1.5M is *"a clear underestimate"* | Cites **Sen and Greenough** (BO p.96 and n.76) — i.e. the two sources H2 shows are one |
| Janam Mukherjee 2015 | ISI: **2 million in 1943 alone**; his own summary, **3–5 million** over the war years | ISI 1943 estimate (JM p.140); the 3–5M is his own framing (JM p.251) |
| Roy 2019 | No figure given in the famine passage | — |

**The live disagreement, stated precisely.** Greenough's **2.0M excess for 1943 alone** is close to
Dyson & Maharatna's **2.1M for the entire event**. That is not a small gap and it is not rhetorical.
**But the lever is the same one D&M identify**: the choice of "normal" mortality to subtract.
Mahalanobis used a 1931-census baseline; Greenough replaced it with a Sen-corrected 1941–42 average of
~1.7M *"as given by the civil registration data and suitably corrected in the fashion indicated by
Amartya Sen"* (GR p.309). **So Greenough's 1943 figure inherits Sen's correction factor**, which is
exactly the quantity D&M revise. And Mahalanobis's own sampling error on the 3,123,000 base was
**±300,000** (GR p.308).

**Recommendation: H1 stands, and the on-screen wording should acknowledge the older consensus rather
than ignore it.** Our H6 formulation already does this correctly. **Do not add Greenough or Bose to
the corroborating column for any figure above 3 million — they are downstream of Sen, and Greenough
says so.**

## 6. THE APPORTIONMENT QUESTION, ANSWERED AS A REFEREE

### 6.1 Where serious scholars put the weight

| | Bengal ministry / provincial administration | Delhi | London |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Roy (RP)** | **Primary.** Suhrawardy, ministerial conflict, misinformation, no administrative capacity to run a controlled distribution | Secondary; the Basic Plan and the free-market decisions are not his focus | **Minimal, and not causal via Churchill.** London acted on what Bengal told it, under a real shipping war |
| **Janam Mukherjee (JM)** | **Heavily culpable, both parties, by name.** But it is the *structure* of priority feeding, not the ministers, that does the causal work | **Central.** Denial, the Excess Profits Tax write-off, the Basic Plan and its abrogation, the Viceroy's instruction to bypass elected ministers and use the ICS for procurement (JM p.93) | **Culpable at the import margin.** The January 1943 refusal (130,000 t against 600,000 t requested) and the Ceylon trade-off (JM p.93); the 1944 refusal of any further programme (JM p.187) |
| **Greenough (GR)** | **The proximate decision-maker.** The priority classes scheme, the December requisition, the failure to ration until 1944 are Bengal-government acts. He also carries the Bengal Relief Commissioner's *"mass of corruption and dishonesty from top to bottom"* (p.137) | Present as the fiscal and strategic frame | Implicit: the defence of *"the second city of the empire"* is the premise the priority rested on (GR p.110) — **but he declines to apportion at all**: *"A search for the man or men who made the Bengal famine thus comes to little"* (p.138) |
| **Bose (BO)** | Ministry conservative and slow; the FIC's toll a *"clear underestimate"* | Denial policy financed by the Government of India | Not his subject. His causation is **structural and long-run** — jute–rice terms of trade, the post-depression collapse of grain credit, the microfundium (BO p.275) |

### 6.2 Where the disagreement is genuine, and where it is only emphasis

**GENUINE — three questions, and all three are open:**

1. **Did food availability actually fall, and by how much?** Greenough (with Sen) says no; Roy says
   probably yes and unmeasurably; Bose says the question is mis-specified because household-level
   availability can collapse without a provincial decline. **Nobody in this set claims the magnitude
   is known.** Our file's "CONTESTED, ~6%–50%" is, if anything, more confident than the field.
2. **Is London a causal node at all?** Roy: no — the Cabinet's decisions follow from what Bengal told
   it. Mukherjee: yes — the import refusals are decisions with dates. **This is a real disagreement
   and it is the video's actual subject.** It is decidable in part, on our own E-series: the Cabinet
   was told repeatedly and by multiple channels that Bengal was in famine (E11e, E11f, E7, K1), which
   damages Roy's "believed what the Bengalis told it" as an account of *information* while leaving it
   intact as an account of *motivated belief*.
3. **The death toll.** ~2.1M (D&M 1991) against ~3–3.8M (Sen 1981 and everything downstream). **One
   ancestry, two generations, and the field's textbook number is the older one.**

**EMPHASIS ONLY — four points on which all four scholars agree, and which the video can therefore
state flatly:**

1. **Calcutta was fed first, deliberately, as war policy.** Roy does not contest it; Greenough
   quantifies it; Mukherjee narrates it; the Commission's own appendix tabulates it.
2. **The hoarding diagnosis was false as an explanation** — including in the defence's own text (RP
   p.131).
3. **The Bengal ministry's conduct was bad, and both communal blocs played politics with food.**
   Mukherjee (p.111) and Roy (pp.129–130) agree, from opposite directions.
4. **The Famine Inquiry Commission's 1.5M is too low.** Bose p.96, Greenough p.298, Roy implicitly.

### 6.2b ⚠ The uncomfortable count

**Of the four scholars read, three decline to apportion.** Roy: culpability *"has not been proven"*
(p.131). Greenough: the search for the man or men *"comes to little"* (p.138). Bose: the famine is the
culmination of a long-run structural contradiction (p.275) and the FAD/entitlement battle-lines are
mis-drawn (p.95 n.74). **Only Janam Mukherjee builds a chain of responsibility, and even he makes it a
chain of ideologies — *"war, colonialism and capital"* (p.251) — rather than of men.**

**This is the finding a referee is obliged to put in front of the creator before scripting.** It does
not mean the video's charge is wrong; our E-series is a documentary record none of these four
examined at that granularity, and Greenough and Bose wrote before it was convenient to. But it does
mean the video cannot say *"historians agree that responsibility runs upward."* **They do not.** The
defensible sentence is narrower and better:

> Most historians of this famine do not think the question "who did it" has an answer. The Cabinet
> minutes are still the Cabinet minutes.

### 6.3 The referee's answer

**Nobody with a chair says one government caused it, and nobody with a chair says none of them did.**
The apportionment that survives all four books is a sequence rather than a share:

> **Bengal's administration chose who ate — the 77 priority participants, a million war workers, the
> control shops. Delhi built the frame that made the choice necessary — denial, the Excess Profits
> Tax write-off, the Basic Plan and its abrogation — and supplied the arithmetic for the belief that
> nothing more was needed. London was the only one of the three that could have changed the total,
> was told repeatedly that it needed to, and decided on 4 August 1943 that the problem was hoarding.**
> Each of the three had a theory that excused it, and the theory was the same theory.

**The honest caveat, and it belongs on screen:** the shortfall London declined to fill cannot be
sized. That uncertainty is not a defence — a government that acts decisively on an unmeasured quantity
owns the outcome — but it does mean the counterfactual ("500,000 tons would have ended it") cannot be
asserted, and this project should stop asserting it.

**What can be asserted instead, and it is stronger because it is not a counterfactual.** The measures
that ended the famine are known, because they were taken: **take Calcutta off the Bengal market and
supply it from outside the province (~650,000 tons, 1944); ration the city; stop both free-trade and
requisitioning; put the army on distribution.** Wavell pledged the first in December 1943; the Bengal
government began the second in January 1944; prices stabilised (GR p.140; TOP IV doc. 347, p.679).
**Every one of those four levers was available in 1942. None was pulled, because until the autumn of
1943 Calcutta, Delhi and London all believed there was nothing to fix.** That is the apportionment —
not a share of blame, but a shared belief, and the belief is documented in all three capitals.

### 6.4 Where I have a thumb on the scale, and where the evidence is thin

- **This sweep confirmed more of our file than it broke, which is a warning sign, not a comfort.**
  Three of the four books predate the specialist literature we already rely on. Greenough (1982) and
  Bose (1986) cannot corroborate a 2026 finding about Goswami 1990 or Dyson & Maharatna 1991; they can
  only be *consistent* with it. **I have not treated their agreement as corroboration anywhere above,
  and neither should the script.**
- **I got one of my own absence checks wrong and it changed a conclusion.** My first pass asserted that
  Greenough's famine chapters do not run through London. Running the check properly returned nine
  Wavell hits and a dozen *Transfer of Power* citations — and with them Greenough's actual verdict,
  which is that the apportionment question *"comes to little."* **The claim is withdrawn in §1.3 and
  logged in the appendix.** It is exactly the failure mode the never-assert-absence rule exists to
  catch, and it caught it only because the rule requires naming the terms.
- **Only one of the four is a live opponent** — Roy, and he is opposing Madhusree Mukerjee, not us.
  **We have not yet read a university-press scholar who has read the Cabinet minutes and thinks the
  Cabinet was right.** ⚠ That absence is a real gap in the project's evidence base and I am not
  claiming it does not exist — only that these four books do not contain it.
- **The Calcutta split table (§2.5) is the strongest new thing here and it is OCR-derived.** Every
  digit needs the page image. If any of them moves, §2.5's headline ratio moves with it.
- **Communal politics.** Every characterisation in §2.6 and §6.1 involving Hindu/Muslim allegation is
  attributed to the scholar or to the named contemporary who made it. **None of it is stated in our
  voice, and none of it should be.** Mukherjee's own handling is the model: he reports the allegations
  and then documents that both parties' concern for the poor tracked political expediency (JM p.111).
