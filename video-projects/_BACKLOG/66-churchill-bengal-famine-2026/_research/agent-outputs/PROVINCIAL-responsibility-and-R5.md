## 1. THE AUTHORITY MAP

### 1.1 Peacetime layer (already established in the project — C1/C2, unchanged)

### 1.2 ⭐ WARTIME LAYER — Defence of India Rules, **rule 81** — FOUND AND READ

**Located:** DIR **rule 81**, printed **pp. 132–136**, pdf pp. 131–135. Marginal note: **"General
control of Industry, etc."** Sub-rule (2) begins on printed p. 132 and its clauses run to p. 136.
**Status: INSPECTED (page images read directly; the PDF's OCR text layer for this volume is badly
degraded and must not be quoted from).**

**Rule 81(2), chapeau — printed p. 132, verbatim:**

> *"The Central Government* ⁸*[or the Provincial Government], so far as appears to it to be necessary
> or expedient for securing the defence of British India or the efficient prosecution of the war, or
> for maintaining supplies and services essential to the life of the community, may by order
> provide—"*

**Rule 81(2)(a) — printed p. 133 — THE MOVEMENT POWER. This is the authority the export bans ran on:**

> *"for regulating or prohibiting the production, treatment, keeping, storage, **movement,
> transport, distribution**, disposal, acquisition, use or consumption of articles or things of any
> description whatsoever* ¹*[and in particular for prohibiting the withholding from sale, either
> generally or to specified persons or classes of persons, of articles or things kept for sale, and
> for requiring articles or things kept for sale to be sold either generally or to specified persons
> or classes of persons or in specified circumstances;]"*

**Rule 81(2)(b) — printed p. 133 — THE PRICE POWER:**

> *"for controlling the prices* ³*[or rates] at which articles or things of any description
> whatsoever may be sold* ³*[or hired]* ⁴*[and for relaxing any maximum or minimum limits otherwise
> imposed on such prices or rates.]"*

**Rule 81(2)(aa) — printed p. 133 — bears on boat denial** (inserted 21 Sept 1940): power *"for
controlling the rates at which any vessel registered in British India may be hired and the rates at
which persons or goods may be carried in or on any such vessel."*

#### ⭐ The load-bearing detail: WHEN the province got this power

The words **"or the Provincial Government"** are not original to rule 81(2). They are an amendment.
Footnote **8** on printed p. 132:

> *"Inserted by D. C. Dept notification No. **899-SM/41**, dated the **29th November 1941**."*

(The same notification 899-SM/41 of 29 Nov 1941 also inserts "or the Provincial Government" into
clause (c) and clause (f) — footnote 5 on printed p. 134 and footnote 3 on printed p. 135.)

**What that establishes, cleanly and on one page:**

1. Until **29 November 1941**, rule 81(2) was a **Central Government** power only. The power to
   prohibit the movement of foodstuffs across India was Delhi's alone.
2. From 29 November 1941 the power was **concurrent** — the province could exercise it too. Bengal's
   ban of **1 June 1942**, Bihar's of **1 July 1942** and Orissa's of the same month all fall in the
   **seven months after** the amendment.
3. **Delhi did not lose the power when it shared it.** The text is "The Central Government *or* the
   Provincial Government" — Delhi kept its own. **So the inter-provincial barriers were not something
   the Centre lacked authority to remove. It had held that exact authority since 1939 and never gave
   it up.**

Point 3 is the mechanism the whole divided-responsibility half turns on, and Law-Smith reaches the
same conclusion from the archive rather than the statute:

> *"The 1935 India Act together with Defence of India rules gave the Viceroy the authority to
> intervene in provincial matters in prescribed circumstances. The crisis in Bengal warranted his
> intervention but Linlithgow, who put the 1935 Act in place, saw it as a last resort step which
> risked the progress of future reform by Constitutional means; **a step he did not take**."*
> — **LS p. 54**, her note 23 citing *Government of India Act, 1935, Sections **93, 52(1)(a), 126A***

**The 29 Nov 1941 insertion date — INSPECTED/SETTLED** (footnote read off the same page).
**"Bengal's June/July 1942 bans were made *under rule 81(2)*" — INFERRED, not yet SOURCED.** No source

### 2.2 Fazlul Huq — steelman first

Ó Gráda's characterisation, attributed to him:

> *"Fazlul Huq, first minister of Bengal from 1937 to 1943, was more willing to collaborate with
> Hindu politicians, and **not trusted by the colonial authorities, who connived in the collapse of
> his weakening coalition in late March 1943 and its replacement by a more pliant Muslim League
> administration**, headed by Khawaja Nazimuddin."* — **ÓG p. 15**

Brennan puts it harder and in the active voice: Herbert *"forced the resignation of Fazlul Huq's
ministry"* (**BR p. 546**). **Note the divergence and attribute it** — "connived in the collapse of"
(Ó Gráda) and "forced the resignation of" (Brennan) are not the same claim, and the video should use
whichever it can source, not blend them.

Two things the Huq ministry gets credit for, from opponents' mouths:

- Its agriculture minister, the **Nawab Bahadur of Dacca**, announced in **February 1943** that
  estimated 1942/3 rice production (**6.9 m tons**) fell far short of requirements (**9.3 m tons**) —
  i.e. **the outgoing Indian ministry publicly stated there was a shortage**, months before the War
  Cabinet's 4 August "not physical deficiency but hoarding" (project E2). ÓG p. 11.
- A prominent opposition leader in the July 1943 Assembly debate noted that the previous ministry
  had at least *"declared that there was shortage of food in Bengal and they made the Government of
  India accept that position"*, whereas Suhrawardy had played *"a colossal hoax"* on the people.
  ÓG p. 13, citing *Statesman* 15/7/1943.

⚠ The Nawab's February statement is also blamed for the price spike: *People's War* said it
*"encouraged hoarding right and left"* (ÓG p. 12). **Both things are true and the script should say
both** — telling the truth about the shortage moved the price. That is the honest version of the
"was candour possible?" question the video is actually about.

### 2.3 ⭐ H. S. Suhrawardy — the reconstruction, dated

This is the spine of the provincial half. **All of it is Ó Gráda's newspaper reconstruction unless
marked otherwise**, and Ó Gráda's own sources are *The Statesman* (S) and *Amrita Bazar Patrika*
(ABP), cited by date. Treat the underlying newspaper dates as the checkable layer.

1. **Takes office 24 April 1943** (BR p. 546) — i.e. **after** the crisis was already acute in
   Midnapore and east Bengal (ÓG p. 5, March–April).
2. **Insists there is enough food.** *"In power, its main spokesman, H.S. Suhrawardy, insisted at
   first that despite local difficulties and maldistribution, the province contained enough food. He
   promised soon to publish 'full statistical details' that would clearly show that there was 'a
   sufficiency'…"* — **ÓG p. 12**, citing S 3/5/1943.
3. **The Suhrawardy plan, 11 May 1943.** *"'Sufficiency' was the cornerstone of the Suhrawardy plan,
   announced on May 11th, which promised to identify hoards and arrange for their fair
   re-distribution, and to open more controlled shops… establish food committees… help organize a
   Grow More Food campaign, and 'bring in supplies from outside'. Its slogan, **'do not grind the
   faces of the poor'**, was directed at hoarders."* — **ÓG p. 12**
4. **He is contradicted immediately, by named scholars, in public.** Prof. **Radhakamal Mukherjee**
   at a Howrah meeting: it was *"not safe to take too optimistic a view of Bengal's food resources at
   this juncture and stress… psychological factors… rather than the economic factor of serious
   actual food shortage."* — **ÓG p. 13**, S 16/5/1943. **This matters enormously: the "psychological
   factors" framing the War Cabinet later used (project E10) was being publicly refuted in Calcutta in
   May, by an Indian economist, in a newspaper.**
5. **Food drive I, from 7 June 1943.** Excluded Calcutta and Howrah. Local food committees plus
   **thirty thousand temporary workers**. Suhrawardy framed the committees as an extension of the
   *panchayat* and promised not to intervene except *"where persuasion has failed, or where a surplus
   in one area has to be transferred to a deficit area."* His public vow: to *"enter every household
   and look under every taktaposh and…drag out the hoards."* — **ÓG pp. 21–22**
6. **It found nothing.** *"The drive unearthed little rice, however. At first, Suhrawardy claimed
   that boats and carts had been used to conceal stocks; some, he said, had been shifted 'into
   jungles'. Soon however, it was clear that the vast majority of the rural population was short of
   food; the drive had revealed **'an acute shortage'**."* Under pressure for numbers he admitted he
   had none: *"the general picture was that in most places a deficit had been reported."* — **ÓG p. 22**
7. **He nonetheless told the legislature a number.** *"seven to eight million maunds [or 0.2 to 0.3
   million tons]"* was *"approximately…the quantity that has been discovered in hoards."*
   — **ÓG n. 28**, citing **S 6/7/1943**
8. **The early-July climb-down — the single best verbatim from this layer:**
   > *"I have found criticisms made against me that I had stated that there was no shortage when
   > actually there was serious shortage in the Province. I do not plead guilty to the charge. It
   > appeared to me that **insistence on shortages would only increase panic and stimulate hoarding**
   > and thereby aggravate the general food scarcity and push up prices."*
   > — Suhrawardy, **ÓG pp. 13–14**, citing **S 6/7/1943**
9. **Food drive II, 7–8 August 1943, Calcutta and Howrah.** Both days declared public holidays.
   **2,850 officers visited 250,000 units** (≈100 each), each accompanied by two volunteers. Merchant
   stocks of **20 maunds** or more required a licence; the official ration view was **1.25 maunds per
   person** (excluding under-4s) for the rest of the year. — **ÓG p. 23**
10. **It also found nothing.** Within a fortnight Suhrawardy conceded Calcutta stocks in the hands of
    consumers, traders and employers were *"not considerable"*; the food-search had turned up about a
    **month's** subsistence for the city. — **ÓG pp. 23–24**
11. **The Government of India's own verdict on his drives** — confidential Food Department memo,
    forwarded by **Linlithgow to Amery, 7 September 1943**:
    > *"The much-heralded 'anti-hoarding' drive in the Bengal districts and in Calcutta has achieved
    > very little that is positive. The Bengal Government themselves do not claim that it is more than
    > a 'food census', disclosing stocks in the districts amounting to rather more than **300,000
    > tons**. The Bengal Government emphasises that this is 'stock', and is in no sense 'surplus',
    > except to a negligible extent. **In Calcutta itself practically no stocks were disclosed which
    > would be classified as 'hoards'**…"* — **ÓG p. 24**
12. **Still at it in mid-October**, describing the crisis as *"unprecedented famine"* while still
    appealing to cultivators and traders to release stocks; the *Statesman*, which had backed him,
    now cited his *"earlier disingenuousness or ill-informed propagandistic optimism."*
    — **ÓG pp. 10, 14**, S 12/10/1943

**Ó Gráda's own framing of Suhrawardy — quote it as his, and note it is a defence as much as an
indictment:**

> *"Suhrawardy, new to his post, was probably misled at first by the gospel of 'plenty' propagated by
> the colonial authorities. Soon enough, he saw that Bengal was in serious trouble, although **under
> pressure from Delhi and London to stress 'sufficiency' and 'hoarding'**. There was little effective
> that he could do…"* — **ÓG p. 9**

**⚠ Bias check, both directions.** Ó Gráda's paper argues a thesis — that the famine was caused by
*"the failure of the imperial power to make good a harvest shortfall"* (ÓG p. 32) — so his Suhrawardy
is a man squeezed from above. Brennan, working the relief archive rather than the newspapers, is
harsher and puts Suhrawardy's *political* dominance at the centre of the failure (see 2.4). **A script
that uses Ó Gráda's chronology with Brennan's judgement, or vice versa, is cherry-picking. Say that
the two archives produce two emphases.**

### 2.4 ⭐ Brennan's counterweight: the structural finding, and it is the strongest thing in this pass

Brennan's argument is not about a villain. It is about **two departments and no single authority** —
which is exactly this channel's HOW>WHY lane.

- Bengal ran food through **two** departments: **Civil Supplies (DCS)** — "supply", moderate the
  market — and **Revenue** — "relief", the Famine Code. Each had a minister and an executive officer,
  **and over the famine all four changed.** BR pp. 543, 562.
- The **one chance to merge them died with the ministry**: Banerjee accepted Civil Supplies alongside
  Revenue on 11 March 1943 *"because… the control of civil supplies was inseparable from dealing with
  the famine of unprecedented character that faced them"* — **and the merger was lost on 29 March
  when Herbert forced Huq out.** BR p. 546.
- After 24 April, **DCS dominated**: *"Suhrawardy, an aggressive Muslim League politician, was far
  more powerful in the cabinet than the new revenue minister, T. N. Mukherjee."* BR p. 546.
- The consequence, in a Bengal official's own words to the Commission — **S. Banerjee**, revenue
  secretary from 3 August 1943:
  > *"when the Revenue Department attempted to 'take a little active interest in this matter [relief]
  > we were told that after all it was the concern of the Civil Supplies Department, as it was not a
  > question of relief under the Famine Code but only a question of supply."*
  > — **BR p. 547**, Nanavati 1944:837
  and, of the Revenue minister: *"My Hon'ble Minister did not appear to be aware of the position even
  then and in the Conference of Collectors held in July **H. M. Revenue was not even asked to be
  present**."* — same page.
- **Brennan's attribution of the "supply" doctrine names both layers together:**
  > *"The dominance of the 'supply' theory can partly be explained by the political position of its
  > supporters — **Herbert and Suhrawardy**."* — **BR p. 561**

  **That single clause is the divided-responsibility thesis in six words, from the archive-richest
  scholar of provincial relief: the British Governor and the Bengali minister were on the same side
  of the argument.**
- Brennan's control test — and it is the reason the "colonial collapse" explanation fails:
  > *"a consideration of the food crises across India at this time shows that **only in Bengal was
  > there a massive collapse of the relief process**. Bombay and Madras provinces, each of which was
  > seriously deficient in food grains, were able to order their economies and administrations to
  > secure a degree of equity in food availability… Of equal significance, however, were their
  > **unified political structures**."* — **BR p. 562**
- Whose words Brennan uses for the diagnosis: **Fazlul Huq's own, 1942** — the *"difficulty [of] the
  task of coordinating the responsibility of the Ministers with the responsibility that the
  departmental officers owe to the administration as a whole."* BR p. 562, GBHP 1942 file 362.
- Brennan's final apportionment, verbatim, and it is deliberately two-handed:
  > *"The tragedy of the 1943 famine must be laid at the door of the forces that destroyed the rice
  > market in Bengal and led to the reluctance of other provinces to export food to the province. But
  > there was also a failure, mainly in the higher political and administrative reaches, to understand
  > the situation, to work together to establish an equitable relief policy, and to coordinate its
  > implementation."* — **BR p. 563**

### 2.5 ⭐ Governor John Herbert — an active food-policy actor, not a bystander

This is where R5's second half is won, and the evidence is better than the project currently knows.

- **He set up the Calcutta controlled shops himself.** *"Herbert attempted to contain prices by
  setting up a system of controlled shops in Calcutta where the workforce could purchase rice at
  subsidised prices, instead of linking wages to cost of living indices or paying dearness
  allowances."* — **LS p. 55**, citing GFR 5 Sept 1942.
- **He said, in writing, that he could direct policy — because the ministries were weak.**
  > *"Herbert considered that both parties were reluctant to take any firm stand which might
  > jeopardise their insecure majorities, **making it easier for him to direct policy**."*
  > — **LS p. 50**, her notes 1–2 citing **Governors' Fortnightly Reports, L/P&J/5, Feb–Apr 1943**
  and Nugent's 1977 Queensland PhD, p. 342–3.

  **This is the exhibit that decides R5.** It is the Governor of Bengal, in his own fortnightly
  reports to the Viceroy, in the months around the famine, recording that ministerial weakness let
  him steer. The claim "Bengal had self-rule" cannot survive it.
- **He forced out the elected premier** (BR p. 546) — see 2.2.
- **He warned Delhi repeatedly and was disregarded.** Law-Smith lists the dates from the fortnightly
  reports: Herbert informed Linlithgow of the deteriorating rice position on **2 January, 3 January,
  5 February, 18 March, 28 April, 7 May, 6 June and 16 July** 1943. — **LS p. 60**
- **He sounded the alarm privately in early July — and Linlithgow read his own evidence backwards:**
  > *"Bengal Governor John Herbert began to sound the alarm in private in early July, but his report
  > that the food drive had located only **100,000 tons** in stocks of 400 maunds or more was
  > interpreted by Linlithgow as **evidence of 'how much is in fact available'**."*
  > — **ÓG p. 8**, n. 28 citing Mansergh 1973: 44, 60
- **The Pinnell letter, and Herbert's death.** Ó Gráda's n. 27, verbatim:
  > *"Herbert died of cancer at the height of the famine. His widow worried that his attempts to raise
  > the alarm, which were ignored by Linlithgow, would be forgotten. See British Library, OIOC…
  > **Mss. Eur. D911/9, letter from Government House to L. Pinnell, 1/1/1944**."*

  Law-Smith corroborates the illness and the timing independently: *"the previous month [September
  1943], due to illness, Herbert, Governor of Bengal, was replaced by Rutherford as Acting-Governor
  until the appointment of Casey who took over in January 1944."* — **LS p. 50**

  **Status: CORROBORATED** that Herbert was withdrawn ill in September 1943, that he died of cancer,
  that he had warned Linlithgow repeatedly, and that his widow feared this would be forgotten.
  **⚠ NOT VERIFIED: Herbert's exact date of death.** Neither source gives one. **Do not state a date.**

  ⏳ The Pinnell letter (**Mss. Eur. D911/9, BL/OIOC**) is the single most cinematic unseen document in

### 2.6 Communal politics — attribute, never adopt

Everything in this sub-section is **Ó Gráda's characterisation**, and the script must say so.
Corroboration from Shila Sen / Harun-or-Rashid / Janam Mukherjee is **NOT YET DONE** (§7).

- *"Bengali Muslims were poorer and less educated, but well mobilized politically. The poorest strata
  among the peasantry were disproportionately Muslim… Hindu politicians were more likely to represent
  landlord and trading interests, as well as the genteel and literate bhadralok."* — ÓG p. 15
- *"the hoarders targeted by the Muslim League during the famine were likely to be Hindus."* — ÓG p. 16
- Contested on both sides at the time: the League was criticised for giving contracts to **Isphahani
  Brothers**, League supporters; the **Hindu Mahasabha** attacked the government and *"big firms,
  particularly non-Bengalis"* for holding stocks. — ÓG p. 16
- The Communist Party (**P. C. Joshi**, *People's War*) backed the food drives and kept attacking
  hoarders after the shortage was conceded; **Asok Mitra** later castigated them, saying that *"if
  anyone were hoarding to the point of forcing a famine on the country it was the central and
  provincial governments and their purchasing agents."* — ÓG p. 27
- Ó Gráda's own conclusion is the moderating one and should be carried if any of the above is:
  > *"The problem in Bengal in 1943 was **not internecine strife**, but the failure of the imperial
  > power to make good a harvest shortfall that would have been manageable in peacetime."* — **ÓG p. 32**
- And his regression finds **the Muslim share of population had no explanatory power** for excess
  deaths or the birth-rate fall (PCMUSLIM insignificant; ÓG p. 31). **If the script touches communal
  politics at all, this result is the guardrail** — the famine did not kill along the communal line.

⚠ **Bias warning to the script.** There is a live rhetorical trap here: "Bengali Muslim ministers ran
the food policy" is exactly the sentence a bad-faith clip would lift. The evidence does not support
loading the famine onto the League. It supports: a weak two-department provincial government, a
Governor inside its decisions, a Centre that would not open the borders, and a War Cabinet that would
not send the ships.

## 3. R5 ADJUDICATED

**The audience claim:** *"In 1943 Bengal was not ruled by Britain, it had self-rule, it was governed
by Bengali Indians."*
**Project's current rating:** partly true, fundamentally misleading.
**After this pass: the rating STANDS, and is now carried by evidence rather than by the statute alone.**

### 3.1 Steelman — what the claim gets right (all CORROBORATED)

1. **The subjects genuinely were provincial.** GOIA 1935 List II items 27/29/30/32 (project C1).
2. **The wartime power genuinely was shared with the province** from 29 Nov 1941 — DIR 81(2), §1.2.
3. **Bengal had an elected ministry through almost all of the famine**, and its ministers made the
   decisions that are on the charge sheet: the sufficiency doctrine, the food drives, the export ban
   of 1 June / 16 July 1942, the price-control order of 20 August 1943.
4. **The Viceroy said so himself, in writing, in August 1943** — to Sircar, of the Bengal judiciary:
   > *"…you make various suggestions in your letter as to what Government should do. **Government in
   > this case is the Provincial Government in Bengal … it is to Provincial Government that it will
   > fall to deal with them**…"* — **LS p. 60**, n. 71 (Linlithgow to Sircar, Nanavati Papers File 4)
5. **The 1945 Commission agreed**: *"While other Governments in India were admittedly faced with a
   much less serious situation than the Government of Bengal, their generally successful handling of
   the food situation … stand in contrast to the failure in Bengal."* — FIC, quoted **LS p. 51**
6. **Brennan's control case supports it partly**: Bombay and Madras, also grain-deficient, did not
   collapse (BR p. 562). Provincial capacity was not uniformly denied by the Raj — Bengal's failed.

**That is a real case, and the script must put it before the rebuttal.**

### 3.3 The verdict, in the form the script can use

**"Self-rule" is the wrong word for a government whose premier the Governor could remove, whose
policy the Governor recorded he could direct, whose borders only Delhi could open, whose currency
Delhi inflated, and whose neighbour Bihar had no ministry at all.**

**But "Britain ruled Bengal and Indians had no part in it" is also wrong.** Indian ministers held real
statutory power over food supply and poor relief, and used it — to preach sufficiency, to run two
fruitless house-to-house searches, and to ban rice exports at the province's own border in June 1942.

⚠ **Honest limit, and the script should say it.** On item 10, the causal *sequence* of the sufficiency
doctrine is **CONTESTED, not settled**. Ó Gráda names the mantra as shared three ways — *"Amery in
London, Linlithgow in Delhi, and Suhrawardy in Calcutta"* (ÓG p. 7) — without settling who moved
first. Braund's memo (LS p. 60) says Bengal was *forced into* it by Delhi. Brennan says the
anti-shortage propaganda *"derived from the Government of India as well as from Suhrawardy"*
(BR p. 546 n.8). **G2 in `01-VERIFIED-RESEARCH.md` currently says the War Cabinet's 4 August diagnosis
was "three months downstream of Bengal's own minister preaching it." That directional claim is not
supported — the sources point the other way or refuse to choose. G2 needs rewording before it is
scripted.** The defensible line is: *the same doctrine was being spoken in all three capitals at once,
and the men in Calcutta said they were under pressure from the other two.*

### 4.4 Damage to households (context, not attribution)

**1.7 million land transfers in 1943**; **22.9 %** of families forced to sell or mortgage all or part
of their paddy land; families in debt roughly **doubled** 1943→1944 (ÓG p. 26, Table 1 p. 32).
**65 %** of excess mortality mid-1943 – mid-1944 occurred in east Bengal (ÓG p. 29, after Maharatna 1996).

### 4.5 ⚠ The honest answer on attribution

**No source we read apportions a share of mortality to the provincial layer, and none can.** Brennan
comes closest — his Faridpur comparison shows the northern subdivisions, which got cash relief, had a
death rate of **7.21 %** against **10.29 %** in the south, which did not (BR p. 557, death rates from
Greenough 1982:307–8). **He does not present this as a controlled estimate, and it must not be
presented as one.** His own words are guarded: *"These figures point to the value of the relief that
was given to the northern subdivisions before July 1943."*

**Do not put a percentage of the dead on the provincial layer. The defensible statement is:
relief reached a small single-digit percentage of the population where it was tried, was cut in July
at the worst moment, and was skewed toward loans for cultivators rather than food for the destitute —
and where cash relief did reach people, the death rate was measurably lower.**

**Three corrections to G5:**

1. **"Destroyed" is not Brennan's published claim.** In print he says *not located*. The word

   Ó Gráda reports it as a **suspicion**. **On screen it must be attributed exactly that way:** *"the
   historian who went looking for them suspects they were destroyed; what he published is that they
   could not be found."* Anything stronger is not supportable.
2. **It is not only Suhrawardy's department.** Brennan's footnote covers **both** the DCS **and the
   Revenue Department** — i.e. **both halves of Bengal's food and relief machinery**.
3. **A substantial body of DCS working papers does survive — in London, in a British official's
   private file.** Every one of Brennan's DCS citations (DCS 1943a, 1943b, 1944a–1944i) resolves to
   the **Pinnell Papers, MSS Eur. D911, India Office Records** — including *"Control by Embargo"*
   (D911/4), *"Causes of the Famine"* (D911/1), *"Figures of Despatches of Rice/Paddy ex Calcutta
   During 1942"* (D911/4) and Pinnell's circular to all district officers of 25 March 1943 (D911/2).

### 5.1 ⭐ The second destruction — better documented, and the script should prefer it

Law-Smith records that **the Famine Inquiry Commission's own evidence was taken on the understanding
that it would be destroyed**:

> *"Evidence was taken in camera with the intention that **the collected information be destroyed when
> the Report was completed**. One member of the Commission, however, retained a copy of the collected
> evidence together with some off-the-record statements, and **it is on the basis of this material
> that the selective use of evidence to protect the Government of India becomes apparent**."*
> — **LS p. 51**, note 7 citing **B. Bhatia, *Famines in India* (London, 1967), p. 310 n. 4**; note 8
> identifying the survival as the **Nanavati Papers, National Archives of India, New Delhi**

That member was **M. B. Nanavati**. **Both** Brennan and Law-Smith — the two archive-rich accounts of
the provincial layer — build their case out of the file one commissioner declined to destroy.

### 5.2 What this means for the video, and it is a beat, not a footnote

The channel's subject is what the documents can and cannot settle. Here the answer is unusually sharp:

- **London's half is on the record.** The War Cabinet minutes of 4 August, 24 September and 10 November
  1943 are printed, published, page-numbered and already rendered as exhibits in this project.
- **Bengal's half is not.** The provincial departments' famine files were not found in the West Bengal
  Archives. What survives is a British official's private collection in London, a commissioner's
  retained copy of evidence that was meant to be destroyed, and two Calcutta newspapers.
- **So the asymmetry in the historiography is partly an asymmetry in the archive.** It is easier to
  prove what London decided than what Calcutta decided, and any confident apportionment between the
  two is standing on uneven ground. **Say that on screen.** It is the most intellectually honest thing
  in the episode and it costs the thesis nothing.
