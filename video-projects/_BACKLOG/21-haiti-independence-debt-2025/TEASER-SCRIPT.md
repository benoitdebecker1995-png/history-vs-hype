## COLD OPEN — The Ransom Note (0:00-0:45)

**[B-ROLL: 1825 French Ordinance filling screen immediately]**

This isn't an invoice. It's a ransom note.

**[ZOOM: Key French text — Article 2]**

Dated April 17th, 1825. Delivered to Haiti at gunpoint. Demanding 150 million francs.

**[B-ROLL: Map showing Louisiana Territory]**

For context — 22 years earlier, France sold the Louisiana Territory to the United States. Two million square kilometers. Seventy-seven times the size of Haiti. Price: 80 million francs.

**[B-ROLL: Return to ransom note]**

Haiti's bill was almost double that. For zero territory.

They were paying for the "loss" of enslaved human beings.

**[Beat]**

But the 150 million wasn't even the full demand.

**[B-ROLL: Calculation graphic]**

The French commission valued Haiti at 1.5 billion francs. The ransom was one-tenth of that — a down payment.

And France still needed 14 warships to collect it.

## BEAT 1 — The Threat (0:45-2:15)

**[ON SCREEN: "JULY 3, 1825"]**

**[B-ROLL: Map showing French fleet approach + document overlay]**

July 3rd, 1825. Fourteen French warships. 528 cannons. A man named Baron de Mackau — France's envoy — carrying sealed orders from Paris.

**[TALKING HEAD]**

Here's what those orders actually said.

**[B-ROLL: Text on screen — FRENCH then ENGLISH]**

Mackau wrote to his superiors:

> "My mission to the people of our former colony is not a negotiation... I lack the powers to make the slightest modification."

**[TALKING HEAD]**

This wasn't diplomacy. Mackau was a bagman delivering an ultimatum.

**[B-ROLL: Next section of instructions]**

And if Haiti refused?

> "Announce to the leaders of that government that henceforth they will be treated as enemies of France."

The instructions specified a blockade until the island had — exact wording — "submitted itself, without any conditions, to the supremacy of France."

**[Short declarative]**

Not recognition. Submission.

**[TALKING HEAD]**

And these weren't abstract threats. Eighteen months earlier, Haitian President Jean-Pierre Boyer — an independence war veteran who'd been running the country for seven years — had told his army:

**[B-ROLL: Boyer proclamation text]**

> "You will be determined to bury yourselves under the ruins of our country, rather than submit to the ignominious yoke of the tyrants."

**[Beat]**

That was January 1824. By July 1825, Boyer folded.

What changed? The cannons arrived.

## BEAT 2 — The Inside Man (2:15-4:15)

**[ON SCREEN: "THE INSIDE MAN"]**

**[TALKING HEAD]**

Boyer appointed a commission of advisers to review the terms. Their response was immediate.

According to historian Laurent Dubois: "The members of the commission, shocked by what they read, rejected the document immediately and categorically."

**[Short declarative]**

The experts said no. Boyer said yes anyway.

**[TALKING HEAD]**

On July 7th, he convened a secret council — senators and generals. But here's the critical detail.

**[B-ROLL: Boyer's three questions on screen — translated]**

He never showed them the actual French document.

Instead, he presented three questions he'd written himself.

**[B-ROLL: Question 1 on screen]**

First: "Can the King of France reserve half-tariffs for French commerce?"

**[B-ROLL: Question 2 on screen]**

Second: "Could France use this against future trade deals?"

**[B-ROLL: Question 3 on screen]**

Third: "Since independence is finally being recognized, wouldn't rejecting this risk losing the only chance to conclude this great affair?"

**[TALKING HEAD]**

Notice how Question 3 is framed. Not "should we accept extortion?" but "can we afford to miss this opportunity?"

According to Beaubrun Ardouin — a 19th-century Haitian historian who had access to participants — Boyer "was able to induce them to think" the terms were similar to earlier negotiations.

He manipulated his own council.

**[Beat]**

On July 11th, Boyer accepted. The Haitian Senate heard the terms read aloud — then the document was locked in a velvet case.

It took months for Haitians to learn what Boyer had actually agreed to — when newspapers from France finally arrived describing the terms.

**[B-ROLL: American Monitor quote on screen]**

The American Monitor — a newspaper at the time — put it bluntly:

> "By purchasing the acknowledgment of what was before an undisputed fact, he has afforded room to doubt that Haiti had the means of keeping that which she had begged herself to buy."

**[Short declarative]**

From "bury yourselves under the ruins" to a velvet case in 18 months. That's what 528 cannons and one inside man can do.

**[TALKING HEAD]**

And it wasn't safe to say so. Félix Darfour, a journalist at the national printing office, wrote to the Chamber of Deputies accusing Boyer of having "sold the country to the whites."

Darfour was arrested, tried in a military court despite being a civilian, and executed.

That was the price of public opposition.

## BEAT 3 — The Trap (4:15-5:45)

**[ON SCREEN: "THE TRAP"]**

**[TALKING HEAD]**

The ransom was bad enough. But France designed a system.

**[B-ROLL: Payment schedule graphic + money trail arrows]**

The first installment — 30 million francs — was due December 31st, 1825. That was more than Haiti's entire annual budget.

Haiti didn't have the cash. So they borrowed from a French bank — Ternaux Grandolphe. The bank provided only 24 million after fees, but required repayment of the full 30 million.

**[B-ROLL: Money trail: Haiti → French Bank → French Treasury]**

As historian Anthony Phillips documents: the money made "a short trip across Paris" — from the French bank's vaults to the French treasury.

**[Short declarative]**

Within months, Haiti owed the ransom AND the loans to pay the ransom. Economists call this the "double debt."

**[TALKING HEAD]**

By 1838, Haiti was drowning. France "reduced" the total to 90 million francs. Sounds like mercy. But to pay the reduced amount, Haiti had to take out another massive loan — from another French bank.

The reduction was a refinancing. The extraction continued.

**[B-ROLL: Budget percentage graphic — animated]**

By 1898: 50 percent of Haiti's entire government budget went to France and French banks.

By 1914: 80 percent.

**[Short declarative]**

80 percent. That's not an economy. That's an extraction zone.

**[B-ROLL: Eiffel Tower + bank logo]**

And some of the profits from Haiti's debt helped finance the Eiffel Tower. France's most famous monument — built while Haiti was paying to exist.

## CLOSE + TEASE (5:45-6:45)

**[TALKING HEAD]**

The debt wasn't fully paid until 1947. A hundred and twenty-two years. Five generations.

Haiti is the only country in history where the enslaved compensated their enslavers. Britain abolished slavery in 1833 and British taxpayers paid the slaveholders. France flipped the script entirely — the people who'd been enslaved paid their former enslavers.

That's what makes this case unique in world history.

**[Beat]**

But there's more to this story. An American banker who was simultaneously vice president of a New York bank AND Haiti's national bank — and used that position to seize $500,000 in gold. A US Marine general who later confessed: "I helped make Haiti a decent place for the National City Bank boys to collect revenues in." France's own lawyers, who said in 1829 that the whole thing was illegal — and France collected anyway.

And the president who finally ended it in 1947 and called it Haiti's "financial emancipation."

**[B-ROLL: Return to 1825 Ordinance — slow zoom to Article 2]**

That's the full story. I'm working on it now.

If you want more evidence-based history that goes to the actual documents — subscribe. Sources are in the description.

**[END SCREEN]**
