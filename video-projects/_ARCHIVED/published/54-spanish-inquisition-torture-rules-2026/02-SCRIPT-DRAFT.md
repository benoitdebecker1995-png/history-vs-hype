## §1 — HOOK (0:00–0:35)

Everyone thinks they know what the Spanish Inquisition was.

Torture chambers, thumbscrews, dungeons where people disappeared — centuries of religious terror operating entirely outside the law.

That last part — outside any law — is where the story breaks down. They didn't act outside the law. They wrote one — and they wrote it down. The rules for torture, who had to be in the room, what could and couldn't be done, what made a confession legally valid. And the exceptions.

[ON SCREEN: Compilación de las Instrucciones del Oficio de la Santa Inquisición — title page]

This is the Compilación de las Instrucciones — a procedural manual for running an Inquisition tribunal, written in fourteen eighty-four and used for over two centuries. One paragraph in particular contains everything the rest of this video is about. Paragraph fifteen.

[ON SCREEN: Compilación f.6v — §XV — Spanish text]

## §2 — TURN: THE DOCUMENT (0:35–1:30)

It was written in fourteen eighty-four — by Tomás de Torquemada, the first Grand Inquisitor. The rules for everything: how to investigate suspected heretics, how to interrogate them, how to torture them, how to sentence them.

Ron Hassner — a political scientist at UC Berkeley — spent years in the Inquisition archives. Here's what he found.

"The torture practices of the Inquisition were the result of centuries of institutional learning... It tortured as part of a bureaucratic procedure designed to collect information. It tortured in cold blood."

[Hassner, *Anatomy of Torture*, p. 18]

After September eleventh, CIA interrogators tortured rashly, with no oversight and no records. Hassner calls that "hot-blooded torture" — improvised, vindictive, reserved for whoever seemed guilty.

The Inquisition is the opposite of that. Hassner calls it the most extraordinary interrogational torture campaign in recorded history. And the Inquisition's records survived. That's why we know exactly what happened to Marina González.

## §3 — THE RULES (1:30–2:30)

April twenty-ninth, fourteen ninety-four. Ciudad Real, Spain.

A scribe sits beside Marina González in the torture chamber — not to help her, but to write down everything.

[Hassner, *Anatomy of Torture*, pp. 12–13]

The scribe's first line:

"She was stripped of her old skirts and put on the rack, and her arms and legs were tied tightly with cords."

The only reason we have this record is because article fifteen required it.

[ON SCREEN: §XV — highlight personnel clause]

To legally authorize a torture session, the Instrucciones required four people in that room before anyone touched her: the inquisitor, a representative of the local bishop, a notary to record every word spoken, and a doctor monitoring the prisoner's condition throughout.

Before any of that, the accused had to be warned three times on separate days — urged to confess voluntarily. Only after a written order from the inquisitors could the session begin — limited to three methods, chosen because they were the least likely to cause permanent injury.

And if the prisoner confessed?

[ON SCREEN: §XV — highlight ratification clause]

Here's what §XV says, word for word:

"If the accused confesses the crime under torture, and afterward ratifies or confirms his confession on the next or third day, he shall be punished as convicted."

[Homza, *The Spanish Inquisition, 1478–1614*, pp. 67–68]

In other words, a confession under torture couldn't convict you on its own. What counted was coming back the next morning — unbound, free — and repeating the same thing voluntarily in front of witnesses. The evidence wasn't what you said under torture. It was the ratification.

## §4 — THE LOOPHOLE (2:30–3:10)

Now read the last line of that same paragraph.

[ON SCREEN: §XV — highlight final sentence]

"The aforesaid does not deny that the inquisitors can repeat the question of torture in a case where they must and can do so by law."

[Homza, *The Spanish Inquisition, 1478–1614*, p. 68 fn. 11]

Same article, same page — the rule and its escape hatch, written together.

In practice, inquisitors called this *suspensión* — suspension. Instead of ending a torture session, you suspend it, and the next session is legally a continuation of the first, which means the prohibition on repeat torture technically never applies.

Hassner writes on page twenty-seven: "The procedures of the Inquisition prohibited torturing an individual more than once. This guideline could be circumvented by suspending a torture session, rather than ending it."

The loophole wasn't discovered later or added by someone else — it was written into the original text by the same hand that wrote the rule. Multi-day torture wasn't an abuse of the system. It was built in from day one.

## §5 — THE CASE: DÍAZ DE CÁCERES (3:10–3:55)

A century later, those same instructions were in use on the other side of the Atlantic.

March second, sixteen-oh-one. Mexico City.

Hassner reconstructs the case from the surviving Inquisition trial records.

[Hassner, *Anatomy of Torture*, p. 115]

Antonio Díaz de Cáceres is brought to the torture chamber. He's been in an Inquisition cell since December fifteen ninety-six. The charge: Judaizing — secretly practicing Jewish rites after forced conversion to Christianity. His family members have already testified against him.

After his first interrogation in fifteen ninety-seven, he sat in his cell for nearly three years without questions or contact. The Inquisition kept prisoners incommunicado — Hassner: "The prisoner could receive no information from friends or relatives about who was arrested subsequently or who testified before the tribunal." Four years passed before they brought Díaz back to the chamber.

He is hoisted to the ceiling by ropes tied to his twisted arms, then dropped — twelve times. Ropes are wrapped around his calves, thighs, and shins and tightened — twelve times.

Seven jars of water are forced down his throat.

"He screamed that they were killing him, but he revealed nothing."

Under §XV — the same rules that authorized the session — silence counts as innocence. The Inquisition took it as proof he had nothing to confess.

He was reconciled to the Church and cleared.

Unbeknownst to him — Hassner's words — the friends and family he'd refused to name had been burned the same month he was arrested. He'd been protecting people who were already gone.

## §6 — COUNTER-BALANCE (3:55–4:30)

His friends and family weren't the only ones. The figure most cited about the Inquisition is around three thousand executions over three hundred and fifty years. That's Henry Kamen's estimate. It's probably close.

Kamen also wrote this.

"The apparently low overall death rate masks a very high rate in the first half century of the Inquisition, and a consistently high rate affecting people of Jewish and Muslim origin."

[Kamen, *The Spanish Inquisition: A Historical Revision*, p. 254]

In other words, the Inquisition wasn't operating against the whole population. It was a targeting machine — aimed almost entirely at *conversos*, descendants of Jews and Muslims forced to convert to Christianity. The three thousand executions weren't spread evenly — they concentrated on the people the Spanish state never quite trusted to be Christian.

Most people remember the executions and the torture. They rarely think about what it meant to live under the Inquisition.

A converso writer in Toledo, fifteen thirty-eight:

"Bit by bit, many rich people leave the country for foreign realms, in order not to live all their lives in fear and trembling every time an officer of the Inquisition enters their house — for continual fear is a worse death than a sudden demise."

[Kamen, p. 232]

This is what the system produced — not just three thousand executions, but generational fear. The rules weren't there to protect the accused — they were there to make the Inquisition run efficiently, and harder to escape.

## §7 — CLOSE (4:30–5:00)

Torquemada didn't invent cruelty — he organized it.

He wrote rules that required a doctor in the room while you were being waterboarded. He wrote rules that made your confession invalid unless you repeated it freely the next morning. He wrote rules that said you couldn't be tortured twice for the same evidence.

And in the same paragraph, he wrote the clause that made all of it negotiable.

The defenders of the Inquisition cite those protections. The critics cite the same document — the same procedures that authorized what happened to Marina González, to Díaz de Cáceres. They're both right. They just don't read paragraph fifteen all the way through.
