On April 29th, 1494, in Ciudad Real, Spain, a scribe sat next to
Marina Gonzalez in a torture chamber. Not to help her — to write
everything down. The reason we still have that record is because
Article 15 of a 1484 manual required it.

The Spanish Inquisition is one of the few institutions in pre-modern
history where the torture is documented to the line. Tomás de
Torquemada wrote the *Compilación de las Instrucciones* in 1484. It
specified who had to be in the room (the inquisitor, the bishop's
representative, a notary, and a doctor), how many warnings the
accused had to receive, which three torture methods were permitted,
and what made a confession legally valid.

Paragraph 15 contained the rule: torture once, ratify the next
morning, or the confession doesn't count.

The same paragraph contained a single line — about the inquisitors'
right to "repeat the question of torture" in cases where they
"must and can do so by law" — that produced a procedural workaround
the inquisitors called *suspension*. End the session: limit applies.
Suspend the session: next session is a legal continuation, no limit.

Ron E. Hassner, in *Anatomy of Torture* (Cornell University Press,
2022), reads the surviving trial records — including Marina
Gonzalez's 1494 case in Ciudad Real and Antonio Diaz de Caceres's
1601 case in Mexico City — to show how the rules and the workaround
operated in practice.

This video reads paragraph 15 word for word. It does not argue
whether the Inquisition was good or bad. It shows what the document
actually says.
