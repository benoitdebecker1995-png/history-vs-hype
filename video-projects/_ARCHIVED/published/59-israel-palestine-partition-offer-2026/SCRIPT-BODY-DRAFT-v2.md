## BEAT 2 — ACT 1: THE TWO STORIES  *(NEW DRAFT — the 7a fix; replaces v1's one-sided "story everyone tells")*

By 1947, Britain had ruled Palestine for thirty years. For most of that time, its Arab and Jewish communities had been in conflict — flaring into violence again and again, each round worse than the last. At the root was a fight over immigration, over land, and over who the country belonged to. That much isn't in dispute. What's in dispute is the story of what happened next.

So before we test anything, here's each side's story in full. Both are everywhere, and each one, on its own, sounds completely convincing. Here's the first.

In November 1947, the UN voted to partition British-ruled Palestine into a Jewish state and an Arab one. The Jews said yes — even though they'd wanted all of Palestine. The Arabs said no. And the day after the British pulled out, the armies of the surrounding Arab states marched into the new state of Israel. One side chose compromise, the other chose war — and everything since flows from that one choice.

[SHOW: Pole-1 comment-cards, usernames + avatars blurred — `TWO-EXTREMES-EVIDENCE.md`. e.g. "Arabs were offered their own state. Jews said yes. Arabs said no…"]

Now the same history, from the Arab side. For centuries, the land was overwhelmingly Arab. Then, after the Second World War, a committee of foreign governments handed more than half of it to a mostly European Jewish minority — outsiders drawing borders for the people who'd lived there for generations. In this version, the Arabs didn't turn down a fair deal. They refused to sign away their own country.

[SHOW: Pole-2 comment-cards, blurred — e.g. "when u leave the land to go to Europe and come take it with force, that's just wrong."]

Both are told as settled history. But neither one holds up once you actually read the plan they're fighting over. The Israeli version fails on whether the deal was ever fair; the Arab version, on whether anything was really imposed. So let's start with fair.

## BEAT 3 — ACT 2: THE BASELINE + WHO WAS ACTUALLY THERE  *(🔒 SECTION-LOCKED 2026-06-23 — creator manual rewrite, read-aloud-clean. Plain-pass applied; Karsh conceded-then-answered; Khoury VO cut.)*

Benny Morris — an Israeli historian — sums up the Arab objection plainly. The Arabs, he says, couldn't see why it was fair for them, the majority on their own land, to be turned overnight into a minority in a Jewish state. That's why they refused to accept the plan. They weren't arguing over where the border went; they were refusing to split their country at all.

And the size of that split isn't really disputed. The plan gave the Jewish state about fifty-five percent of the land. Jews were about a third of the population, and owned roughly seven percent of the land.

But "empty" is doing a lot of work there — and it doesn't hold up. To be fair to Karsh, the Negev itself was poor farming land; that part is true. But it wasn't unpopulated. In the Beersheba district Britain's own 1946 census counted about a hundred and three thousand Bedouin Arabs to a thousand Jews. So while it is true that the plan gave the Jewish state bad land, it also gave it the majority of the good farmable land. The plan handed about eighty-four percent of it to the Jewish state, along with most of the citrus, the country's biggest export.

Either way, this was a proposed Jewish state that was barely Jewish — and by the Arab count, not majority-Jewish at all.

## BEAT 4 — ACT 3: THE STATE THAT COULDN'T STAND  *(EDITED — v1 Beat 4 carries; unworkability analysis sharpened, C5/C6)*

That was the first problem with the deal. The second had nothing to do with fairness — it was whether the proposed countries could function as countries.

Neither side got a single, connected country. The Jewish state was three blocks of land scattered down the map; the Arab state was three more, wedged into the gaps between them. To get from one piece of the Jewish state to the next, you had to cross Arab land — and the same the other way around.

That fragmentation created an economic problem the plan couldn't solve. Under British rule, Palestine worked as one economy. It had one rail network, with goods and labour moving across the whole territory. And Jerusalem's water came from inside the proposed Jewish state, then had to cross the Arab state to reach the city. Split into six pieces, none of them could function alone. So the plan tried to keep the economy whole while dividing the country in two: it required both states to share a single currency, a customs union, and a joint board to run the trade between them — and gave them until the first of April, 1948 to agree to all of it, or have it forced on them. Two populations already in conflict were supposed to run a shared central bank and a common customs system together — in a matter of months.

This wasn't only the Arab side's argument. UNSCOP — the UN committee that drew up the partition plan — put the question to an independent economic expert, P.C. Reedman. His verdict: the Arab state "would definitely not be viable without a customs union." The Jewish state had been handed the better land, the ports, the developed areas, so it could survive on its own. The Arab state couldn't — it could only survive if the side it had just been split from agreed to keep it going. What was on offer for the Arab side wasn't really an independent country.

## BEAT 5 — ACT 4: ⭐ IT WASN'T LAW  *(🔒 SECTION-LOCKED 2026-06-23 — THE LYNCHPIN. Restructured to "document imposed nothing → but the 'imposed' claim has a grain of truth in two places: (1) legality never settled [World Court 21–20], (2) the partition vote was leveraged [US pressure, C18, MOVED here from Act 6/7A]." But to impose something, there first has to be something binding — a law, an order, something with real authority behind it. And Resolution 181 was none of those.

So look at the words the resolution uses. It "recommends" partition, and it "requests" the Security Council to carry it out. That wording was deliberate. Under the UN Charter, the General Assembly — the room where every country gets a vote — can't make law. It can only recommend.

Not everyone accepted that. The UN's own secretary-general, and the brand-new Israeli government, argued it WAS binding — that the UN had inherited authority over Palestine from the old League of Nations. But the UN's own lawyers rejected that: the resolution, they concluded, had "no obligatory character whatsoever." The Assembly could recommend a partition. It couldn't impose one, it couldn't hand over land it didn't own, and it couldn't create two states on its own. It could only propose them — and then both sides had to agree. They never did.

So the document imposed nothing. But the claim that it was forced on the Arabs isn't baseless — it holds a grain of truth, in two places.

The first is legality — the question we opened with. Whether the UN even had the right to partition Palestine went to a vote, lost by one, and was never actually settled.

The second is how the vote was won. To get partition passed, the United States leaned on smaller countries to change their votes. Liberia, which lived off its rubber exports, was told the Firestone company might reconsider expanding there — and it switched. France was warned its American aid could be cut, and switched two hours before the count. The Philippines had called partition a breach of the UN Charter, until two US Supreme Court justices personally pressed its president. It voted yes.

The "international birth certificate," as one historian calls it. But read the document itself: it didn't create anything. It only recommended. For all the weight the world puts on it, it never carried the force of law.

So you had a recommendation — not a command. And that's the key to the second claim. If partition was really imposed on the Arabs, then someone had to impose it — and forcing it onto a land where most people rejected it would have taken an army. So did anyone send one?

The UN had a body built for exactly this: the Security Council, the one part of it that could give binding orders and put soldiers on the ground. The resolution asked the Council to act. It never did. And the small commission the UN sent to manage the handover had no force of its own — within weeks, it was warning that the whole thing would collapse into bloodshed the moment Britain left.

**[Act 5 → Act 6 — flow-in / locked lead-in]**

So both claims about the document fall apart. It wasn't a fair deal, and it was never really imposed — because nothing was ever enforced. But that still leaves the thing people actually argue about. Strip away the document, and one fact remains: the Arabs were offered a state, and they said no.

**[the fork]**

So was that "no" a historic blunder — they bet they could reject the plan, fight, and keep the whole country, and lost everything instead? Or was saying yes never really an option — because it meant accepting they'd be a permanent minority in their own homeland?

**[blunder — Morris]**

Benny Morris reads it as a blunder. The Arabs, he argues, were offered a state of their own, backed by the world. They turned it down, gambled on a war to stop partition altogether, and lost — walking away with far less than they'd just refused.

**[principled — Khalidi]**

Rashid Khalidi sees the opposite, and his case rests on what saying yes would have cost. The Arabs were two-thirds of the country on their own land. Partition would have made them a minority inside the Jewish state, with no say over who ruled them, what their children were taught, or — the thing the whole fight was really about — who was allowed to immigrate. And because the Jewish state existed to take in Jewish immigrants, even that minority share would only shrink. For Khalidi, none of this was new: from the start of British rule, the Arab majority — about ninety percent of the people — had been granted only civil and religious rights, never political or national ones, and named in the founding documents not as a people, but as "the non-Jewish communities." To him, partition was the last turn of that screw — a formula, as he puts it, "designed to negate their existence."

**[pushback — on the document]**

But Khalidi writes as an open advocate for the Palestinian side, and that's his reading — not what the document says. The plan didn't erase the Arabs: it gave them a state of their own, on nearly half the land, and required the Jewish state to guarantee its Arab minority equal, non-discriminatory rights, written into the constitution each state had to adopt. What they were being asked to give up wasn't their existence — it was the majority's claim to the whole country.

## BEAT 8 — ACT 7: THE VERDICT / THE GHOST MAP  *(🔒 SECTION-LOCKED 2026-06-23 — creator read-aloud. Mirror verdict + legitimacy-not-border precision (C30) + 55→~78% (C29) + Ghost Map "on paper" payoff.)*

The first — "the Arabs rejected a fair deal" — falls apart on the word *fair*. There was nothing fair about handing more than half the land to a third of the people, in a "Jewish state" that would have started out barely majority-Jewish.

The second claim — "it was colonial theft, imposed on them" — is trickier, because it's half right, and the half that's wrong is the important half. To impose something, somebody has to force it into being — and nobody did. There was no army behind the plan, the Security Council never acted, and Britain — the power on the ground — refused to carry it out and simply left.

But the precise version matters. What the resolution *did* impose was legitimacy. What it did *not* impose was a border — that was drawn later, through conflict instead of a vote. When the war broke out the moment Britain left, the Jewish state grew from the fifty-five percent it had been given to about seventy-eight percent on the ground. And the Arab state in the plan never came into being — its land ended up split between Israel, Jordan, and Egypt.

And that's the strangest part. The 1947 map — the one everybody argues about, in every textbook and every comment section — was voted on, and then the war drew different borders before it ever took hold. And both sides make the same move: they hold up that map, and skip the words around it — the words that made it a recommendation no one would enforce. They're fighting over something that was never the law and never the border — a plan that only ever existed on paper.
