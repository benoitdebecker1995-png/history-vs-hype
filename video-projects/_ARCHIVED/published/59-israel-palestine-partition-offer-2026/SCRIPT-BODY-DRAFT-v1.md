## BEAT 2 — THE STORY EVERYONE TELLS + THE TURN

Here's the version most people know, and it's worth telling straight. In November 1947, the UN voted to split British-ruled Palestine into two states — one Jewish, one Arab. The Jews said yes. The Arabs said no. And the day after the British pulled out, five Arab armies marched into the new state of Israel. So the conclusion looks obvious: one side took a peace deal, the other chose war, and everything since flows from that one choice. A lot of people treat it as settled history.

So I went and actually read the thing it all rests on — the partition plan itself, the committee reports behind it, and the record of the night it was voted on. And once you do that, three big parts of the familiar story stop holding up. Let's take them one at a time.

[SHOW: Exhibit A — UN GA Resolution 181(II), full text + partition map. NLM `e18813a6`.]

## BEAT 3 — ONE: IT WASN'T A FAIR SPLIT

Start with the map itself. The plan gave the Jewish state about fifty-five percent of the land. At that point Jews were roughly a third of the people living there, and they owned about seven percent of the ground. And that's not a Palestinian figure — it's Benny Morris's, one of Israel's own historians. So the very first thing the document does is hand more than half the country to the smaller group, on land it didn't own.

[SOURCE: 55% + ~7% owned = Morris's OWN voice, *Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem Revisited* (2004) p.141, NLM `3ad51d87`. ⚠️ do NOT cite Morris *1948* p.63 (attribution inversion). ~one-third Jewish population = multi-source (Pappé p.17, Said p.79, Khalidi p.106). SHOW: map shaded 55/45 + a "~7% Jewish-owned" overlay.]

There's a second number, and it's a bigger one. When UNSCOP counted heads, it left out about ninety thousand Bedouin in the south, on the reasoning that they were nomads and shouldn't count as settled residents of any state. The Arab side's sub-committee disagreed. Using Britain's own 1946 census, they put the Bedouin closer to a hundred and twenty-seven thousand, with around a hundred and five thousand of them living inside the proposed Jewish state. Add those people back in, and the count flips: roughly five hundred and ten thousand Arabs to four hundred and ninety-nine thousand Jews — an Arab majority inside the "Jewish state," on day one. Now, whether people who move with the seasons should count as residents of a fixed state is a fair thing to argue about — and that argument is exactly the point. On the Arab side's count, the Jewish state didn't even begin with a Jewish majority.

[SOURCE: C2 — Sub-Cttee 2 report (A/AC.14/32), NLM `2ece8af6`. ⚠️ OCR-garbled — pull on-screen verbatim from a clean A/AC.14/32 PDF. LABEL on screen as Sub-Cttee 2's contested correction, NOT "the truth" (auditor's edge).]

And if that "fair deal" framing sounds familiar, it's because it's the same line you'll see under any video about this conflict today — they were offered a state and said no. The difference is, now you've actually seen what that offer looked like on the page.

---

## BEAT 4 — TWO: IT WASN'T A WORKABLE STATE

So that's the first problem — the split wasn't fair. The second problem is the map itself.

Neither side got a single, connected country. The Jewish state was three separate blocks of land scattered down the map — and the Arab state was three separate blocks too, slotted into the gaps between them. The two were interlocked: to get from one piece of the Jewish state to the next, you'd have to cross through Arab land and back again, and the same was true in reverse.

And that geography created an economic problem. Palestine ran as a single economy — one rail network, one set of ports, goods and labour moving across the whole territory. Split into six disconnected pieces, no single piece had everything it needed to support itself. That's why the plan tried to keep the economy whole even as it divided the country: it required both states to share one currency, one customs union, and a joint board to run the trade between them.

And the UN's own committee was blunt about why this mattered. The Jewish state had been given the better land — the coast, the citrus groves, the developed areas — so on its own it might just survive. The Arab state, they wrote, was "so lacking in resources as to be definitely not viable" without that union. In plain terms: the Arabs were being offered a state that couldn't support itself. It could only survive if the side it had just been split from agreed to keep it afloat.

[SOURCE: geometry/economic union = Res 181 Part I §D, NLM `e18813a6` (C4, C5). "definitely not viable" + "best parts" = Sub-Cttee 2, NLM `2ece8af6` (C6). ⚠️ OCR — re-verify verbatim. SHOW: trace the six blocks + the two "kissing points."]

## BEAT 5 — THREE: NOBODY COULD MAKE IT HAPPEN  *(THE SPINE)*

Fairness and viability are almost beside the point next to the third problem — because the plan had no one behind it with the power and the will to carry it out.

The General Assembly that passed it couldn't enforce anything — it could only recommend. The one body that could enforce, the Security Council, was asked to step in and never did. The commission the UN set up to run the handover had no army of its own; within two months it was writing to the Security Council that it expected security and basic services to collapse the moment Britain left.

[SOURCE: GA recommends / SC enforcement punt = Res 181 Part I §B, Arts 39/41, NLM `e18813a6` (C7). Commission's predicted collapse = First Monthly Progress Report S/663, 29 Jan 1948, NLM `bd1325df` (C9).]

So why did Britain just hand the problem back and leave, instead of staying to enforce the plan it had set in motion? Here the historians split again. One camp — William Roger Louis, Michael Cohen — says it was simple exhaustion. Britain had come out of the war effectively bankrupt; that winter it was rationing coal at home; and it had no intention of spending soldiers' lives to force through a partition the Arab side completely rejected. "We are not going to use British bayonets" for this, Bevin said — so Britain announced it would leave, and leave so cleanly that its own high commissioner admitted there was "nobody to whom to hand over." But there's a second reading, and it doesn't so much contradict the first as sit underneath it. The historian James Barr argues Britain wasn't only walking away — it was "leaving through the door and returning through the window." Even as it gave up the Mandate, London was quietly backing King Abdullah of Transjordan, whose army was trained and led by British officers, to move into the Arab parts of Palestine the moment the British flag came down. And Barr has Bevin all but saying so, to Abdullah's prime minister in February 1948: moving into the Arab areas "seems the obvious thing to do" — just "do not go and invade the areas allotted to the Jews." Whichever reading you take, Britain made sure of one thing: it would not be the one holding Palestine together when the Mandate ended.

[SOURCE: FORK 3. Abdication = Louis `24e4886a`, Cohen. Tilt + Bevin–Tawfiq quote = Barr, *Line in the Sand*, NLM `edbfba54`. Cadogan "cannot relinquish it piecemeal" (C8) = neutral floor, NLM `bd1325df`. SHOW card: the Bevin–Tawfiq "do not invade the areas allotted to the Jews" line.]

And the clearest proof comes from the plan's biggest champion. Eleven days before the vote, the US State Department wrote itself a private memo predicting exactly what would happen: partition passes, fighting breaks out, and it lands at the Security Council, with someone demanding troops to enforce it. The memo admitted that America, having pushed for partition, would be "morally bound" to go along with that. And then it decided the opposite. Enforcing the plan, it concluded, would wreck America's standing in the Arab world and hand the Soviets a way into the Middle East. So the plan's loudest champion had already decided, before anyone even voted, that it would never enforce the thing it was voting for.

[SOURCE: FRUS 1947 vol. V, Memorandum for the Secretary of State, 18 Nov 1947 (Doc 872), NLM `6911ab48` (C20). PRIMARY. SHOW card: "The US should seek to discourage the use of force for implementation…"]

So there it is. The General Assembly could recommend but not compel. The one body that could compel — the Security Council — chose not to. And the two powers actually on the ground, Britain and the United States, had each quietly decided to stay out. The plan everyone calls "the offer" was a recommendation with nobody behind it.

## BEAT 6 — FOUR: IT ALMOST NEVER GOT THE CHANCE  *(THE LEGAL GEM)*

And there's one more thing, underneath all of that. It wasn't only that nobody would enforce the plan — it's that the plan may never have been legal in the first place.

It's worth being exact about what the resolution actually was, because the UN didn't pass a law. Read it, and the key word is right there: it "recommends" partition, and "requests" the Security Council to carry it out. That distinction is deliberate. The General Assembly — the room where every country gets a vote — can't bind anyone; it can only recommend. As the jurist Hans Kelsen put it, a General Assembly resolution has "no obligatory character whatsoever." Not everyone agreed — the UN's own secretary-general and the new Israeli government argued it *was* binding, that the UN had inherited authority over Palestine from the old League of Nations. But on its face, the document called itself a recommendation. And a recommendation is a very different thing from a border.

[SOURCE: C11. Text = NLM `e18813a6`. Kelsen / "no obligatory character" = Quigley `f141c666`, Kattan `94a0ed73`. CONTESTED legal-status fork (Trygve Lie / Israel) = label both poles.]

Then, five days before the partition vote, the Arab states tried something different. Their case wasn't just that the plan was unfair — it was that the UN had no legal right to make it. You can't, they argued, take a country away from the majority of the people living in it and hand it to a minority; that's beyond what the UN's own Charter allows. So Egypt and Syria moved to put the question to the International Court of Justice — let the judges at The Hague rule on whether partition was even legal before anyone voted on the map. It came down to one question: was the UN competent to enforce a partition against the wishes of the people who lived there? It lost by a single vote, twenty-one to twenty. And it was that close partly by chance — the Soviet bloc, which actually *backed* partition, abstained, because on principle it didn't like sending things to the Court at all. Five days later the map passed, and the question of whether any of it was legal was never answered.

[SOURCE: C12. Kattan citing the Ad Hoc Cttee record, NLM `94a0ed73` pp.150–151; corrob. Cohen p.289. Chyron: "Referral to World Court — defeated by one vote, 24 Nov 1947."]

## BEAT 7 — THE NIGHT OF THE VOTE

### 7A — how the majority was assembled  *(most cuttable for runtime)*

And there's a reason that two-thirds majority was so hard to get: in the final days, the United States leaned on smaller countries to flip their votes — and we can now name the cases, from the historian Michael Cohen. Liberia, which lived on its rubber exports, was told the Firestone company might rethink its plantations there if it didn't change its vote. It changed. France was warned its American aid could be cut, and switched two hours before the count. The Philippines had called partition a breach of the UN Charter — until two US Supreme Court justices wired its president directly. It voted yes. It didn't always work — Greece got the same treatment too late and stayed a no. There were also rumors of cash changing hands, but those stay rumors — the one delegate who alleged it could never show where the money went.

[SOURCE: C18. Cohen `d7ba048e` (grounded): Liberia/Firestone p.297, France/Baruch p.298, Philippines justices pp.296–297, Greece p.297. ⛔ NOT China (ungrounded — dropped). Bribery = Belt's allegation; Morris: documentation "hard to find." SHOW card: "…isolate millions and millions of American friends and supporters if they continued in their effort to vote against partition." — Justices Murphy & Frankfurter; Cohen p.297.]

### 7B — what was actually said

So they voted, and the plan passed: thirty-three in favour, thirteen against, ten abstaining. But listen to what the thirteen actually said, because it wasn't simply "no to a Jewish state." Iraq's delegate called the decision "illegal" and "contrary to the Charter." Lebanon's delegate told the hall the UN's job was to find solutions that "command universal acceptance" — not ones that can be "applied only by force."

Some of it was theatrical — Syria's delegate told the hall, "the Charter is dead… it was murdered, and you all know who is guilty." But everyone in that room knew who he meant. That two-thirds majority hadn't come together on its own — it had been pressured into line, country by country. The accusation was dramatic; what it pointed at was real.

And the Arabs didn't only object — there was a real alternative on the table. That same night, Lebanon proposed a single federal Palestine: one country, but with self-governing Arab and Jewish cantons inside it, a constitution modelled on America's, and protection for the holy places. Iran asked the assembly to hold off and study it. But partition had been entered first in the order of business, so the chairman put partition to the vote — and once it passed, the federal plan was simply never taken up. Whether it could have worked, nobody knows. It never got the chance.

[SOURCE: C15 (result), C16 (floor quotes — Iraq, Lebanon/Chamoun, Syria/Arslan), C17 (Lebanon federal plan + Iran's adjournment motion). All A/PV.128, NLM `013c47e2`. Syria's "guilty" anchored to the US vote-pressure established in 7A = C18 (Cohen `d7ba048e`), not re-litigated here.]

### 7C — principled or intransigent? (THE SHOWCASE FORK)

So was that "no" a refusal of peace, or something else? Put yourself in the shoes of an Arab farmer in the Galilee in 1947. Your community is about two-thirds of the country — the majority. Then the plan tells you the land you farm now sits inside the Jewish state, where you'll be a permanent minority, about a third of the population. And being the minority here isn't a small thing. It means you no longer have a say in who governs, what language your kids are taught in, or — the heart of the whole conflict — who's allowed to immigrate. And because the whole purpose of the new state was to take in Jewish immigrants, that one-third share would only shrink over time. You didn't move; the border moved. And saying yes means agreeing to all of that — signing away the majority you already had.

So was the rejection a tragic blunder or a principled stand? This is where serious historians genuinely split — and both sides are reasoning from the same facts. Take Benny Morris first — again, an Israeli historian, not a Palestinian one. He calls it a flat no: the Arab leadership refused to accept a Jewish state in any part of Palestine, held out for all of it, and ended up with nothing. In his words, they "plucked defeat from the jaws of victory" — they could have had a state on most of the land, and walked away from the table instead. Now take Rashid Khalidi and Edward Said. Their case is that the no wasn't blind rejectionism — it was a refusal to ratify something that had already been done to them. Under British rule, the Arab majority — around ninety percent of the population when the Mandate began — had been written into the legal documents not as a nation with political rights, but as "non-Jewish communities." Saying yes to partition meant formally signing off on that: giving up more than half the country and accepting minority status on land they'd lived on for centuries. And the two historians don't actually disagree about what happened. They agree the split was lopsided; they agree the Arabs said no. They disagree on one thing only — whether that no was a costly miscalculation or the only self-respecting answer on offer.

[SOURCE: FORK 2 (showcase). Morris `234bb4fe` ("flat no," "plucked defeat from the jaws of victory"). Khalidi `4d114b2e` ("iron cage," "non-Jewish communities," ~90% majority). Said `d8189d95`. Demographics inside zones = C1. Present BOTH poles, attribute by name, DO NOT resolve.]

## BEAT 8 — THE VERDICT  *(mirror test + "on paper" payoff)*

So let's put the two claims side by side and see what's left of each. The first — "the Arabs rejected a fair deal" — falls apart on the word *fair*. There was nothing fair about handing more than half the land to a third of the people, drawing a "Jewish state" that was barely majority-Jewish, and then refusing even to vote on the one compromise the other side put forward.

The second claim — "it was colonial theft, imposed on them" — falls apart on the word *imposed*. To impose something, somebody has to actually force it into being. And nobody ever did. Passing the resolution was not the same as making it happen — and no one made it happen. There was no UN army to enforce the partition. Britain, the power actually running the country, refused to carry it out and simply left. The Security Council, the one body that could have forced it through, declined to act. And within four months the UN had quietly walked away from its own plan. So nothing was imposed on anyone — the map was passed, and then abandoned. The line that exists on the ground today wasn't drawn by this map at all; it was drawn by the war that broke out the moment Britain left.

And that's what both sides miss. They argue about the offer as if it were a real, solid thing that got accepted or refused. It never was. It was a recommendation nobody would enforce — a plan that only ever existed on paper.

[SOURCE: C22 mirror verdict. "imposed" breakdown = C7/C8/C20/C11 + Quigley (UN abandoned within four months). The 1948 war drew the real border.]
