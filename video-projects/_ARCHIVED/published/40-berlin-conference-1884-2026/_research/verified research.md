African Diplomacy and the Realities of the Colonial Partition
Based on Thomas Pakenham’s The Scramble for Africa, H.L. Wesseling’s Divide and Rule, and other scholarly sources in the collection, here are the answers to your questions:
1. Colonial Borders Following Existing Logic
While the majority of colonial boundaries were arbitrary, there were specific instances where the 1884–1914 partition followed pre-existing political, ethnic, or geographic logic:

    Pre-existing Political Units: A "handful of instances" saw pre-colonial state units persist within the territorial frame of the colonial order [1]. Notable examples include Botswana (Bechuanaland), Swaziland, Lesotho (Basutoland), Rwanda, Burundi, and Zanzibar [1-3]. In these cases, the colonial borders largely coincided with the historical territorial frames of the kingdoms they encompassed [1, 4].
    Geographic and Natural Frontiers: In West Africa, the River Volta was used as a line of demarcation between British and German spheres of influence in 1887 [5]. Similarly, the River Congo and its tributaries often served as the defined boundaries for the Congo Free State's agreements with France and Portugal [6, 7].
    Strategic Sovereignty: In the case of Ethiopia, the borders established after the Battle of Adwa (1896) were the result of an African state acting as a "partitioner" rather than a victim, successfully negotiating boundaries that recognized its expanded imperial reach [2, 8].

2. African Rulers Successfully Negotiating Terms
Several African rulers utilized diplomacy and "protection" treaties to secure the survival of their people or their own political authority:

    King Khama III (Ngwato/Bechuanaland): Perhaps the most successful, Khama used missionaries as intermediaries to secure a British protectorate in 1885 [9-11]. This move successfully rescued his people from being subjugated by the Boers or the British South Africa Company, ensuring that northern Bechuanaland remained a native sanctuary [11].
    Sir Apolo Kaggwa (Buganda): A "tireless moderniser," Kaggwa led the Protestant party into a profitable alliance with the British and negotiated the 1900 Uganda Agreement [12]. This treaty ensured Buganda maintained significant autonomy, preserved its monarchy, and allowed Christian leaders to distribute land among themselves as freehold property [13].
    King Lewanika (Lozi/Barotseland): Facing instability and external threats from the Ndebele, Lewanika successfully negotiated for a British protectorate in 1888 to protect his throne and territory [14, 15].
    Emperor Menelik II (Ethiopia): Through a combination of military victory at Adowa and adroit diplomacy, Menelik forced European powers to recognize Ethiopia's sovereign independence and negotiated definitive border treaties with Italy, France, and Britain [2, 8, 16].

3. Steelmanning the Counter-Position: Overstated Negative Effects
The strongest scholarly argument that the Berlin Conference’s negative effects have been overstated focuses on the conference as a diplomatic stabilization mechanism rather than a primary cause of colonization:

    The Conference as a "Safety Valve": Wesseling and other historians argue the conference was not intended to "carve up" Africa but to provide a "safety valve" to prevent a "stampede" that could lead to war between European powers [17, 18]. It successfully averted a general European war over African claims, which were considered relatively trivial commercial options at the time [19, 20].
    Limited Practical Significance: Contrary to popular belief, the conference did not initiate the partition [21]. Most territorial acquisitions were decided through bilateral agreements negotiated outside the conference [21]. The doctrine of "effective occupation" was restricted only to the coasts, where very little territory remained to be claimed, and had "little bearing on the partition of Africa inland" [21-23].
    The "Loaded Pause": Rather than accelerating conquest, the conference was intended as a "loaded pause" to forestall a scramble and put off the "evil day" of administrative liability [24, 25]. The colonial powers were actually reluctant to assume the high costs of governing the interior and preferred to resign the bulk of claims to chartered companies or leave them as paper "spheres of influence" [26, 27].
    Ground Reality vs. Paper Maps: Scholars point out that the maps drawn in Berlin did not reflect reality; they were "pre-emptive cartography" [28]. The real meaning of the partition was determined later on the ground by African resistance and local political dynamics, which eventually forced the "paper partition" to become colonial monopoly [25]. Wesseling summarizes: "In Europe conquests preceded the drawing of maps; in Africa the map was drawn, and then it was decided what was going to happen" [29].

Sovereignty and Statecraft in Pre-Colonial Africa
Modern scholarship fundamentally refutes the 19th-century European depiction of Africa as a "stateless" or "primitive" continent. Instead, academic sources document a sophisticated and diverse political landscape consisting of centralized empires, hereditary kingdoms, and complex confederacies that were actively evolving at the time of the Berlin Conference.
1. Major African Political Entities Affected by Partition
On the eve of the 1884–1885 Berlin Conference, an "overwhelming majority" of African polities were enjoying sovereign existence under recognized rulers [1]. These entities existed across all regions of the continent:

    West Africa (Savannah and Sahel): The great medieval empires of Ghana, Mali, and Songhay had previously established a legacy of large-scale statecraft [2, 3]. By 1880, these were succeeded by even larger centralized states, including the Sokoto Caliphate (the largest state in West Africa), the Tukulor Empire, and the empire of Samori Ture [4, 5].
    West Africa (Forest and Coast): Highly developed states such as the Kingdom of Benin (famed for its sophisticated bronze art and 10,000 kilometers of earth boundaries) and the Asante Empire were prominent [2, 6]. On the coast, "mercantilist states" arose in the Niger Delta, and constitutional experiments like the Fante Confederation and the Egba United Board of Management were active [7-9].
    Central and Southern Africa: The Kingdom of Kongo had long functioned as a sophisticated state [10, 11]. To the east and south, the Luba and Lunda Empires exerted suzerainty over vast territories, while Great Zimbabwe stood as evidence of a powerful earlier kingdom with global trade links [2, 12]. The Zulu Kingdom and Sotho states represented significant 19th-century political consolidations [2, 13].
    East Africa and the Horn: Centralized kingdoms flourished in the Great Lakes region, most notably Buganda and Bunyoro [4, 14]. Ethiopia existed as a single centralized state that had doubled its empire size during the Scramble era, and the Imerina Kingdom had unified Madagascar [15-17].

2. Deconstructing the "Stateless" and "Tribal" Claims
Contemporary Europeans justified the partition by categorizing Africa as a "land of childhood" or a "savage" void [18, 19]. Legally, they claimed that "tribal organization" did not constitute a "State proper" under international law, allowing territory to be treated as terra nullius (nobody's land) [20, 21].
However, modern academic sources provide a sharp corrective:

    Active Revolution: By 1880, Africa was "far from being primitive, static, and asleep"; it was in a "mood of change and revolution," experiencing internal dynamics of modernization and political centralization [22].
    Legal Reality: Major communities were politically organized, governed by sovereigns, and operated under centuries-old legal systems and cultural traditions [23]. Even in regions termed "stateless" (such as Igboland), society achieved "social complexity without state formation," utilizing decentralized village self-government that was often more democratic than the European models imposed later [24, 25].
    Misleading Terminology: Scholars note that the word "tribe" was often a colonial administrative invention used to "containerize" Africans into manageable minorities, rather than a reflection of pre-colonial reality [26, 27].

3. Modern Scholarship on Pre-Colonial Political Organization
Modern historians and political scientists (such as John Iliffe, Crawford Young, and Jeffrey Herbst) identify several key features of pre-colonial African political organization:

    Diverse State Forms: Africa possessed a "rich endowment of state forms," including quasi-feudal monarchies, divine kingships, Islamic caliphates, and military-conquest states [28].
    The Power Gradient: Unlike the European model of "hard" territorial borders, African power was often conceived as concentric circles radiating from a core [29, 30]. Authority was absolute at the center (the capital) and became progressively attenuated or "symbolic" at the periphery, allowing for "shared sovereignty" and fluid frontiers [31, 32].
    Unbundling Land and People: Many African theories of sovereignty distinguished between the "guardians of the land" and the "guardians of the people," allowing for nuanced political arrangements that did not require the zero-sum territorial control typical of the European nation-state [33, 34].
    Centralization Trend: By the late 19th century, there was a clear trend toward greater centralization [35]. African rulers were making "brutal power calculations" and expanding their reach through improved military technologies and administrative innovations, a process only interrupted by the "suddenness and unpredictability" of European conquest [1, 36].

The Magnificent African Cake: King Leopold’s Double Life
According to Adam Hochschild’s King Leopold’s Ghost, the following quotes illustrate the vast discrepancy between Leopold II's public rhetoric and his private colonial ambitions:
1. Public Mission and Humanitarian Statements
Leopold’s most explicit humanitarian statement was delivered during his welcoming speech at the Geographical Conference in Brussels on September 12, 1876. He framed his interest in Africa as a selfless, civilizing mission.

    Public Quote (1876): "To open to civilization the only part of our globe which it has not yet penetrated, to pierce the darkness which hangs over entire peoples, is, I dare say, a crusade worthy of this century of progress. . . . Need I say that in bringing you to Brussels I was guided by no egotism? No, gentlemen, Belgium may be a small country, but she is happy and satisfied with her fate; I have no other ambition than to serve her well." [1]
    Public Quote (1890): In an interview with George Washington Williams, Leopold asserted: "What I do there is done as a Christian duty to the poor African; and I do not wish to have one franc back of all the money I have expended." [2]

2. Private Correspondence and Actual Aims
In private letters and instructions to his agents, Leopold was blunt about his desire for personal wealth, territory, and absolute control.

    Private Quote (November 17, 1877): Writing to Baron Solvyns, his minister in London, regarding the explorer Henry Morton Stanley, Leopold revealed his true motivation: "I do not want to risk . . . losing a fine chance to secure for ourselves a slice of this magnificent African cake." [3, 4]
    Private Quote (early career): Leopold complained to an adviser about the Belgian public's lack of interest in colonization: "Belgium doesn’t exploit the world. It’s a taste we have got to make her learn." [5]
    Private Quote (October 16, 1882): Leopold’s instructions to Colonel Strauch regarding the treaties Stanley was to make with African chiefs emphasized total acquisition: "The treaties must be as brief as possible, and in a couple of articles must grant us everything." [6, 7]

3. The Most Striking Contrast
The most striking contrast exists between his 1876 claim of absolute selflessness and his 1877 private admission of greed, as well as his 1908 defiance as he attempted to hide the evidence of his rule.

    Public (1876): "I was guided by no egotism... I have no other ambition than to serve [Belgium] well." [1]
    Private (1877): He was obsessed with securing a "slice of this magnificent African cake." [3]

A second profound contrast is found in his public portrayal as a "trustee" of civilization versus his final private words to his military aide, Gustave Stinglhamber, in August 1908, as he ordered the state archives to be burned:

    Private (1908): "I will give them my Congo, but they have no right to know what I did there." [8]

The Bakassi Peninsula Dispute: Legal Origins and Sovereign Resolution
Based on the provided documents regarding the Bakassi Peninsula and the international legal proceedings surrounding it, here is the information requested:
1. The 1884-Era Agreement and the Disputed Boundary
The disputed boundary's origin stems from conflicting interpretations of colonial-era treaties. Nigeria’s claim to the Bakassi Peninsula was based on an 1884 agreement (a treaty of protection) that the British had made with local chiefs [1]. Nigeria argued that this 1884 agreement established the official border [1]. In contrast, Cameroon’s claim was based on the Anglo-German border agreements of 1913 [1, 2].
2. The ICJ Case (Nigeria v. Cameroon, 2002)
In its judgment delivered on October 10, 2002, the International Court of Justice (ICJ) ruled in favor of Cameroon regarding sovereignty over the Bakassi Peninsula [2, 3].

    Treaty Basis: The Court determined that the boundary in the Bakassi area was definitively delimited by the Anglo-German Agreement of 11 March 1913, specifically citing Articles XVIII to XX [3].
    Rejection of Nigeria’s Basis: By confirming the 1913 treaty as the international border, the Court effectively rejected Nigeria's argument that the 1884 treaties with local chiefs established a historical title that superseded the 1913 agreement [2, 3].
    Mandate: The Court requested that Nigeria expeditiously and without condition withdraw its administration, military, and police forces from the peninsula [4].

3. Timeline for Final Resolution and Transfer
The resolution and transfer of the territory followed a multi-decade timeline of legal and diplomatic actions:

    March 29, 1994: Cameroon formally filed proceedings against Nigeria at the ICJ to resolve the sovereignty dispute and avoid a full-scale war [2, 5].
    October 10, 2002: The ICJ issued its final judgment awarding the Bakassi Peninsula to Cameroon [2, 3].
    June 12, 2006: Nigeria and Cameroon signed the Greentree Agreement under United Nations pressure. This agreement established a framework for the transfer, allowing Nigeria to maintain a civil administration in Bakassi for a transitional period [6, 7].
    August 14, 2008: The Nigerian Army completed its full withdrawal from the peninsula, and the territory transitioned to Cameroonian administrative control [6, 8].
    August 14, 2009: Cameroon assumed complete control of Bakassi following the official withdrawal period [8].
    August 15, 2013: Cameroon gained full sovereignty over the peninsula. This date marked the end of a five-year special transitional regime during which the local population had been tax-exempt [9].

Colonial Foundations of Modern African Conflict
Based on the provided academic sources and reports, here are the historical and colonial-era traces for the Sudan Darfur conflict, the DRC eastern instability, and the Ogaden War:
1. Sudan Darfur Conflict: Colonial Ethnic Categorization
Academic sources trace the ethnic divisions in Darfur to the British colonial administrative and census practices of the 1920s. While Darfur was only conquered by the British during the First World War in 1916, it was soon subjected to the logic of "indirect rule" [1, 2].

    The 1929 Population Census: A defining colonial decision was the #929 census frame, designed by administrator Harold MacMichael, which aimed to gather "tribal information" to categorize the population [3].
    Race vs. Culture: In Darfur, the census preferenced race over culture or language. Even though the majority of Darfuris spoke Arabic at home (54.6%), the census recorded only 28.2% as "Arabs" [4]. The remaining majority was classified as "Other Negroid 'Westerners'"—effectively defining them as "locals" while categorizing Arabic-speakers as a "settler race" not tied to a specific locality [4, 5].
    Legacy of Marginalization: This colonial "history of neglect of the peripheries" by the central government continued through independence, eventually leading to the racialization of the 2003 conflict where groups were categorized as "African" vs. "Arab" despite sharing Islamic piety and the Arabic language [6-8].

Sources:

    Mamdani, Neither Settler nor Native, pp. 215–216 [4, 5].
    Young, The Postcolonial State in Africa, p. 275 [7, 8].

2. DRC Eastern Instability: Administrative Integration and Kivu
The presence of populations of Rwandan origin (including the Banyamulenge) and the resulting instability in eastern Congo (Kivu) is academically traced to specific Belgian colonial administrative decisions.

    Administrative Integration: During the colonial period, the Ruanda-Urundi trust territory was administratively integrated with the Belgian Congo [9]. This integration led some Belgians and early nationalists to assume the territories would remain united, facilitating the movement of people across what was then a porous administrative line [9].
    Expansion Area: Historically, the Kivu province had long been an expansion area for Rwanda's "surplus population" [10].
    Performance of the Nation: Today, this presence is a source of intense friction; academics note that "performing the nation" in the DRC often involves a "marked antagonism to the national 'other' beyond the frontier, Rwanda," specifically targeting populations of Rwandan origin within Kivu [11].

Sources:

    Young, The Postcolonial State in Africa, p. 387 (Note 11) [9] and p. 191 [11].
    Iliffe, Africans: The History of a Continent, p. 308 [10].

3. The Ogaden War (1977-78): The 1897 Boundary
The Ogaden War was a direct result of contesting the colonial-era boundary established in 1897 between Great Britain and Ethiopia.

    The 1897 Treaty: This boundary delimitation occurred during the "Scramble for Africa" when Ethiopia, under Emperor Menelik II, secured the Ogaden region through military victory and adroit diplomacy against European powers [12, 13].
    Contested Rights: The conflict was exacerbated by Ethiopia's unilateral suspension of grazing rights that had been a core part of the original 1897 boundary agreement with Britain [14].
    Pan-Somali Irredentism: When Somalia became independent in 1960, it did not accept the colonial borders [15]. The war was driven by "pan-Somali irredentism"—the desire to recover the Ogaden, which is almost exclusively occupied by ethnic Somalis but remains part of Ethiopia due to the colonial partition [13, 16].

Sources:

    Michalopoulos and Papaioannou, American Economic Review, p. 1809 [13].
    Craven, The Decolonization of International Law, p. 183 [14].

The Casement Report: Atrocities in the Congo Free State
Based on the provided excerpts from the Casement Report (1904) and supporting historical accounts in the sources, here are the details regarding Roger Casement’s investigation into the Congo Free State:
1. Documentation of Mutilation and Forced Labor
Casement documented numerous specific instances of mutilation and forced labor, often involving the use of children as victims to intimidate adults.

    Witnessing Mutilation: While at Lake Mantumba, Casement personally documented two victims: "One, a young man, both of whose hands had been beaten off with the butt-ends of rifles against a tree, the other a young lad of eleven or twelve years of age, whose right hand was cut off at the wrist" [1, 2]. He later encountered another boy, approximately 15 years old, named II, whose "left hand had been hacked off by the wrist" and who accused a sentry named KK to his face [3-6].
    Forced Labor and Hostages: Casement documented the "maison des otages" (house of hostages) system, where women and children were imprisoned to compel men to work. In one instance, he witnessed "sixteen natives, five men tied neck to neck, with five untied women and six young children" being led to a prison shed [7, 8]. In another, he found "fifteen women, three pregnant and five of them with babies," tied neck to neck and ankle to ankle at night [9-12].

2. Statistics on Population Decline
Casement provided stark comparisons between the population centers he had known in 1887 and what he found during his 1903 investigation:

    Lukolela: Casement noted that this district had "numbered fully 5,000 people" in 1887; by 1903, a careful enumeration found it had fallen to "less than 600" [13-15].
    Stanley Pool: The southern shores formerly had a population of "fully 5,000 Batekes" across three towns; by 1903, he found only scattered European houses and estimated fewer than "100 of the original natives or their descendants" remained [16-18].
    Lake Mantumba: He estimated that the population of the lake-side towns had diminished within the last ten years by "60 or 70 per cent" [19-21].
    Bolobo: A mission station that once had 40,000 inhabitants was reduced to just 1,000 [22].

3. The Relationship Between Rubber Quotas and Atrocities
Casement described a direct, systemic link between the state’s demand for rubber and the atrocities committed by its agents and soldiers.

    The "Cartridge Rule": Casement documented that soldiers were issued cartridges and were required to return a severed right hand for every one used to prove they had not "wasted" ammunition on animals or missed shots [23-25]. One official estimated that in six months on the Momboyo River, 6,000 cartridges had been used, implying 6,000 people killed or mutilated [23, 25].
    Enforcement by Terror: Refugees told Casement they fled because "nothing had remained for them at home but to be killed for failure to bring in a certain amount of rubber or to die from starvation or exposure in their attempts to satisfy the demands made upon them" [26-28].
    Punitive Expeditions: When quotas were not met, the white men sent soldiers to "punish us for not bringing in enough rubber" [28, 29]. This included shooting people in lines to save ammunition—"shoot through all our bodies" with one cartridge [30-32].

Compelling Passage
The following passage summarizes Casement's conclusion that these atrocities were not "native customs" but a deliberate tool of European colonial administration:
> "Of acts of persistent mutilation by Government soldiers of this nature I had many statements made to me... Of the fact of this mutilation and the causes inducing it there can be no shadow of doubt. It was not a native custom prior to the coming of the white man; it was not the outcome of the primitive instincts of savages in their fights between village and village; it was the deliberate act of the soldiers of a European administration, and these men themselves never made any concealment that in committing these acts they were but obeying the positive orders of their superiors." [33-35]

Blood and Rubber: The Leopoldian Terror in the Congo
Based on Adam Hochschild’s King Leopold’s Ghost, the Casement Report, and other supporting sources, here are the answers to your questions regarding the rubber regime in the Congo Free State:
1. Rubber Quota Requirements
Rubber quotas were imposed on villages with extreme severity and varied by region and the specific concession company in control.

    Fortnightly Quotas: In the A.B.I.R. concession territory, the standard quota assigned to each village was "three to four kilos of dried rubber per adult male per fortnight" [1].
    Labor Time: Meeting these quotas essentially required "full-time labor" [1]. In the Mongala River basin, gatherers were often forced to spend "twenty-four days a month in the forest" searching for rubber vines, where they were exposed to starvation and wild animals [1].
    Scale of Imposition: One specific village of 100 people was reportedly ordered to produce "1000 kilos monthly" [2].

2. Consequences for Villages Failing to Meet Quotas
Villages that fell short of their assigned quotas faced systematic terror, including hostage-taking, flogging, mutilation, and mass murder [3, 4]. To enforce compliance, state and company agents frequently seized women, children, or chiefs as hostages to be held in stockades—where many died of starvation—until the men of the village brought in the required amount of rubber [5-7].
**Direct Quote from the Casement Report:**Roger Casement recorded the testimony of refugees who had fled the rubber zones, concluding that they had:
> "endured such ill-treatment at the hands of the Government officials and the Government soldiers in their own country that life had become intolerable, that nothing had remained for them at home but to be killed for failure to bring in a certain amount of rubber or to die from starvation or exposure in their attempts to satisfy the demands made upon them." [8, 9]
3. Credible Scholarly Estimate for Deaths (1885–1908)
Estimating the exact death toll is difficult due to the lack of formal census data; however, historians use a combination of murder counts, starvation rates, disease impact, and falling birth rates to arrive at a consensus [4].

    Percentage Estimate: The most authoritative judgment, provided by Jan Vansina (professor emeritus at the University of Wisconsin), states that between 1880 and 1920, the population of the Congo was cut "by at least a half" [10]. This figure aligns with an official Belgian government commission estimate from 1919 [10].
    Absolute Number: Based on a later 1924 reckoning of ten million inhabitants, this 50 percent loss suggests that during the Leopoldian era and its immediate aftermath, the population dropped by "approximately ten million people" [11].
    Higher Estimates: Other scholars, such as Congolese historian Isidore Ndaywel è Nziem, have placed the estimate as high as thirteen million deaths [12, 13].

4. Destruction of Archives in 1908
In August 1908, shortly before the formal handover of the Congo to the Belgian government, Leopold II ordered the systematic destruction of the state's financial and administrative records [14, 15]. He did this "to cover his tracks" and prevent his successors or the public from learning the full extent of his activities and the riches he had siphoned from the territory [14-16]. The furnaces in Brussels were kept burning for eight days to reduce the archives to ash [15].
**Direct Quote from Leopold II:**Leopold expressed his defiance regarding the handover to his young military aide, Gustave Stinglhamber:
> "I will give them my Congo, but they have no right to know what I did there." [15]

Leopold II and the Personal Acquisition of the Congo
Based on Adam Hochschild’s King Leopold’s Ghost and the General Act of the Berlin Conference, here are the details regarding Leopold II’s acquisition of the Congo:
1. Personal Acquisition and Legal Mechanism
Leopold II acquired the Congo Free State not through a single grant by the Berlin Conference, but through a "web of bilateral agreements" negotiated with each of the participating powers during and after the conference [1]. While the conference itself established rules for the "Scramble," it did not officially grant territory; instead, it provided the diplomatic stage for Leopold to secure individual treaties of recognition for his front organization, the Association Internationale du Congo (AIC) [1, 2].
The legal mechanism involved the recognition of the AIC as a sovereign state by the 14 powers, with the United States being the first to do so on April 22, 1884 [3]. At the closing session of the conference in February 1885, the AIC formally adhered to the General Act as a "friendly" and sovereign power, effectively turning Leopold’s private association into an internationally recognized government [4, 5].

    Source Citation: Hochschild, p. 81, 84 [1, 3, 5].

2. Belgian Colony vs. Personal Property
The Congo Free State was Leopold II’s personal property, not a Belgian colony [6]. The distinction was absolute:

    Sovereignty: Leopold was the "King-Sovereign" or "proprietor" of the Congo [6]. His power was shared in no way with the Belgian government, whose ministers often only learned of Congo’s new laws or treaties through the newspapers [6].
    Constitutional Authority: In Belgium, he was a constitutional monarch bound by parliament; in the Congo, he was an absolute monarch [6]. As contemporary jurist Professor Cattier noted: "Sovereignty does not reside in the Congolese nation, it is vested in the person of the Sovereign. Leopold II is not the trustee, but the titulary of sovereignty" [7].
    Financial Separation: Leopold had to promise the Belgian Parliament that the Congo would never be a financial drain on Belgium to receive permission to become its monarch [8].
    Source Citation: Hochschild, p. 87-88 [6, 8]; Morel/Cattier, p. 20-21 [7].

3. Promises to the Conference
In exchange for diplomatic recognition, Leopold made several specific humanitarian and economic promises:

    Free Trade: He promised a free-trade zone across the Congo basin, guaranteeing that no import duties would be levied on the goods of any nation [1, 9].
    Humanitarian Progress: He asserted his aim was to abolish slavery and the slave trade [10, 11].
    Neutrality: He pledged the neutrality of the territory to prevent it from becoming a source of conflict between European powers [11, 12].
    Welfare: Article 6 of the General Act, which Leopold adhered to, required signatories to "watch over the preservation of the native tribes, and to care for the improvement of the conditions of their moral and material well-being" [13, 14].
    Source Citation: Hochschild, p. 80, 84 [1, 11]; General Act, Art. 6 [13].

4. The International Association of the Congo (AIC)

    Creation: The AIC was created around the end of 1882 [15, 16]. It was a successor to his previous front, the Comité d’Études du Haut-Congo [16, 17].
    Claims: It claimed to be a philanthropic, scientific, and humanitarian organization [11, 18]. Leopold used the name because it was confusingly similar to the older, more respected "International African Association" (AIA) [16, 17]. He claimed the AIC was a "Society of the Red Cross" formed with the "noble aim of rendering lasting and disinterested services to the cause of progress" [19].
    Source Citation: Hochschild, p. 64-66 [11, 16, 19].

Leopold’s Humanitarian Statements
Leopold used high-minded rhetoric to mask his commercial intentions. Notable quotes include:

    1876 Opening Address: "To open to civilization the only part of our globe where it has not yet penetrated, to pierce the darkness that envelops whole populations, is, I venture to say, a crusade worthy of this century of progress" [20, 21].
    On Self-Interest: "I was guided by no egotism... Belgium may be a small country, but she is happy and satisfied with her fate; I have no other ambition than to serve her well" [21].
    Christian Duty: Leopold told George Washington Williams, "What I do there is done as a Christian duty to the poor African; and I do not wish to have one franc back of all the money I have expended" [22].
    Moral Regeneration: "Our only programme, I am anxious to repeat, is the work of moral and material regeneration" [23].
    Source Citation: Hochschild, p. 43 [21], p. 106 [22]; Ewans, p. 31 [20].

Arbitrary Lines: African Ethnic Groups and Colonial Borders
Based on the sources provided, here are the details regarding the partitioned ethnic groups and colonial borders:
1. The Ewe People
The Ewe people were split by the Togo-Ghana border, which was originally established by Great Britain and Germany [1, 2]. Today, they are primarily divided across the following states:

    Ghana
    Togo
    Benin [3].

Historical accounts note that while Ghana and Togo raised objections to this boundary at independence, the colonial borders remained unchanged [1].
2. The Bakongo
The Bakongo (or Kongo people) were partitioned across several states in Central Africa during the Scramble for Africa [4]. They are divided today across:

    Angola
    Republic of the Congo (Brazzaville)
    Democratic Republic of the Congo (formerly Zaire/Leopoldville) [4]
    Gabon [3].

The Bakongo were part of a sophisticated fifteenth-century kingdom that spanned these regions, and despite their partition, they maintained a strong sense of unique identity that later influenced political parties like ABAKO during the decolonization era [5, 6].
3. The Afar People
The Afar people are currently divided across three states in the Horn of Africa:

    Ethiopia (specifically North-East Ethiopia) [7].
    Eritrea (the former Italian colony established around Massawa) [8].
    Djibouti (the former French foothold established at Obock) [8].

They are identified as a "split group" that has faced significant political marginalization and government-led discrimination, particularly within the Ethiopian context [7].
4. Colonial Borders Following Existing Boundaries
While most colonial borders were arbitrary, there were several notable exceptions where Europeans either utilized or preserved pre-existing political or ethnic geographies:

    Swaziland and Burundi: These are cited as rare instances where Europeans attempted to take into account existing political geography [9].
    Rwanda and Lesotho (Basutoland): These colonies were composed of pre-colonial states that largely maintained their historical territorial frames [10, 11].
    Botswana (Bechuanaland), Morocco, Tunisia, Egypt, and Zanzibar: These are identified as a "handful of instances" where pre-existing state units coincided with and persisted within the territorial frame of the colonial order [11].

Despite these exceptions, historians like H.L. Wesseling emphasize that in Africa, "the map was drawn, and then it was decided what was going to happen," often creating a reality that did not previously exist on the ground [12].

The Partition and Irredentism of the Somali Ethnic Group
Based on the provided sources, particularly Michalopoulos & Papaioannou (2016) and relevant historical accounts, here is the information regarding the division of the Somali ethnic group:
1. States Dividing the Somali Group Today
Today, the Somali ethnic and linguistic group is divided among four states:

    Somalia (which includes the territory of the former Italian and British Somalilands) [1, 2].
    Ethiopia (specifically the Ogaden region/Southern Ethiopia) [1, 3, 4].
    Kenya (specifically the Northern Frontier District/Northern Kenya) [1, 4].
    Djibouti (formerly French Somaliland) [1].

The five-pointed star on the flag of Somalia symbolizes these five partitioned regions: Italian Somaliland, British Somaliland, French Somaliland (Djibouti), Northern Kenya, and Southern Ethiopia [1].
2. Formalization of Borders and Colonial Powers
The borders dividing Somali territory were created during the "Scramble for Africa" through the turn of the twentieth century by the following powers [5]:

    Great Britain: Created the borders for the Somaliland Protectorate (British Somaliland) and the Northern Frontier District of Kenya [1, 6].
    Italy: Established Italian Somaliland (later known as the Benadir Coast) [1, 7].
    France: Established a foothold at Obock, which became French Somaliland (Djibouti) [1, 8].
    Ethiopia: Under Emperor Menelik II, Ethiopia secured the Ogaden region [1, 3].

These divisions were finalized through a series of bilateral treaties between 1888 and 1908, such as the Anglo-French Agreement (1888), the Anglo-Italian Protocol (1891), and the Anglo-Ethiopian Treaty (1897) [9-11].
3. Specific 1884-Era Agreements
Several key agreements and diplomatic events around 1884–1885 laid the groundwork for these borders:

    The Berlin Conference (Nov 1884 – Feb 1885): While it primarily discussed the Congo, it established the "effective occupation" principles used by European powers to divide the entire continent, including the Somali coast [5, 12].
    British Abandonment (1884): Due to lack of economic self-sufficiency, the British quietly abandoned their holdings in the Somali region in 1884–85, which were then claimed by other powers [8].
    Secret Anglo-Italian Agreement (Feb 1885): Italy took control of the port of Massawa by a secret agreement with the British, which served as a starting point for their regional colonial ambitions [8].
    Anglo-French Agreements (1888): A formal exchange of notes between the British and French governments defined their respective rights in the Gulf of Tajourah and on the Somali Coast [9, 13].

4. Academic Term for Somali Territorial Aspiration
The academic term for the desire to unify these partitioned territories into a single state is irredentism (specifically "pan-Somali irredentism") [1, 14-17]. This movement seeks to recover "lost" territories that are ethnically Somali but fall under the jurisdiction of neighboring states [1, 14].

The Long Shadow of African Border Partitioning
In their 2016 article published in the American Economic Review, Stelios Michalopoulos and Elias Papaioannou uncover the following regarding the consequences of the Scramble for Africa:
1. Number of Partitioned Ethnic Groups
The authors identified 229 ethnic groups (representing 27.7 percent of their total sample) as being "partitioned" [1].

    Definition of "Partitioned": The authors defined an ethnicity as partitioned (or "SPLIT") if at least 10 percent of its historical surface area fell into more than one contemporary state [1].
    Alternative Definition: When the authors lowered this threshold to a 5 percent land-area requirement, they identified 266 partitioned groups [1].
    Page Number: These statistics and definitions are found on page 1812 of the article [1].

2. Main Findings on Conflict Rates
The authors found a significant and detrimental correlation between ethnic partitioning and political violence [2]. Their primary findings include:

    Conflict Intensity and Likelihood: Conflict intensity is approximately 40 percent higher and the likelihood of conflict is 8 percent higher in the homelands of partitioned ethnicities compared to non-split groups [3].
    Total Political Violence: Partitioned ethnicities experience a 57 percent increase in political violence incidents [4].
    Types of Conflict: Partitioning is specifically linked to "two-sided" conflict between government troops and rebel groups, as well as violence against civilians [5, 6]. It is not significantly associated with riots, protests, or conflict between non-state actors [5, 6].
    Ethnic Wars and Discrimination: Partitioned groups have an 11 to 14 percent increased likelihood of participating in civil wars with an explicit ethnic dimension [7]. Furthermore, the likelihood of a split group facing political discrimination from the national government is approximately 7 percentage points higher than for non-split groups [7, 8].
    Spillover Effects: Partitioning in one homeland also affects neighboring regions; conflict intensity is roughly 30 percent higher in homelands where at least half of the adjacent groups are themselves partitioned [4, 9].
    Economic Impact: Respondents identifying with split groups register lower levels of wealth and have, on average, half a year less of schooling than those from non-split groups [10, 11].
    Page Numbers: General statistics are on page 1804 [3]; violence incidents on page 1820 [4]; spillover data on page 1821 [4]; ethnic war and discrimination data on pages 1805 and 1835 [7, 8, 12]; and individual well-being data on pages 1842 and 1844 [10, 11].

The authors constructed their dataset by overlaying modern political boundaries onto a historical map of ethnic homelands [13].

    Pre-colonial Map: The authors used George Peter Murdock’s Ethnolinguistic Map (1959), which depicts the spatial distribution of 843 African ethnicities at the time of European colonization in the late 19th and early 20th centuries [13].
    National Borders: These were projected from the 2000 Digital Chart of the World [13].
    Page Number: Information on the map and dataset construction is on page 1811 [13].

4. Mention of the Somali Case
Yes, the authors specifically mention the Somali case as a prominent example of the detrimental effects of partitioning and irredentism [14, 15].

    Details provided: They note that Somalis were split during the Scramble for Africa between four different European colonies and the Ogaden region of Ethiopia [14].
    Somali Flag: They explain that the five-pointed star on the flag of Somalia symbolizes the desire to unify these five partitioned regions: Italian Somaliland, British Somaliland, French Somaliland (Djibouti), Northern Kenya, and Southern Ethiopia [14].
    Warfare: The authors point to three long-lasting wars driven by the desire of Somalis in Ethiopia to unify with Somalia [14].

The Mandate of Empire: Berlin Conference and African Partition
Based on the General Act of the Berlin Conference (1885) and the historical accounts provided in the sources, here are the requirements of Articles 34 and 35 and their practical effects on colonization:
1. Article 34: Requirements for Claiming New Territories
Article 34 of the General Act requires that any Power taking possession of a tract of land on the African coasts (outside of its existing possessions) or assuming a protectorate there must notify the other Signatory Powers of the Act [1-3]. This notification was intended to provide other nations the opportunity to "make good any claims of their own" (counterclaims) if they deemed it necessary [1, 3, 4].
2. Article 35: Requirements for "Effective Occupation"
Article 35 establishes the obligation of Signatory Powers to maintain a minimum level of authority in the coastal territories they claim [3, 5, 6].
Article 35 word-for-word:
> "The Signatory Powers of the present Act recognize the obligation to insure the establishment of authority in the regions occupied by them on the coasts of the African Continent sufficient to protect existing rights, and, as the case may be, freedom of trade and of transit under the conditions agreed upon." [3, 5, 7]
3. Practical Effects on the Speed of Colonization
While the doctrine of "effective occupation" was legally restricted to the African coasts—where very little territory remained unclaimed—it had a profound impact on the acceleration and methodology of the partition of the entire continent [8-11].

    Acceleration of the Scramble: The Berlin Conference did not start the scramble for Africa, but it provided the rules that accelerated a race already in progress [8]. Following the ratification of the Act, European occupation continued with "renewed vim and vigor" [8].
    The "Hegemony Imperative": The doctrine made it clear that "vague spheres of influence" would no longer suffice for proprietary title [12]. This created a "hegemony imperative" that required the rapid establishment of a skeletal grid of regional administration, military garrisons, and outposts on the ground [12-14].
    Pre-emptive Colonization: Because the Act required notification of claims, it encouraged powers to engage in "pre-emptive colonization" to secure lands before rivals could do so [15, 16]. Once a "paper partition" appeared on a map, it awakened national pressure to make those claims good on the ground to prevent counterclaims [17].
    Expansion Inland: Although technically limited to the coasts, the spirit of the rule precipitated rapid expansion into the interior, particularly in West Africa, as nations rushed to secure "hinterlands" for their coastal settlements [11, 18, 19].
    Avoidance of Conflict: By formalizing these procedures, the Act provided a diplomatic framework that allowed the continent to be partitioned quickly and without significant military conflict between the European powers themselves [10, 20].

The Berlin Conference: African Exclusion and Erasure
Based on the sources provided, particularly Thomas Pakenham’s The Scramble for Africa and the scholarly assessments in Bismarck, Europe, and Africa (which includes contributions from H.L. Wesseling), here are the answers to your questions regarding African representation at the Berlin Conference:
1. African Invitations and Presence
No African rulers, governments, or representatives were invited to or present at the Berlin Conference. The conference was a strictly European and Western affair.

    Exact Quote: "This Berlin Conference was attended by every Western nation except Switzerland and the United States but not by even a single African state..." [1]
    Exact Quote: "In 1884 no Africans were invited to the Conference, except that, for tactical reasons, the British had initially considered whether or not the Sultan of Zanzibar should be represented." [2]
    Exact Quote: "Not a single African was at the table in Berlin." [3]
    Exact Quote: "The most irrelevant factor in deciding the fate of the continent was the Africans themselves who were neither consulted nor apprised of the conference..." [4]

2. Awareness and Protest at the Time
African leaders were generally unaware of the conference while it was happening, as the diplomatic negotiations took place entirely in Europe. Most African resistance and protests occurred later, during the "third stage" of the scramble, when European armies arrived to enforce the treaties and boundaries decided in Berlin [5].

    The Sultan of Zanzibar: One notable exception was the Sultan of Zanzibar, who attempted to assert his authority and later protested when he realized his mainland territories were being claimed. However, his formal protest arrived in Berlin in May 1885, shortly after the conference had already concluded [6, 7].
    Lack of Knowledge: Regarding the general awareness of African rulers, the sources state: "Since the second stage of the Scramble, that of the bilateral European treaties, took place in Europe without the knowledge or participation of the Africans themselves, one would not expect any reactions." [5, 8]
    Reaction to Results: The sources note that African leaders only realized the significance of the conference once occupation began: "African chiefs and rulers, unaware of the reality and factual significance of what had transpired, began to resist measures being introduced by the agents of the new protectorates." [9]

3. Discussion Regarding the Exclusion of Africans
While the conference did not include Africans, the participants were aware of and occasionally mentioned their absence, primarily to frame the conference in a humanitarian light.

    Sir Edward Malet (Great Britain): The British representative explicitly noted the absence of Africans in his address to the conference.
        Exact Quote: "I cannot forget that the natives are not represented amongst us, and that the decisions of the Conference will, nevertheless, have an extreme importance for them." [10, 11]
    Leopold II’s Strategy: Leopold II did not discuss including Africans; instead, he used his agents to frame his private enterprise as a "confederation of free negro republics" to gain international sympathy, while privately instructing his staff that "There is no question of granting the slightest political power to negroes. That would be absurd." [12]
    Tactical Considerations: The only actual discussion of potential African representation mentioned in the sources was a tactical thought by the British regarding the Sultan of Zanzibar, but this did not result in an invitation [2].

The Berlin Conference: Protocols of the African Scramble
Based on the General Act of Berlin (1885) and Thomas Pakenham's The Scramble for Africa, here are the answers to your questions:
1. Running Dates of the Berlin Conference
The Berlin Conference officially opened on Saturday, 15 November 1884 [1]. It concluded with the signing of the General Act on 26 February 1885 [2].
2. The 14 Signatory Powers
The 14 powers that were represented and signed the General Act of the Berlin Conference are:

    Great Britain (United Kingdom) [1, 3]
    France [1, 3]
    Germany [1, 3]
    Austria-Hungary [1, 3]
    Belgium [1, 3]
    Denmark [1, 3]

3. Role of the United States
The United States was a signatory to the General Act of Berlin, though it did not ratify the agreement [4]. The U.S. role was that of an active participant; it was the first nation to recognize King Leopold II's claim to the Congo earlier in 1884 [5]. At the conference, the U.S. was represented by two delegates, including Henry Shelton Sanford, and employed the famous explorer Henry Morton Stanley as a "technical adviser" to the delegation [6].
4. Bismarck's Stated Reason for Convening the Conference
In his opening address, Chancellor Bismarck stated that the aim of the conference was to "promote the civilization of the African natives by opening the interior of that continent to commerce" [7, 8]. He sought to facilitate access for all commercial nations to the interior of Africa and regulate conditions for trade and navigation to avoid international friction [7, 9].
