## HOOK + CONCEED-BEAT (0:00 - 2:00)

August 8th, 1444.

Prince Henry the Navigator sits on horseback in Lagos, Portugal, watching 235 captured Africans being divided into lots. He takes his royal fifth — 46 people for his personal estate — while captives cling to one another, so close they can hardly be separated.

The claim you've probably seen online goes like this:

[CLIP — Asmongold/streamer]

Europeans never kidnapped anyone. They just bought slaves from a pre-existing African market. Other black people sold them.

But the chronicle we just opened with was written by Prince Henry's own court historian. Europe organized slave raids — and when those raids couldn't keep up with demand, they built a system to do it at scale.

Now — slavery did exist in Africa before Europeans arrived. But it came in two specific forms — and neither was the system Europeans tapped into.

The first was the Trans-Saharan trade — Muslim merchants moving captives across the desert. In 1614, the Timbuktu scholar Ahmed Baba ruled on which captives could legally be held:

"Non-belief is that which makes enslavement permissible, whether the enslaved were black or white."

Enslavement was bound to religion, not race. Any Muslim, of any color, could not be enslaved.

The second was sub-Saharan inter-polity slavery. Olaudah Equiano was an Igbo man, from what is now southeastern Nigeria — kidnapped as a child in 1756, later sold into the Atlantic trade. He's describing his community's internal slavery before the Atlantic system reached them:

"They always carry slaves through our land; but the strictest account is exacted of their manner of procuring them before they are suffered to pass. Sometimes indeed we sold slaves to them, but they were only prisoners of war, or such among us as had been convicted of kidnapping, for adultery, and some other crimes, which we esteemed heinous."

Equiano's community had slavery. His own father owned slaves. But the limits were strict — only war captives or convicted criminals.

Both systems had hard lines around who could be sold. The Atlantic system erased them — and replaced them with one: race. It didn't just change the rules. It changed the scale.

The Trans-Saharan trade averaged about a thousand people a year per route. The Atlantic system peaked at eighty thousand a year.

And it didn't borrow from the African systems. Before 1450, enslaved people in Africa were absorbed into households and communities. The Atlantic trade turned them into the output of an industry — what historian Paul Lovejoy calls the shift from a marginal institution to a "mode of production."

That kind of slavery — racially coded, demographically catastrophic, industrial in scale — was created by the Atlantic trade. Not borrowed from it.

## THE TURN — Afonso I Letter (2:00 - 2:45)

The first major crack in this myth comes from inside Africa itself.

By 1526, the King of Kongo, Afonso the First, wasn't managing a booming domestic slave market. He was writing desperate letters to the King of Portugal, begging him to stop the trade.

"The said merchants take our countrymen the sons of our land and the sons of our noblemen every day... our land is being all but depopulated."

He didn't stop there:

"Our will is that in this Kingdom there be no slave trade nor [any] outlet for it."

If Europeans were just buying from an existing market, why is the King of Kongo demanding it stop? Because by 1526, the Atlantic market had been running on European demand for three quarters of a century.

Where did it start?

## EVIDENCE 1 — Zurara Chapter XIX (2:45 - 3:45)

It's 1444. A Portuguese fleet attacks the Arguin Bank, off the coast of modern Mauritania. Zurara — Prince Henry's court chronicler — recorded the operation.

"But they, shouting out 'St. James', 'St. George', 'Portugal', at once attacked them, killing and taking all they could."

And the aftermath:

"Then might you see mothers forsaking their children, and husbands their wives... others stowed their children among the sea-weed, where our men found them afterwards."

Zurara is describing a raid. 165 people — children pulled from seaweed, mothers separated from their families — taken back to Portugal.

This wasn't trade. It was kidnapping. And it wasn't a one-off — the records show constant raids throughout the 1440s.

Then something changed.

## BRIDGE — From Raid To System (3:45 - 4:45)

The raids didn't stop because Europeans had a change of heart. They stopped because African forces shut them down. In 1445, Africans wielding poisoned arrows killed twenty of twenty-two Portuguese on one expedition — including the mission leader, Nuno Tristão.

The lasso wasn't working. So in 1448, Prince Henry issued a formal order: no more military raids. From now on — trade.

But here's what "trade" meant in practice.

By the 1450s, a Portuguese factory at Arguim — a fortified post off modern Mauritania — was supplying eight hundred to a thousand captives a year. Those captives weren't being offered voluntarily. African intermediaries were raiding their own neighbors to supply European demand — because the price made it profitable. Portugal stopped doing the kidnapping directly. The system still ran on kidnapping.

Meanwhile, Iberian sugar plantations were taking root on the Atlantic islands — Madeira, Cape Verde, São Tomé. These were the prototypes New World plantations would copy.

And the numbers climbed. Between 1450 and 1500, eighty-one thousand enslaved Africans crossed into the Atlantic system. The next century: three hundred thirty-eight thousand.

This is what Afonso was writing about in 1526. Seventy-five years of systematic extraction.

So how did Europe respond?

## MID-PIVOT — João III (4:45 - 5:30)

In 1529, João III, King of Portugal, replied to Afonso. He told the King of Kongo the depopulation wasn't happening.

Read it. The denial contains the confession.

"You say in your letters that you do not want there to be any slave trade in your kingdom because it is depopulating your land. I can believe that you only say this as a result of the suffering that the Portuguese cause you, because I am told of the great size of Kongo and how it is so populated that it appears that not a single slave has left it."

He's not saying "no slaves are being taken." He's saying Kongo is so large you wouldn't notice. The denial isn't "we're not doing this" — it's "you won't miss them."

## EVIDENCE 2 — Pieter de Marees (5:30 - 7:00)

The Crown denied the scale. But the merchants were documenting it.

In 1602, a Dutch slave-trader on the Gold Coast writes back to his investors to explain why he can't fill his ships:

"They also enslave one another, but not in the same manner as in Angola or Conge, for it would not be possible to load ships here with blacks. They do not have at their disposal a multitude of Captives, and so on the Gold Coast they cannot be purchased in large numbers."

De Marees is admitting two things.

Yes — indigenous slavery existed even on the Gold Coast. He says so. But the volume Europeans needed wasn't there. He couldn't fill his ships. Indigenous markets couldn't supply Atlantic demand.

So if the Atlantic system was running — and by 1602 it was, in massive volume — the captives weren't coming from a pre-existing market. They were being newly captured because Europeans were buying.

Patrick Manning's economic history shows the math. The price of a newly-captured person reflected capture cost — which could be very low. The price at the coast reflected productive value to the buyer. In Dahomey, Manning found a fifteen-to-one ratio: a slave at the coast sold for fifteen times what the king paid his soldiers per captive taken. That gap is what made raiding economically rational. European demand created the gap.

The Atlantic system didn't tap into a market. It created the incentive to build one.

## EVIDENCE 3 — Romanus Pontifex (7:00 - 8:30)

Which brings us to the fourth document — the one that licensed this before it even scaled.

On January 8th, 1455, Pope Nicholas V — the highest legal and moral authority in Europe — issued a papal bull called Romanus Pontifex, granting Portugal an exclusive monopoly in Africa.

On the left, the original Latin — on the right, the English.

"...illorumque personas in perpetuam servitutem redigendi..."

"...and to reduce their persons to perpetual slavery..."

And this wasn't the first time. Three years earlier, in June 1452, Nicholas V had already signed Dum Diversas — same operative clause, word for word.

Same Pope, same words — Romanus Pontifex was the upgrade, giving Portugal exclusive rights across Africa.

The bull authorizes Portugal to invade, search out, and capture —

— to vanquish and subdue all Saracens and pagans whatsoever —

— and to reduce their persons to perpetual slavery.

This was 1455. The legal authority for perpetual slavery was drawn up before the commerce existed. And the operators relied on it — Prince Henry himself cited "various earlier papal bulls" as the "powerful authority" for his slave trade.

The Pope didn't license a market. He licensed a conquest.

---

## EVIDENCE 4 — 1518 Gorrevod Asiento (8:30 - 9:30)

The document that shows what that conquest looked like in practice is this.

Sixty-three years later, in August 1518, the King of Spain issued a license to Lorenzo de Gorrevod:

"Know ye that I have given permission... to Lorenzo de Gorrevod... whereby he... may proceed to take to the Indies, the islands and the mainland of the ocean sea... four thousand negro slaves both male and female, provided they be Christians... without coming to Seville, as many as possible being taken from Cape Verde."

Four thousand people. In a single contract. The "provided they be Christians" clause was the legal alibi — captives would be baptized on arrival, reframing kidnapping as salvation. Sourced from Cape Verde, a Portuguese entrepôt feeding African captives into the Atlantic system.

This is the asiento. The moment the papal license became a commercial contract. The bull authorized perpetual slavery in 1455; by 1518, the Spanish crown was contracting for it by the thousand.

The Pope didn't just bless a market. He chartered an industry.

## CLOSING (9:30 - 10:15)

Five documents —

the chronicle of the first raids,

the desperate letter of an African king,

the frustration of a European buyer,

the legal decree of the Pope,

and the royal license that put it into practice.

The talking point is too broad to show the full picture. Yes, slavery existed before Europeans arrived. But what didn't exist was the scale, the legal authority, and the racial logic that made it permanent.
