## HOOK + CONCEED-BEAT (0:00 - 2:30)

August 8th, 1444.

Prince Henry the Navigator sits on horseback in Lagos, Portugal, watching 235 captured Africans being divided into lots. He takes his royal fifth — 46 people for his personal estate — while captives cling to one another, so close they can hardly be separated.

The claim you've probably seen online goes like this:

[CLIP — Asmongold/streamer]

Europeans never kidnapped anyone. They just bought slaves from a pre-existing African market. Other black people sold them.

But the chronicle we just opened with was written by Prince Henry's own court historian.

Europe organized slave raids. And when those raids couldn't keep up with European demand, they built a system to do it at scale — the system we now know as the transatlantic slave trade.

Now — there is a kernel of truth in this claim. Slavery did exist in Africa before Europeans arrived. It existed in most civilizations in history. But the slavery that existed in Africa before the Atlantic trade came in two very specific forms — and neither one was the system Europeans tapped into.

The first was the Trans-Saharan trade — the system Muslim merchants ran across the desert from West Africa to North Africa and the Middle East. It had its own legal framework. In 1614, the Timbuktu scholar Ahmed Baba was asked by trans-Saharan slave merchants which captives they could legally hold. His ruling laid out the boundaries:

"Non-belief is that which makes enslavement permissible, whether the enslaved were black or white."

In the Muslim parts of Africa, enslavement was bound to religion, not race. Any Muslim, of any color, could not be enslaved.

The second form was sub-Saharan inter-polity slavery. Olaudah Equiano was an Igbo man — from what is now southeastern Nigeria, a region that was not a major slave-trading state — kidnapped as a child in 1756 and later sold into the Atlantic trade. He's not a representative of a polity that sold people to Europeans. He's describing his community's internal slavery before the Atlantic system reached them. His testimony:

"They always carry slaves through our land; but the strictest account is exacted of their manner of procuring them before they are suffered to pass. Sometimes indeed we sold slaves to them, but they were only prisoners of war, or such among us as had been convicted of kidnapping, for adultery, and some other crimes, which we esteemed heinous."

Equiano's community had slavery. His own father owned slaves. But the limits were strict — only war captives or convicted criminals.

Both regional systems had categorical lines around who could be sold. The Atlantic system erased those lines — and replaced them with one: race. And it didn't just change the rules. It changed the scale.

The Trans-Saharan trade averaged about a thousand people a year per route.

The Atlantic system hit a peak of eighty thousand people a year. An eighty-times multiplier.

And it didn't borrow from the African systems. Historian Paul Lovejoy — Cambridge University Press, one of the most cited scholars of African slavery — documents this directly: before 1450, enslaved people in Africa were absorbed into households and communities. The Atlantic trade turned them into the output of an industry. Lovejoy calls this the shift to a "mode of production" — slavery went from a marginal institution to the organizing economic principle of entire regions.

That kind of slavery — racially coded, demographically catastrophic, industrial in scale — was created by the Atlantic trade. Not borrowed from it.

## THE TURN — Afonso I Letter (2:30 - 3:30)

The first major crack in the "we just bought what they sold" myth comes from inside Africa itself.

By 1526, the King of Kongo, Afonso the First, wasn't managing a booming domestic slave market. He was writing desperate letters to the King of Portugal, begging him to stop the trade.

Here are Afonso's exact words:

"The said merchants take our countrymen the sons of our land and the sons of our noblemen every day... our land is being all but depopulated."

He didn't stop there. He issued an explicit demand to shut the system down:

"Our will is that in this Kingdom there be no slave trade nor [any] outlet for it."

If the Atlantic system was just Europeans buying from an existing market, why is a reigning African king on the record demanding that it stop?

Because by 1526, the Atlantic market had been running on European demand for more than three quarters of a century. The chronicle we opened with — written in the 1440s — is the beginning of that record.

## EVIDENCE 1 — Zurara Chapter XIX (3:30 - 4:30)

Let's look at that chronicle more closely.

It's 1444. A Portuguese fleet attacks the Arguin Bank, off the coast of modern-day Mauritania. We know exactly what happened because Prince Henry's official court chronicler, Gomes Eanes de Zurara, recorded the entire military operation.

He wrote:

"But they, shouting out 'St. James', 'St. George', 'Portugal', at once attacked them, killing and taking all they could."

And he described the aftermath:

"Then might you see mothers forsaking their children, and husbands their wives... others stowed their children among the sea-weed, where our men found them afterwards."

What Zurara is describing is a raid. People — children pulled from seaweed, mothers separated from their families — taken back to Portugal. 165 of them, in that single raid alone.

This wasn't trade. It was kidnapping. And it wasn't a one-off — the records show constant raids throughout the 1440s.

Then something changed.

---

## BRIDGE — From Raid To System (4:30 - 5:30)

The raids didn't stop because Europeans had a change of heart. They stopped because African forces shut them down. Zurara himself records that in 1445, Africans wielding poisoned arrows killed twenty of the twenty-two Portuguese on one expedition — including the leader of the mission, Nuno Tristão. Zurara called their way of fighting something that "would be frightful to any reasonable man."

The lasso wasn't working. So in 1448, Prince Henry issued a formal order: no more military raids. From now on — trade.

But here is what "trade" meant in practice.

By the 1450s, a Portuguese trading factory at Arguim — a fortified post off the coast of modern Mauritania — was supplying eight hundred to a thousand captives a year to the European market. Those captives weren't being offered voluntarily. African intermediaries were raiding their own neighbors to supply European demand — because the price Europeans were paying made it profitable. Portugal stopped doing the kidnapping directly. The system still ran on kidnapping.

Meanwhile, Iberian sugar plantations were taking root on the Atlantic islands — Madeira, the Canaries, Cape Verde, São Tomé. These were the systems New World plantations would later copy.

And the numbers climbed. Between 1450 and 1500, eighty-one thousand enslaved Africans crossed into the Atlantic system. The next century: three hundred thirty-eight thousand.

This is what Afonso of Kongo was writing about in 1526. Not a few raids — seventy-five years of systematic extraction, drawing tens of thousands of people from coastal factories across West Africa.

So how did Europe respond?

## MID-PIVOT — João III (5:30 - 6:30)

Let's look at how Portugal replied.

In 1529, the King of Portugal, João III, responded to Afonso. He told the King of Kongo that the depopulation he was protesting wasn't actually happening.

Read it. The denial contains the confession.

"You say in your letters that you do not want there to be any slave trade in your kingdom because it is depopulating your land. I can believe that you only say this as a result of the suffering that the Portuguese cause you, because I am told of the great size of Kongo and how it is so populated that it appears that not a single slave has left it."

Read it again. He's not saying "no slaves are being taken." He's saying Kongo is so large that even if slaves ARE being taken, you wouldn't notice it. The denial isn't "we're not doing this" — it's "you won't miss them."

The buyer tells the king his country is so vast he won't even notice the people being taken.

---

## EVIDENCE 2 — Pieter de Marees (6:30 - 7:45)

The Crown denied the scale. But the merchants were documenting it themselves.

Let's look at what a Dutch slave-trader writing on the Gold Coast around 1602 is sending back to his investors to explain why he can't find enough cargo.

"They also enslave one another, but not in the same manner as in Angola or Conge, for it would not be possible to load ships here with blacks. They do not have at their disposal a multitude of Captives, and so on the Gold Coast they cannot be purchased in large numbers."

De Marees is admitting two things at once.

First — yes, indigenous slavery existed even on the Gold Coast. He says so.

Second — the volume Europeans needed wasn't there. He couldn't fill his ships from existing captives. Indigenous markets couldn't supply Atlantic demand.

So if the Atlantic system was running — and by 1602 it was, in massive volume — the captives weren't coming from a pre-existing market. They were coming from people being newly captured because Europeans were buying.

Here's how historian Patrick Manning explains the economics. The price paid for a newly captured person was based on the cost of capturing them — which could be very low. The price at the coast for a slave ready to work was based on their productive value to the buyer. Manning found a fifteen-to-one ratio in the Kingdom of Dahomey: a slave at the coast sold for fifteen times what the king nominally paid his soldiers for each captive taken. That gap — between capture cost and market value — is what made raiding economically rational. European demand created that gap.

The Atlantic system didn't tap into a market. It created the incentive to build one.

## EVIDENCE 3 — Romanus Pontifex (7:45 - 9:00)

Which brings us to the fourth document — the one that licensed this system before it even scaled.

On January 8th, 1455, Pope Nicholas V issued a papal bull called Romanus Pontifex. The Pope was the highest legal and moral authority in Europe, and he issued this at the request of the King of Portugal, granting them an exclusive monopoly in Africa.

On the left, the original Latin from 1455 — on the right, the English translation.

"...illorumque personas in perpetuam servitutem redigendi..."

"...and to reduce their persons to perpetual slavery..."

And this wasn't the first time Nicholas V had issued that phrase. Three years earlier, in June 1452, he'd already signed Dum Diversas — with the same operative clause, word for word.

"...illorumque personas in perpetuam servitutem redigendi..."

Same Pope, same words — Romanus Pontifex was the upgrade, giving Portugal exclusive rights across Africa.

The bull authorizes Portugal to invade, search out, and capture —

— to vanquish and subdue all Saracens and pagans whatsoever —

— and to reduce their persons to perpetual slavery.

This was 1455. The legal authority for open-ended, permanent, perpetual slavery was drawn up before the commerce existed. And the operators relied on it. Historian P.E. Russell documents that Prince Henry himself cited "various earlier papal bulls" as the "powerful authority" for his slave trade.

The Pope didn't license a market. He licensed a conquest.

---

## EVIDENCE 4 — 1518 Gorrevod Asiento (9:00 - 10:00)

The document that shows what that conquest looked like in practice is this.

Sixty-three years after Romanus Pontifex, in August 1518, the King of Spain issued a license to a man named Lorenzo de Gorrevod. The terms:

"Know ye that I have given permission... to Lorenzo de Gorrevod... whereby he... may proceed to take to the Indies, the islands and the mainland of the ocean sea... four thousand negro slaves both male and female, provided they be Christians... without coming to Seville, as many as possible being taken from Cape Verde."

Four thousand people. In a single contract. The "provided they be Christians" clause was the legal alibi: captives would be baptized on arrival at the Atlantic islands — a bureaucratic ceremony that reframed kidnapping as salvation. Sourced from Cape Verde — by then a Portuguese entrepôt feeding African captives into the Atlantic system.

This is the asiento. The moment the papal license became a commercial contract. The bull authorized perpetual slavery in 1455; by 1518, the Spanish crown was contracting for it by the thousand.

The Pope didn't just bless a market. He chartered an industry.

## CLOSING (10:00 - 10:45)

Five documents —

the chronicle of the first raids,

the desperate letter of an African king,

the frustration of a European buyer,

the legal decree of the Pope,

and the royal license that put it into practice.

The talking point is too broad and too generalized to show the full picture. Yes, slavery existed before Europeans arrived. But what didn't exist was the scale, the legal authority, and the racial logic that made it permanent.
