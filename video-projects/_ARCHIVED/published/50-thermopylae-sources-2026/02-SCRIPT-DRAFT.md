## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:40)

[VISUAL: Text on screen — "MOLON LABE" in large font, zoom out to bumper sticker / tattoo / rifle grip]

You've seen this phrase. "Molon labe." Come and take them. Supposedly said by King Leonidas to the Persian emperor Xerxes at Thermopylae, 480 BCE. It's on bumper stickers, rifle grips, t-shirts. One of the most famous quotes in military history.

[VISUAL: Talking head]

It doesn't appear anywhere in the earliest account of the battle.

Three ancient writers described Thermopylae. They don't agree on the armies, the ending, or who betrayed the Greeks. So I read all three.

---

## ACT 1 — WITNESS ONE: HERODOTUS (0:40–3:30)

[VISUAL: Landmark Herodotus edition on screen]

Herodotus of Halicarnassus. Born around 484 BCE — four years after the battle. He wrote his account roughly 40 years later, based on interviews with people who were actually there. By the time he's writing, these veterans are in their 60s and 70s.

He's considered the first person to systematically collect evidence and try to write down what actually happened — that's why ancient critics called him the "Father of History." They also called him the "Father of Lies" — because he regularly includes things that are clearly exaggerated or impossible.

As Cambridge historian Paul Cartledge puts it: we either write a history of Thermopylae with Herodotus, or we don't write one at all.

[VISUAL: Talking head]

The Thermopylae section is in Book Seven, sections 201 to 233.

One thing that immediately jumps out are the numbers.

[VISUAL: On-screen quote — Herodotus 7.60]

Herodotus says the Persian land army was 1.7 million men. And he describes how they counted them. Section 7.60: "They gathered groups of 10,000 men together at one spot, packed them in as closely as they could, and drew a circle around them. They erected a dry wall on the edge high enough to reach a man's navel. Then they had others go into the enclosure, until they had counted them all."

[VISUAL: Talking head]

He's not randomly inventing a large number. He's getting the wrong answer very carefully. As historian Rupert Matthews calculated: a column that large would stretch 2,000 miles. The front would reach Greece while the back was still in Iran.

[VISUAL: Map — Iran to Greece]

Modern historians put the Persian army somewhere between 150,000 and 300,000. Still massive — but not 1.7 million.

Now. The Greeks. Herodotus says about 7,000 held the pass. Not 300. Seven thousand — including 300 Spartan citizens plus allies from Thespiae, Thebes, and other cities.

And Herodotus doesn't describe a wall of muscle. He describes a real army. Section 7.210: "The Medes charged headlong into the Hellenes, and great numbers of them fell. Although others rushed forth to replace them, even they could not drive the Hellenes away. Indeed, the Hellenes made it clear to everyone, and especially to the King himself, that although there were many in his army, there were few real men."

[VISUAL: On-screen quote — Herodotus 7.210]

And they used feign tactics. Section 7.211: "The Lacedaemonians would turn their backs and feign flight all together, and the barbarians, seeing this, would pursue them with much clatter and shouting. The Lacedaemonians would allow the barbarians to catch up with them and then suddenly turn around to face them, at which point they would slay countless numbers of them."

[VISUAL: On-screen quote — Herodotus 7.211]

Then someone betrayed the pass. Section 7.213: "Ephialtes of Malis, son of Eurydemos, came to speak with the King, expecting to win some great reward for telling him of the path through the mountain."

[VISUAL: Name EPHIALTES on screen]

The Persians used that path to encircle the Greeks. When Leonidas found out the flanking force was coming, he told most of the allies to retreat. He stayed behind with men he'd picked specifically because they had living sons — he knew this was a one-way mission. Their job was to cover the retreat.

And section 7.225: "On this spot they tried to defend themselves with their daggers if they still had them, or if not, with their hands and their teeth."

[VISUAL: On-screen quote — Herodotus 7.225]

If you've seen the movie, read the graphic novel, or played any of the games — that's this version. One source, written 40 years after the fact, based on oral testimony.

## ACT 2 — WITNESS TWO: DIODORUS (3:30–5:30)

[VISUAL: Diodorus Siculus — Library of History on screen]

Diodorus Siculus. A Sicilian Greek writing roughly 430 years after the battle. He had a source we don't — a historian named Ephorus, who wrote 150 years after Thermopylae. Ephorus's 30-book history is completely lost. We only know what he said because Diodorus copied him.

And Diodorus tells the story differently.

[VISUAL: Side-by-side numbers on screen]

His Persian army: 800,000. Not 1.7 million.

His Spartan force, section 11.4.2: "Leonidas announced that only one thousand men should follow him. There were, then, of the Lacedaemonians one thousand, and with them three hundred Spartiates."

[VISUAL: On-screen quote — Diodorus 11.4.2]

One thousand Lacedaemonians — 300 full citizens plus 700 others. Both sources agree on 300 Spartiates. They disagree on how many came with them.

The betrayer was not named Ephialtes. Section 11.8.4: "There came to the king a certain Trachinian, a native of the region." A nameless local. Not Herodotus's named traitor.

[VISUAL: Talking head]

And his ending — this is where it gets genuinely strange. Diodorus says the Greeks didn't die defending a hillock. They launched a night raid on the Persian camp. Section 11.10: "The Greeks broke into the pavilion and slew almost to a man all whom they caught there. So long as it was night they wandered throughout the entire camp seeking Xerxes."

[VISUAL: On-screen quote — Diodorus 11.10.1-3]

A suicide assassination attempt. Not found in Herodotus at all. Modern historians call it almost certainly fictional — probably an invention from Ephorus, adding drama four centuries later.

## ACT 3 — WITNESS THREE: CTESIAS (5:30–6:45)

[VISUAL: Ctesias — Routledge edition on screen]

Ctesias of Cnidus. A Greek physician working at the Persian royal court for 17 years, writing about 80 years after the battle. He called Herodotus "a liar and a writer of fables." He claimed access to Persian royal records.

We don't even have his original text. What survives is a summary by Photius — a church father writing 1,300 years later, who preserved fragments of ancient works that would otherwise have been lost entirely.

[VISUAL: On-screen quote — Fragment 27]

Fragment 27: "The great Persian horde was cut down, while only two or three Spartans were killed. Then Xerxes ordered them to attack with 20,000 men. And these were defeated too. Then they were flogged to fire them up for war."

Two or three Spartans. Fantasy numbers.

[VISUAL: Talking head]

But Ctesias matters for one reason. Ephialtes — the traitor, the man who personalized the betrayal at Thermopylae — doesn't appear in his account. At all. The central plot point of Herodotus's version is completely absent from the account written closest to the Persian court.

[VISUAL: On-screen — comparison table, highlight BETRAYER and FINAL STAND rows]

| | Herodotus | Diodorus | Ctesias |
|---|-----------|----------|---------|
| Persian army | 1,700,000 | 800,000 | 800,000 |
| Greek force | ~7,000 | ~4,000 | unclear |
| Betrayer | Ephialtes (named) | "a Trachinian" (anonymous) | "Two leaders of the Trachinians" (named differently) |
| Final stand | Daytime hillock | Night raid | Surrounded from behind |

Look at the bottom two rows. The betrayer — the most dramatic moment in the story — is a named man in one account, anonymous in another, and completely absent in the third. The final stand is a daytime defence in Herodotus, a night assassination raid in Diodorus, and not described at all in Ctesias.

Three accounts, three different stories.

## ACT 4 — THE SILENCE AND THE QUOTES (6:45–8:30)

[VISUAL: Daiva Inscription image]

And I haven't mentioned the biggest problem. All three accounts are Greek. The Persian Empire — the largest on earth — didn't leave a single narrative about Thermopylae.

Paul Cartledge: "Achaemenid Persia had scribes, many of them, but not one historian."

[VISUAL: On-screen quote — Cartledge, p. 32]

Persian inscriptions recorded victories. They didn't record defeats. Every number we cite, every "Persian" quote — all of it comes from Greek sources.

[VISUAL: Talking head]

Which brings us back to the quotes.

"Molon labe." First recorded by Plutarch. 600 years after the battle. Not in Herodotus.

"Tonight we dine in Hades." First in Diodorus. 430 years after. Not in Herodotus.

The one quote with actual evidence? "We shall fight in the shade." That IS in Herodotus. But he doesn't give it to Leonidas. He gives it to a soldier named Dienekes — whom he calls the bravest of all the Spartans.

[VISUAL: On-screen quote — Herodotus 7.226]

And the strongest quote of all? An inscription in physical stone. Recorded by Herodotus at the site:

[VISUAL: Greek original text on screen, quote box overlay with English translation, voiceover]

"Go tell the Spartans, stranger passing by, that here, obedient to their orders, we lie."

Carved within years of the battle. Commissioned by the Amphictyonic League of Delphi — a political body representing Greek city-states. The closest thing to a primary source we have. And it frames their death as obedience to Sparta — not as a strategic rearguard, not as a Panhellenic defense. A military defeat repackaged as Spartan honour and ferocity. The ancients were already building a mythos around this event. The same way we still do today.

There's a companion inscription at the site. Simonides recorded it: "Four thousand from the Peloponnese once fought three million." Four thousand Greeks against an overwhelming horde. That's not a neutral record. That's an us-versus-them story carved in stone within years of the battle.

If this kind of analysis is useful to you, please subscribe.

## CLOSE (8:30–9:30)

[VISUAL: Talking head]

One last thing. 700 Thespians stayed at the pass alongside the 300 Spartans. Fought to the last man. Every source confirms this. The following year at Plataea, Herodotus records that 1,800 Thespians showed up to fight — but "not wearing armor." The city had more men. They just didn't have hoplite equipment anymore. The 700 who died at Thermopylae had been their entire armored force.

Herodotus names several individual Spartans for their bravery. Among the 700 Thespians, he names only one. Dithyrambus, son of Harmatides.

[VISUAL: Side-by-side — Spartan monument vs Thespian monument]

There's a monument to the Spartans at Thermopylae. And there's one for the Thespians too — but it wasn't unveiled until 1997. It depicts Eros — the god Thespiae was famous for — with a broken wing for voluntary sacrifice and a missing head for their anonymous death. The front reads: "In Memory of the 700 Thespians."

The epitaph says the Spartans died "obedient to their orders." The Thespians? Herodotus says they refused to leave. We remember "the 300" from this battle. That bias isn't from the movie. It's in Herodotus's own text.

[VISUAL: Talking head, comparison table returns on screen]

So what can we actually say happened? All three sources agree on the basics: a massive Persian army invaded Greece, a Greek coalition force marched out to hold them at a narrow pass, and at the very end, Spartans and Thespians stayed behind to hold up the Persian army. The flanking path, the betrayer, the ending — all of that depends on which source you read. And the Persian side left nothing at all.

[VISUAL: On-screen — "MOLON LABE" bumper sticker from the opening]

That's not a flaw — that's how history works. Three witnesses who disagree. A quote on a bumper sticker that doesn't appear in the earliest source. And your job — the historian's job — is figuring out what survives the comparison.

[END]
