## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

In 480 BCE, a Greek coalition force of roughly 7,000 men held the narrow pass of Thermopylae against a Persian invasion for three days before being encircled and destroyed. The final rearguard — around 2,000 men including 300 Spartans, 700 Thespians, and 400 Thebans — fought to near-annihilation. Three ancient writers described what happened. They do not agree on a single significant number. Herodotus, writing ~40 years after the battle, gives the foundational account but with a claimed Persian army of 2.6 million that no modern scholar accepts. Diodorus Siculus, writing 460 years later from a lost intermediary source, gives different numbers and adds details Herodotus omits (including a disputed night raid). Ctesias of Cnidus, a Greek physician at the Persian court writing ~80 years post-battle, gives numbers so different from the others that his account has been called "almost worse than useless." No Persian narrative account of the battle survives — not one. The famous quotes everyone associates with Thermopylae ("molon labe," "fight in the shade," "dine in Hades") are almost all recorded by writers working centuries after the events. The pop culture version — 300 Spartans alone, a million Persians, Leonidas as lone hero — erases the 700 Thespians who also died to the last man, massively inflates the Persian army, and draws almost entirely from one biased Greek source. This is exactly the material for a "source criticism" pilot episode.

## SECTION 1: THE THREE MAIN ANCIENT SOURCES

### 1A. Herodotus of Halicarnassus
**The foundational account — and the most contested**

| Attribute | Detail | Verify? |
|-----------|--------|---------|
| Dates | c. 484 – c. 425 BCE | Yes |
| Years after Thermopylae | ~40 years (battle 480 BCE, writing c. 440 BCE) | Yes |
| Primary work | *Histories*, Book VII, sections 7.201–7.233 | Yes — get page refs |
| Relationship to events | Not an eyewitness (born ~4 years after battle). Traveled widely and interviewed survivors and descendants. Probably interviewed Spartans during visit to Greece | Yes |
| Known biases | Admired Athenian democracy. Sympathetic to Greeks generally. Included Persian perspectives (which made Athenians suspicious of him). Called "Father of History" but also "Father of Lies" in antiquity | Yes |
| His Persian army figure | 1,700,000 fighting men (plus equivalent support, totaling ~5 million) — explicitly derived by counting water supplies and camp space | Yes |
| His Greek force figure | ~7,000 total. 300 Spartans + 700 Thespians + 400 Thebans + 1,000 Phocians + other contingents | Yes |
| His casualty figures | Up to 20,000 Persian fatalities; ~4,000 Greeks total over three days | Yes — flag as disputed |
| Survival as source | Complete text survives | Yes |

**What Herodotus says about the battle:**
- Days 1-2: Persians sent waves including the Medes, the Cissians, and the elite Immortals (10,000 men) — all repulsed. Greeks used the phalanx and feigned retreats.
- Day 2 evening: Ephialtes of Trachis, a local man "motivated by desire for a reward," revealed the Anopaea mountain path to Xerxes.
- Hydarnes (commander of the Immortals) was sent with his unit to encircle the Greeks via the mountain path.
- Day 3: Phocians guarding the path heard the Persians coming via the "rustling of oak leaves" and withdrew to higher ground (leaving the path open).
- Leonidas called a council and dismissed most Greek allies. 300 Spartans, 700 Thespians, 400 Thebans, and helots remained.
- The Thebans: Herodotus claims Leonidas held them as unwilling hostages due to Theban sympathy with Persia (medism). He describes them eventually surrendering by moving toward the Persians with hands raised.
- The Thespians: Refused to leave and chose to fight and die alongside the Spartans.
- Leonidas was killed — Herodotus says "shot down by Persian archers." The two sides fought over his body. The Greeks retained it.
- Final stand: The remaining Greeks withdrew to a hillock (Kolonos Hill) and fought until their spears broke. Then they used their swords. Then their hands and teeth. Persians tore down part of the wall and "rained arrows upon them."
- Leonidas's bones were returned to Sparta 40 years later.

**Key cinematic passage (Herodotus, Book VII, on the final stand):**
> "Here they defended themselves to the last, those who still had swords using them, and the others resisting with their hands and teeth."

**Scholarly assessment:** Modern historians consider him the most reliable ancient source for Thermopylae, though Paul Cartledge has noted "we either write a history of Thermopylae with [Herodotus], or not at all." His army figures are universally rejected by modern scholarship. His account of the Thebans is disputed (Plutarch later defended Theban honor). His methodology of citing oral tradition is recognized as limited but genuine.

### 1B. Diodorus Siculus
**The later retelling — drawing on a lost source**

| Attribute | Detail | Verify? |
|-----------|--------|---------|
| Dates | fl. 1st century BCE, c. 90–30 BCE | Yes |
| Years after Thermopylae | ~460 years | Yes |
| Primary work | *Bibliotheca Historica* (Library of History), Book XI, sections 11.4–11.11 | Yes — get page refs |
| Relationship to events | No connection to events whatsoever. Compiled a universal history from earlier sources | Yes |
| His key intermediary source | Ephorus of Cyme (c. 400–330 BCE) — writing ~150 years after Thermopylae, now entirely lost. Most of what Diodorus says about Thermopylae probably derives from Ephorus | Yes — critical point |
| Known biases | Diodorus compiled, did not critically evaluate sources. He is considered less reliable than Herodotus for this period, but his account sometimes preserves details Herodotus omits | Yes |
| His Persian army figure | 800,000 men (vs. Herodotus's 2.6 million) | Yes |
| His Greek force figure | "1,000 Lacedaemonians and 300 Spartans" — ambiguous whether the 300 are included in the 1,000 or separate | Yes — this is a key point of confusion |
| His encirclement force | Hydarnes sent with 20,000 men (vs. Herodotus's 10,000 Immortals) | Yes |
| Survival as source | Complete text survives | Yes |

**What Diodorus says differently from Herodotus:**
- He records a **night raid** by the Greeks against the Persian camp — the Greeks sneaked in at night and attempted to assassinate Xerxes. Modern scholars are highly skeptical of this detail; it appears nowhere in Herodotus.
- A man named **Tyrrhastiadas the Cymaean** warned the Greeks about the encirclement (a different informant from Herodotus's Ephialtes story — though Diodorus does not completely omit Ephialtes).
- His Greek force numbers are ambiguous and potentially different from Herodotus.
- His casualty figures differ.

**Scholarly assessment:** Diodorus is considered less reliable than Herodotus for this period. However, because he drew on Ephorus — who wrote 150 years after Thermopylae but much closer to events than Diodorus himself — he sometimes preserves earlier traditions that Herodotus missed or ignored. The night raid story is generally dismissed by modern historians as "almost certainly fictional." His value is as a record of how the story was told in the 4th century BCE, before the Herodotus version became canonical.

**The Persian-court Greek — hostile to Herodotus, probably worse than him**

| Attribute | Detail | Verify? |
|-----------|--------|---------|
| Dates | fl. late 5th – early 4th century BCE; attended Battle of Cunaxa (401 BCE) | Yes |
| Years after Thermopylae | ~79 years | Yes |
| Primary work | *Persica* (23 books) — now entirely lost. Survives only as fragments and summaries preserved by the 9th-century Byzantine scholar Photius | Yes |
| Relationship to events | Greek physician who served at the Persian royal court under Artaxerxes II. Had access to Persian royal archives (he claimed). Wrote explicitly in opposition to Herodotus | Yes |
| Known biases | Wrote to correct/attack Herodotus. His access to Persian records was real but his use of them was unreliable. Contemporaries ridiculed him for inaccuracy | Yes |
| His Persian army figure | 800,000 men assembled + 1,000 triremes (same as Diodorus — likely common source) | Yes |
| His encirclement force | 40,000 men via the mountain path (vs. Herodotus's 10,000–20,000) | Yes |
| His battle narrative | First Persian wave "cut to ribbons" with only 2–3 Spartan casualties (wildly different from Herodotus) | Yes |
| Missing from his account | The betrayal by Ephialtes — the central narrative element of Herodotus's account — is **absent** from Ctesias's version as preserved by Photius | Yes — major point |
| Survival as source | Only fragments via Photius (9th century CE) — itself ~1,400 years after the events | Yes |

**What makes Ctesias valuable despite his unreliability:**
- He was writing closer to the events than Diodorus.
- He had a different source base (Persian court) — even if he used it badly.
- His account shows what a pro-Persian or Persian-adjacent version of events looked like.
- The absence of Ephialtes in his account raises questions: did the Persian source tradition have a different explanation for how the encirclement happened?

**Scholarly assessment:** The assessment is devastating. The classicist who produced the standard modern edition described Ctesias as "almost worse than useless" for Thermopylae. The satirist Lucian placed Ctesias among history's greatest liars. Modern consensus: "(Ctesias's) unreliability makes Herodotus seem a model of accuracy." However, he is still a data point — and his disagreement with Herodotus on key details is itself historically interesting.

## SECTION 2: WHERE THE SOURCES CONTRADICT

### 2A. Persian Army Size — The Number Nobody Believes

| Source | Figure Claimed | Years After Battle | Modern Assessment |
|--------|---------------|-------------------|------------------|
| Simonides (contemporary poet) | 4,000,000 | ~0 (contemporary) | Propaganda figure |
| Herodotus | 2,600,000 fighting men (~5 million total) | ~40 | Universally rejected |

| Ctesias | 800,000 + 1,000 triremes | ~79 | Rejected |
| Modern consensus | 120,000–300,000 | N/A | Based on logistics/supply analysis |

**The key scholarly argument:** No army of 2+ million could have been fed or supplied on 5th-century roads, particularly through the narrow passes of northern Greece. Modern historians use carrying capacity of roads, river crossings, and food supply calculations. The 120,000–300,000 range is the standard modern position. One outlier (Christos Romas) argues ~400,000.

**Cinematic angle for the video:** Herodotus actually does the math on his 2.6 million — he counts by water supply and camp space. He's not just making up a round number. He's getting the wrong answer very carefully. That's worth showing on screen.

## SECTION 3: THE PERSIAN SILENCE

### What Persian Sources Survive from Xerxes's Era

**Inscriptions that survive:**
- The **Daiva Inscription** (XPh) — Xerxes's religious proclamation declaring the destruction of a "daiva-house" and ordering worship only of Ahura Mazda. Written in Old Persian, Elamite, and Akkadian. Contains no mention of Thermopylae or the Greek campaign.
- The **Persepolis Fortification Tablets** — administrative records (food rations, labor payments). Not historical narratives.
- Various royal building inscriptions from Persepolis and Susa.

**What they do NOT contain:**
- Any narrative account of the Greek campaigns.
- Any mention of Thermopylae, Salamis, or Plataea.
- Any Persian explanation for why the campaign ended.

**Why the silence?**
This is a critical historiographical point for the video. Achaemenid royal inscriptions were structured around victories and divine favor — they recorded the king's conquests, the countries he ruled, and the favor of Ahura Mazda. They did not record defeats. The silence is not a coincidence or an archival loss — it is the standard Persian royal inscriptional practice. Defeats were simply not written down in the official record.

The scholar community studying this (recent Oxford University Press volume *Persia's Greek Campaigns: Kingship, War, and Spectacle on the Achaemenid Frontier*) argues that Xerxes probably framed the Greek campaign differently internally — emphasizing the destruction of Athens as a victory, the conquest of most of mainland Greece, and the retreat as orderly rather than a defeat. The Persian Royal archives recorded the King's glory, not his setbacks.

**The Daiva Inscription specifically:**
The standard scholarly debate about this inscription concerns whether the "daiva-house" destroyed by Xerxes refers to Greek temples (possibly destroyed during the sack of Athens and Thermopylae-era campaigns) or to a heretical shrine elsewhere in the empire. Some scholars have argued this is Xerxes recording, obliquely, the destruction of Greek sacred sites — which would be the only Persian "mention" of the Greek campaigns. This interpretation is contested. Either way, it does not mention Thermopylae.

**The central point:** We have THREE Greek accounts of Thermopylae and ZERO Persian accounts. Every number we cite, every quote attributed to Persians, comes from Greek sources. We are, in effect, reading the winners' narrative — except in this case, the Greeks lost the battle (they just won the war). The sources we have are the accounts of the people who held the pass for three days and died. The other side left nothing.

## SECTION 5: MODERN SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS

### What Modern Historians Think Actually Happened

**Persian army size:**
- Range: 120,000–300,000 (mainstream scholarly consensus)
- Method: Logistical analysis — carrying capacity of roads, river crossings, food supply requirements per soldier per day, water sources along the route
- Outlier estimate: ~400,000 (Christos Romas)
- The ancient figures (2.6 million, 800,000) are universally rejected
- Even at 120,000, this was an overwhelmingly massive force relative to the Greek defenders

**Was the last stand strategic or suicidal?**
Modern scholarship has largely settled on the "strategic rearguard" interpretation:
- Leonidas dismissed the allied contingents so they could escape south
- The open ground beyond the pass would have allowed Persian cavalry to massacre retreating Greeks
- By holding the pass with a rearguard, Leonidas allowed 3,000+ men to escape
- Those men would fight at Salamis and Plataea
- This interpretation is supported by the fact that Leonidas selected only men with living sons for his Spartan contingent — suggesting he knew he would not return

The older "suicidal heroism" interpretation (obeying Spartan law against retreat, fulfilling the oracle's prophecy) is now seen as a secondary motivation layered onto a rational tactical decision.

**The Delphic Oracle:**
Herodotus records the oracle's prophecy to Sparta: either Sparta would be destroyed or a Spartan king would die. Modern scholars are divided on whether (a) Leonidas genuinely believed this prophecy and chose sacrifice, (b) the oracle was composed after the fact to make his death seem prophetic, or (c) both — he may have made the strategic decision to stay and then framed it in prophetic terms for his men's benefit.

**What modern archaeology confirms:**
- 1939 excavations by Spyridon Marinatos found large numbers of **Persian bronze arrowheads on Kolonos Hill** — confirming Herodotus's account of the final stand location (specifically overturning an earlier theory that the last stand was at a smaller hill nearer the wall)
- Core samples confirm the pass was only **100 metres wide** at the time — making Herodotus's account of the tactical situation plausible
- The shoreline has shifted significantly — the pass is now several kilometres inland from the sea, but in 480 BCE water came to the gates

**Was Thermopylae strategically significant?**
Two competing modern views:
1. **Yes, decisively** — the delay allowed Athens to evacuate its civilian population, allowed the Greek fleet to organize at Salamis, and transformed a potential rout into an organized withdrawal. The three days bought by the rearguard were essential preparation time.
2. **Marginally** — the Wikipedia article (citing unnamed scholars) notes "the time bought was negligible given the one-month gap to Salamis." The Battle of Salamis came a month later, which was time enough regardless.

The dominant view is #1, though serious historians acknowledge the counterfactual is hard to prove.

**The political appropriation question:**
The film has been explicitly embraced by far-right and white nationalist movements in the US and Europe, used to frame the Greco-Persian Wars as a racial conflict between "Western civilization" and a dark-skinned Eastern horde. Historians have pushed back on this reading: ancient Greeks and Persians did not organize their self-understanding around race; the conflict was imperial (Achaemenid expansion) versus political autonomy (Greek city-states). The framing of Thermopylae as a proto-European defense against Islam or non-white peoples is a modern projection with no basis in ancient sources.

The further irony: Sparta was a slave society where the state decided who was armed and when. The use of "molon labe" by US gun rights activists appropriates a quote from a culture where individual weapon ownership had no political meaning.

## SECTION 7: LESSER-KNOWN DETAILS (HIGH VALUE FOR THE VIDEO)

### 7A. Aristodemus — The Man Who Survived (and Was Never Forgiven)

Two Spartans were sent home before the final stand due to severe eye disease: Aristodemus and Eurytus. Eurytus demanded his helot guide lead him back to the pass blind, entered the battle, and died. Aristodemus returned to Sparta. He was treated as a social pariah — "no Spartan would give him fire, nor speak with him." He was called "Aristodemus the Trembler."

At the Battle of Plataea the following year (479 BCE), Aristodemus charged suicidally into the Persian lines and died fighting with extraordinary valor. The Spartans did not award him honors. The reason, per Herodotus: he had clearly been trying to die to atone for his shame, and fighting while wanting to die is less honorable than fighting while wanting to live.

This is a remarkable detail about the Spartan honor culture that the movie completely ignores — and it comes from Herodotus himself.

### 7B. The 700 Thespians — The Forgotten Dead

The city of Thespiae was in Boeotia, directly in the Persian line of advance. If Xerxes won, Thespiae would be destroyed (and it was — the Persians burned it after Thermopylae). The Thespians had no escape option regardless. Their 700 hoplites represented nearly their entire military force. They fought and died to the last man.

In 1997, the Greek government unveiled a monument to the Thespians at Thermopylae. It stands next to the Spartan monument. The Spartan monument has been there for centuries. The Thespian monument is less than 30 years old.

A 2025 academic study examined why Herodotus himself — the primary source — diminished the Thespians: when listing the bravest warriors at Thermopylae, he names several Spartans by name but among the 700 Thespians names only one: Dithyrambus son of Harmatides.

**For the video:** The bias toward the "300" is not just a modern movie problem. It is baked into Herodotus's own text. That is a remarkable finding to show on screen.

### 7C. The Thebans — Traitors or Hostages?

400 Thebans were at the final stand. Herodotus says Leonidas held them as unwilling hostages because he suspected Theban medism (sympathy with Persia). He says they eventually surrendered to the Persians with hands raised, and a few were killed before the Persians accepted the surrender.

However, Plutarch later defended Theban honor, and modern historians note that Herodotus was writing at a time when Athens and Thebes were political rivals, and he may have had reasons to portray the Thebans unfavorably. The Theban contingent having medizing sympathies is historically plausible (Thebes did later collaborate with Persia in the wars), but the specific narrative of forced hostages vs. choosing to stay and fight is contested.

This is a concrete example of source bias that the video can demonstrate on screen: Herodotus had political reasons to portray Thebes badly. We have only his word for what happened.

### 7D. The Oracle and Leonidas's Selection of His Men

Herodotus records that Leonidas, before marching, consulted the Oracle at Delphi and received a prophecy that either Sparta would be destroyed or a Spartan king of Heraclid lineage would die. He reportedly selected for his 300 only men who had living sons, so that no Spartan family line would be extinguished. This detail — if accurate — suggests he knew he would not return and was making deliberate preparations. It reframes the "suicidal last stand" as a calculated choice.

The prophecy text as Herodotus records it:
> "For you, inhabitants of wide-wayed Sparta, either your great and glorious city must be wasted by Persian men, or if not that, then the bound of Lacedaemon must mourn a dead king from Heracles' line."

**For Phase 2 verification:** This is a case where the oracle text in Herodotus may have been composed after the fact to make events seem prophetic. Ancient oracles were often "remembered" in hexameter verse after they proved accurate. Scholars have examined whether the Delphic oracle texts in Herodotus are genuine pre-battle prophecies or post-hoc literary constructions.

### 7E. The Serpent Column

After the Greek victory at Plataea (479 BCE), the Greeks dedicated the **Serpent Column** at Delphi — a bronze column of three intertwined snakes listing the 31 Greek city-states that fought against Persia. It is one of the few surviving physical artifacts from the wars. Moved by Constantine to Constantinople (Istanbul), where it still stands in the Hippodrome. The column is sometimes cited as evidence supporting some of Herodotus's specific reports about which city-states participated.

### 7F. What Happened After Thermopylae

The strategic aftermath is often glossed over in pop culture accounts:
- **Immediately after:** Xerxes sacked Athens. Found it empty — Athenians had evacuated (allegedly aided by Themistocles's reading of the oracle's "wooden walls" prophecy as referring to the fleet).
- **Battle of Salamis (480 BCE, ~1 month later):** Greek fleet destroyed the Persian fleet in a narrow strait near Athens, making Xerxes's supply lines untenable.
- **Xerxes's withdrawal:** Xerxes retreated north with much of his army, leaving Mardonius in command.
- **Battle of Plataea (479 BCE):** Mardonius's land force was destroyed. Aristodemus (the Trembler) died here.
- **End of the Persian invasions:** Persia never attempted to conquer mainland Greece again.

The three days at Thermopylae are thus connected in a direct causal chain to the survival of Greek city-state independence — and, by later historians' argument, the development of Athenian democracy in the following decades.
