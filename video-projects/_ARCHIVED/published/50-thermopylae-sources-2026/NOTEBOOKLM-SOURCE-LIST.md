### [P2] Diodorus Siculus, Library of History, Book 11 (chapters 1-13)

Second most important ancient account. Diodorus wrote ~400 years after the battle but used now-lost sources (especially Ephorus). His account differs from Herodotus in key details — useful for cross-checking.

| Option | URL | Format | Notes |
|--------|-----|--------|-------|
| **LacusCurtius (RECOMMENDED)** | https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Diodorus_Siculus/11A*.html | HTML | Loeb Classical Library translation (1946, C.H. Oldfather). Covers chapters 1-19. The Thermopylae battle is chapters 5-11. **Upload as URL to NotebookLM.** |

**Note:** Perseus Digital Library does NOT have Diodorus Book 11 (only Books 1-5 and 18-20). LacusCurtius is the only free English option online.

### [P3] Plutarch, Moralia — "Sayings of the Spartans" (Apophthegmata Laconica)

Contains the famous Thermopylae quotes attributed to Leonidas: "Come and take them" (Molon labe), the shade/arrows quip, and 13 other sayings. Essential for the "myth vs. history" angle — these sayings shaped the legend.

| Option | URL | Format | Notes |
|--------|-----|--------|-------|
| **LacusCurtius (RECOMMENDED)** | https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/Sayings_of_Spartans*/main.html | HTML | Loeb Classical Library translation (Vol. III, 1931). Public domain. Leonidas section on pages 347-351, 15 recorded sayings. **Upload as URL to NotebookLM.** |

### [P4] Plutarch, Life of Themistocles

Themistocles was the Athenian commander at Artemisium (fought simultaneously with Thermopylae). Provides the Athenian perspective and strategic context — why Thermopylae was chosen, what happened after.

### [P5] Strabo, Geography, Book 9, Chapter 4 — Thermopylae Pass Geography

Ancient geographical description of the actual pass. Describes the terrain, hot springs ("Thermopylae" = "hot gates"), fortifications, and the memorial/epitaph inscriptions. Essential for understanding WHY this location was chosen for the stand.

| Option | URL | Format | Notes |
|--------|-----|--------|-------|
| **LacusCurtius** | https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/9D*.html | HTML | Loeb Classical Library translation. Describes the pass, hot waters sacred to Heracles, the narrow terrain, and the polyandrium (mass grave) with Simonides' inscription. **Upload as URL to NotebookLM.** |

### [P6] Ctesias, Persica (fragments) — The Persian-Side Account

Ctesias was a Greek physician at the Persian court (~400 BC). His Persica offered an alternative to Herodotus — generally considered less reliable but valuable as the only account with access to Persian oral tradition. The original is lost; we have Photius's 9th-century epitome and scattered fragments.

| Option | URL | Format | Notes |
|--------|-----|--------|-------|
| Wikipedia summary (best starting point) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ctesias | HTML | Good overview of his works, reliability debates, and what survives. Notes that "his unreliability makes Herodotus seem a model of accuracy." **Upload as URL.** |

**Note on availability:** Full English translations of Ctesias fragments are harder to find free online. The attalus.org fragment collection (previously a go-to) returned 404 errors during verification. The scholarly standard is Lloyd Llewellyn-Jones & James Robson, *Ctesias' History of Persia: Tales of the Orient* (Routledge, 2010) — this is a PAID source ($40-50). For NotebookLM purposes, the Wikipedia article plus Herodotus's own mentions of alternative traditions may suffice for a video-level treatment. If you need deeper Ctesias coverage, purchase the Llewellyn-Jones translation.

### [P7] Simonides' Thermopylae Epitaph

The most famous inscription from the ancient world: "Go tell the Spartans, stranger passing by, that here obedient to their laws we lie." This shaped 2,500 years of Thermopylae mythology.

| Option | URL | Format | Notes |
|--------|-----|--------|-------|
| Wikipedia — Simonides of Ceos | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simonides_of_Ceos | HTML | Contains the Greek text and F.L. Lucas translation. Notes that modern scholars question whether even this epitaph is authentically Simonides. Upload as URL. |
| The epitaph in Herodotus | https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0126%3Abook%3D7%3Achapter%3D228 | HTML | Herodotus 7.228 — the earliest source recording the inscription. Already covered in [P1] but bookmark this chapter specifically. |

**The Greek original:**
> Ὦ ξεῖν', ἀγγέλλειν Λακεδαιμονίοις ὅτι τῇδε κείμεθα, τοῖς κείνων ῥήμασι πειθόμενοι.

**F.L. Lucas translation:** "Tell them in Lacedaemon, passer-by / That here, obedient to their word, we lie."

### [S3] Recommended PAID Academic Sources (buy if budget allows)

These are the gold-standard modern scholarly works. Not free, but worth the investment for video-level depth.

| Book | Author | Publisher | Price | Why It Matters |
|------|--------|-----------|-------|----------------|
| *Thermopylae: The Battle That Changed the World* | Paul Cartledge | Vintage/Overlook, 2006 | ~$15 | Cartledge is the A.G. Leventis Professor of Greek Culture at Cambridge. THE modern authority. Accessible but rigorous. |
| *The Battle of Thermopylae: A Campaign in Context* | Rupert Matthews | Spellmount, 2006 | ~$20 | Military history focus — terrain, tactics, logistics. |
| *Thermopylae: The Battle for the West* | Ernle Bradford | Open Road Media, 2004 (reprint) | ~$12 | Popular history but well-sourced narrative. |
| *The Greco-Persian Wars* | Peter Green | University of California Press, 1996 | ~$25 | Broader context. Green is a top classicist. Covers all the battles with excellent source criticism. |
| *Ctesias' History of Persia: Tales of the Orient* | Llewellyn-Jones & Robson | Routledge, 2010 | ~$45 | The standard English translation of Ctesias fragments. Only buy if pursuing the Persian-perspective angle. |
