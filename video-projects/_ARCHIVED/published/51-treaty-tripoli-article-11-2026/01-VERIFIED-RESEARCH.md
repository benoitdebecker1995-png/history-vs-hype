## CORE FACTS (forensic spine of the script)

### F1 — Article 11 verbatim ✅
> "As the government of the United States of America is not in any sense founded on the Christian Religion,—as it has in itself no character of enmity against the laws, religion or tranquility of Musselmen,—and as the said States never have entered into any war or act of hostility against any Mehomitan nation, it is declared by the parties that no pretext arising from religious opinions shall ever produce an interruption of the harmony existing between the two countries."

**Sources:** Avalon Project (explicit attribution to Hunter Miller, *Treaties and Other International Acts of the United States of America*, Vol. 2, GPO 1931); Spellberg p. [Adams's Treaty with Tripoli, 1797 section]; Meacham [section on Treaty of Peace and Friendship]. All three sources reproduce identical text.

### F2 — Treaty timeline ✅
- Signed at **Tripoli, November 4, 1796** (3 Ramada I, A.H. 1211) by Joel Barlow.
- Co-signed at **Algiers, January 3, 1797** (4 Rajab, A.H. 1211).
- **Submitted to Senate May 29, 1797** (Adams's message of May 26, 1797).
- **Senate resolution of advice and consent: June 7, 1797.**
- **Ratified by Adams: June 10, 1797.** Proclaimed same day.

**Source:** Avalon/Hunter Miller 1931 introduction to the treaty.

### F3 — The Senate vote ✅✅ — PRIMARY-SOURCE CONFIRMED (2026-04-24)
- **Wednesday, June 7, 1797.** Senate Executive Journal page 244.
- **Verbatim resolution:** "Resolved, (two-thirds of the Senators present concurring therein,) That the Senate do advise and consent to the ratification of the treaty of peace and friendship between the United States of America and the Bey and subjects of Tripoli, of Barbary."
- **Vote: Yeas 23.** No nays recorded for this specific vote.
- **23 named senators (Journal records by name):** Bingham, Bloodworth, Blount, Bradford, Brown, Cocke, Foster, Goodhue, Hillhouse, Howard, Langdon, Latimer, Laurance, Livermore, Martin, Paine, Read, Rutherfurd, Sedgwick, Stockton, Tattnall, Tichenor, Tracy.
- **Procedure recorded:** "Mr. Bloodworth, from the Committee to whom was referred the consideration of the treaty... made report, that it be adopted; and the report being amended, On the question to agree to the report as amended, It was determined in the affirmative."
- **Article 11 reference in the Journal: ZERO.** No mention, no debate, no motion. Confirmed by direct primary source.
- **The "amendment" mentioned** is to the committee's adoption-report — not to the treaty text. The Senate ratified the Barlow translation as transmitted.
- 12 senators absent (35 senators total in the 5th Congress; 23 voted yes; the Journal does not record nays for this vote).

**Source (PRIMARY):** *Journal of the Executive Proceedings of the Senate of the United States*, Vol. 1, page 244, June 7, 1797 (`uc1-c109288496-258-1777078898.pdf` in the notebook). Spellberg fn 47 (secondary, now superseded by direct citation).

> **Script note:** the Senate Journal language is "two-thirds of the Senators present concurring therein" — the constitutional treaty-ratification threshold. NOT "unanimous." Use "23 senators, none against." Or even more precisely: "On June 7th, 1797, the United States Senate voted 23 to nothing to ratify it. The Senate Journal records the names of all 23 — and never once mentions Article 11."

### F4 — Adams personally took responsibility, treaty published front-page ✅
- Adams ordered the treaty published with his proclamation prefix.
- Headline: **"Important State Papers."**
- Front-page in newspapers in: Pennsylvania, Connecticut, Rhode Island, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, New York.
- Adams's introductory note: he "had caused to be published and issued this Proclamation, commanding a strict observance of the following Treaty; regularly negotiated and ratified."
- **All newspapers published WITHOUT editorial commentary, except one.**

**Source:** Spellberg footnotes 48–49 (citing the 1797 newspaper print run).

### F5 — The single public objection: William Cobbett ✅
- William Cobbett, Federalist editor of *Porcupine Gazette*, Philadelphia, **June 23, 1797 edition.**
- Cobbett appended a paragraph to the printed treaty:
  > "The eleventh article of this treaty certainly wants some explanation. That 'the government of the United States of America is in no sense founded on the Christian religion,' is a declaration that one might have expected from Soliman Kaya, Hassan Bashaw, or the sansculotte Joel Barlow; but it sounds rather oddly from the President and the Senate. If it will admit of satisfactory explanation, it ought to receive it; for it certainly looks a little like trampling upon the cross."
- Cobbett (a Federalist) was politically constrained: he couldn't directly attack Adams (Federalist president) or the Federalist Senate, so he deflected blame onto "Muslim officials" or Barlow.

**Source:** Spellberg quoting Cobbett directly, with citation footnote 50.

> **Script gold:** Cobbett's "trampling upon the cross" line is a perfect period-voice quote. Use it.

### F6 — Joel Barlow: who he was, what he could and couldn't do ✅
- Diplomat-poet sent by Washington to settle Barbary affairs late in second term; the work was finished under Adams.
- **Barlow did not read Arabic.** None of his American colleagues did either.
- Barlow nonetheless signed a statement saying: **"The foregoing is a literal translation of the writing in Arabic on the opposite page. JOEL BARLOW"** — an attestation that, given his linguistic limits, he could not have personally verified.
- Barlow relied on European intermediaries to vet the Arabic. The Spanish consul **Gerardo de Souza** verified the Arabic seals (per Spellberg fn 69, citing Miller 2:378–79).
- **James Cathcart**, US consul at Tripoli (and earlier prisoner in Algiers for 11 years), "did not read Arabic, although he seems to have been familiar with Turkish and Italian" (Miller 2:382).
- Adams privately said of Barlow: there "was not a more worthless fellow" — comparing him unfavorably to Thomas Paine.
- Barlow's secular-deist views were consistent with Article 11's language; Spellberg concludes Barlow "likely was involved in crafting it."

**Source:** Spellberg pp. [Barlow section], fn 69-71; Avalon/Miller for the Barlow signature line.

### F7 — Hurgronje 1930 Annotated Translation ✅¹ (need page-level Miller verification)
- The forensic discovery of the Article 11 / Arabic discrepancy was made by **Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje of Leiden** — a Dutch scholar (one of the most famous Orientalists/Arabists of the era).
- Hurgronje was **hired by the State Department in 1930** to examine the Arabic original of the 1796 Tripoli treaty.
- His "Annotated Translation of 1930" appears on **pages 368–379** of Hunter Miller's 1931 Volume 2.
- Hunter Miller is the **editor**; Miller's preface (page vi) explicitly thanks "Professor C. Snouck Hurgronje and Dr. J. H. Kramers, of Leiden" for the editorial treatment of the Barbary treaties.
- **Story shift:** the headline in older popular accounts is "Hunter Miller discovered..." — more accurate is **"a Dutch Arabist named Snouck Hurgronje discovered, the State Department published in 1931."** Hurgronje was an internationally known scholar; this is not a footnote-level player.

**Source:** Spellberg fn 71; Miller Vol. 2 preface (page vi, in uploaded PDF).

### F8 — What Hurgronje and Miller actually wrote ✅ — VERBATIM VERIFIED

**Hurgronje, "The Annotated Translation of 1930," in Miller Vol. 2, p. 371:**
> "The eleventh article of the Barlow translation has no equivalent whatever in the Arabic. The Arabic text opposite that article is a letter from Hassan Pasha of Algiers to Yussuf Pasha of Tripoli."

**Hurgronje on the letter's content (same page, p. 371) — extraordinary period-voice passage:**
> "The letter gives notice of the treaty of peace concluded with the Americans and recommends its observation. Three fourths of the letter consists of an introduction, drawn up by a stupid secretary who just knew a certain number of bombastic words and expressions occurring in solemn documents, but entirely failed to catch their real meaning. Here the only thing to be done by a translator is to try to give the reader an impression of the nonsensical original."

**Miller, editor's note, p. 384:**
> "Most extraordinary (and wholly unexplained) is the fact that Article 11 of the Barlow translation, with its famous phrase, 'the government of the United States of America is not in any sense founded on the Christian Religion,' does not exist at all. There is no Article 11."

**Miller on what IS in the Arabic where Article 11 should be, p. 384:**
> "...in form a letter, crude and flamboyant and withal quite unimportant, from the Dey of Algiers to the Pasha of Tripoli."

**Miller's two-mystery framing, p. 384 — the closing-synthesis material:**
> "How that script came to be written and to be regarded, as in the Barlow translation, as Article 11 of the treaty as there written, is a mystery and seemingly must remain so. Nothing in the diplomatic correspondence of the time throws any light whatever on the point."
> "A further and perhaps equal mystery is the fact that since 1797 the Barlow translation has been trustfully and universally accepted as the just equivalent of the Arabic."

**Naming clarification:**
- Pasha of Tripoli = **Yussuf Pasha** (in Barlow's transliteration: Jussuf Bashaw)
- Dey of Algiers = **Hassan Pasha** (in Barlow's transliteration: Hassan Bashaw)
- Letter is **undated** in the manuscript (Hurgronje notes another similar letter at the very end of the treaty IS dated, but the in-place-of-Article-11 letter has no date).

**Source:** `tripoli miller.pdf` (Hunter Miller, *Treaties and Other International Acts...*, Vol. 2, GPO 1931, pp. 371 + 384). Direct verification, no intermediary.

### F9 — Why no scholarly consensus on the discrepancy ✅ — DIRECT VERIFICATION
- **Miller himself called it "wholly unexplained" in 1931** (verbatim quote in F8 above). His exact phrasing: "a mystery and seemingly must remain so."
- Miller noted that **"nothing in the diplomatic correspondence of the time throws any light whatever on the point"** (p. 384) — meaning the State Department's own archival research found nothing.
- Hurgronje did not propose an explanation in his 1930 annotated translation.
- **Sam Haselby**, *The Guardian*, January 3, 2011: titled the puzzle *"The Enigma of America's Secular Roots: Joel Barlow's Disavowal of Christianity as the Basis for US Government in the 1797 Treaty of Tripoli Is a Mystery."*
- Spellberg notes Article 11's content was consistent with Barlow's own views; she stops short of saying Barlow inserted it himself, but "likely was involved in crafting it."
- No scholar in the uploaded sources has resolved the mystery. The discrepancy stands as Miller left it: extraordinary, unexplained.

**Source:** Hunter Miller 1931 Vol. 2, p. 384 (direct); Spellberg fn 57; Haselby (Guardian, 2011).

### F10 — The 1805 superseding treaty ✅
- **1805 Treaty of Tripoli, Article 14**, signed June 4, 1805 in Tripoli; ratified by Senate **April 17, 1806**, vote **21-8** (NOT unanimous).
- Article 14 **OMITS** "the government of the United States of America is not in any sense founded on the Christian Religion" — the central phrase from Article 11.
- Article 14 **RETAINS**: "As the Government of the United States of America has in itself no character of enmity against the Laws, Religion or Tranquility of Musselmen..."
- Article 14 **ADDS** a justification for the recent war: "...except in the defence of their just rights to freely navigate the High Seas." (1805 followed the First Barbary War.)
- Article 14 **ADDS** a religious-freedom-for-consuls clause: "the Consuls and Agents of both Nations respectively, shall have liberty to exercise his Religion in his own house..."
- **Critical contrast:** the 1805 Arabic text DID match the English (unlike 1797's Barlow translation).
- Spellberg's hypothesis on why Jefferson dropped the secular-founding line: 1800 election attacks on Jefferson's Christianity may have made him politically reticent.

**Source:** Spellberg pp. [Jefferson's Tripoli treaty section], fn 105–112.

### F11 — 1797 federal-vs-state legal context (mitigation prep) ✅
- **Federal level was secular by design:**
  - Constitution Article VI, Section 3: explicit prohibition of religious test for federal office (1788).
  - First Amendment: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion" (1791).
- **State level was NOT secular in 1797:**
  - **North Carolina** state constitution (Article 32): officeholders had to swear to "the truth of the Protestant religion."
  - **Connecticut**: citizens were taxed to support the established Congregational church; non-Congregationalists had to obtain government exemption certificates.
  - **Massachusetts/New Hampshire**: Congregational establishments persisted into the 19th century.
  - **South Carolina**: Protestantism was the "established religion."
  - **Georgia**: representatives "shall be of the Protestant religion."
- **Madison's later reflection:** by insisting the US was not a Christian state, the president and Congress "distanced themselves from European Christian powers that had a long history of religion-inspired animosity toward Muslims" (Lambert citing Madison fn 24).
- **Lambert's framing:** America was a "pluralistic society comprising scores of voluntary sects" united in wanting free exercise — not a Christian state. The "starting point was the recent religious settlement reached in the U.S. Constitution: the prohibition of a state church and the guarantee of free religious exercise to all."

**Source:** Spellberg (NC, Connecticut, religious-test debates); Lambert (Adams/Jefferson framing).

> **Script use:** the cleanest defensible framing — "in 1797, the American people were largely Protestant Christians, and several states still had religious establishments. But the federal government, by Article VI and the First Amendment, was already legally secular. Article 11 was a foreign-policy articulation of that federal reality. That's why the Senate ratified it without debate."

### F12 — Modern secular reading is a 20th-century construction (Crane 2020) ✅✅ — VERBATIM VERIFIED via NLM
- **Jacob Crane (2020), "Reading American Secularism in the 1797 Treaty of Tripoli,"** *American Quarterly* 72(2):403–422 (Project MUSE article 758950, Johns Hopkins University Press). PDF in notebook as `crane2020.pdf`.

**Verbatim quote 1 — the rereading thesis (p. 405):**
> "this now-widespread interpretation of Article 11 developed through a long series of rediscoveries and rereadings of the treaty as it was applied to a range of domestic political questions far removed from its original diplomatic context, from the public funding of Christian clergy to regulations prohibiting the sale of alcohol on Sundays."

**Verbatim quote 2 — the bipartisan-weaponization observation (p. 405):**
> "the language of Article 11 was wielded as often by Christian nationalists as by militant secularists."

**Verbatim quote 3 — the Tripoli-as-figure thesis (p. 406):**
> "the history of Article 11 of the Treaty of Tripoli demonstrates how Christian nationalists and emergent secularists alike used the figure of Tripoli to articulate a national character in dialogue with, and in opposition to, the perceived otherness of the Muslim world."

**Source:** Jacob Crane, "Reading American Secularism in the 1797 Treaty of Tripoli," *American Quarterly* 72:2 (2020), pp. 403–422 — verified verbatim in NotebookLM.

> **Script use — this is the closing-beat upgrade.** Instead of ending only on Miller's "wholly unexplained," Crane gives the script a stronger and more honest closing. The modern Christian-nation vs. secular-founding fight is not a fight about what the 1797 Senate self-evidently meant — it's a 19th–20th century overlay. The 1797 document was, in Crane's framing, *largely uncontroversial when ratified.* It became politically combustible later, through rereadings.

### F13 — The second contemporary reaction: Timothy Dwight (Haselby 2015) ✅✅ — VERBATIM VERIFIED via NLM — NEW
- **Sam Haselby (2015), *The Origins of American Religious Nationalism*** (Oxford University Press), p. 109.
- **The forensic discovery:** beyond William Cobbett's *Porcupine Gazette* objection, there is at least one other documented reaction from a high-status American — **Timothy Dwight, president of Yale College, theologian, and Joel Barlow's former mentor at Yale.**

**Verbatim quote (p. 109):**
> "At the end of 1795, James Monroe, the American ambassador to France, sent Barlow to Algiers, as consul, in order to negotiate a treaty with the Barbary states. Proving himself an adept diplomat, Barlow secured the release of American prisoners and produced the Treaty of Tripoli. Article 11 included the historic statement, 'the Government of the United States of America is not, in any sense, founded on the Christian religion.' President John Adams signed the treaty and sent it to the US Senate, where, in June 1797, it passed unanimously. **For Dwight, it was all too much. He made his own translation of the Psalms to replace that of his wayward protégé and, with ceremony, removed portraits of Barlow from Yale.**"

**What this means for the script:**
- This is a **second public reaction** beyond Cobbett — and a more dramatic one. The president of Yale ceremonially removed his former student's portrait over Article 11.
- It corroborates the picture we already have: the contemporary objections existed but were narrow, personal, and from religious-conservative figures. NOT a national debate.
- It also gives the script a perfect dramatic visual: a portrait coming off a wall.
- Dwight is also useful as a steelman: he WAS a serious theologian, and his reaction is the strongest contemporary "this is wrong" gesture from someone who actually mattered intellectually.

**Source:** Sam Haselby, *The Origins of American Religious Nationalism* (Oxford University Press, 2015), p. 109. Verified verbatim in NotebookLM.

> **Script use:** this is a structural addition. Beat 4 or Beat 5 should now include "and Timothy Dwight, president of Yale, took down Barlow's portrait." Visual: a Joel Barlow portrait, then it goes dark/empty. Cobbett's "trampling upon the cross" still works as the public-press reaction; Dwight's portrait removal is the private-elite reaction.
