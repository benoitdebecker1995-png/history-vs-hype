## HOOK (0:00-0:45)

[TALKING HEAD]

In 2023, a group of economists drew a line through Brazil. On one side of that line, towns had an average of 2,628 enslaved people in 1872. On the other side, 1,184. Same country. Same census. Same year. Different empire.

[B-ROLL: Laudares regression discontinuity graph]

Most people know that Brazil speaks Portuguese and the rest of South America speaks Spanish. If you ask why, you'll get something vague about colonization. The Pope drew a line. Portugal got the east. Done.

That answer is incomplete. Because the line wasn't about land. It was about wind.

So I read the economists' paper. They tested the actual treaty meridian, a line drawn in 1494, against modern income data. And the inequality is still there. 0.04 Gini points higher on one side. In 2010. 530 years later.

[TALKING HEAD]

Two countries split the world in half. The line they drew is still measurable, in language, in borders, in income data. Here's what actually happened. It starts with a wind pattern. And it ends with data you can still measure today.

## ACT 1: THE LINE (0:45-2:45)

[B-ROLL: Modern language map of South America — Spanish vs Portuguese]

Open a language map of South America. Every country speaks Spanish except one. Brazil. And the border between Spanish-speaking and Portuguese-speaking South America follows an almost perfect north-south line.

That line isn't natural. It isn't a mountain range or a river. It's a treaty. Signed on June 7th, 1494, in the small Spanish town of Tordesillas.

[B-ROLL: Treaty of Tordesillas text — UNESCO Memory of the World]

The treaty states that the boundary shall be drawn straight from pole to pole at a distance of 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands. Everything west of that line belonged to Spain. Everything east belonged to Portugal.

[B-ROLL: Animate Inter caetera line (100 leagues) shifting to Tordesillas line (370 leagues)]

But this wasn't the first line. A year earlier, Pope Alexander the Sixth, a Spaniard named Rodrigo Borgia, had drawn a line 100 leagues west of the Azores. That line gave Portugal almost nothing. The Portuguese rejected it. And they had the naval power to back up their objection.

[TALKING HEAD]

But the part usually left out of the story is this. The reason Portugal demanded the line move from 100 to 370 leagues wasn't a secret land grab for Brazil. Brazil hadn't been discovered yet. It was about wind.

[B-ROLL: Map showing Volta do Mar arc through mid-Atlantic]

Portuguese mariners had developed a navigational technique called the Volta do Mar, the turn of the sea. To return home from the African coast, they couldn't just sail north. The winds and currents pushed against them. So they swung their ships far west into the open Atlantic, sometimes for weeks out of sight of land, until they caught the westerly winds at higher latitudes. Those winds carried them in a sweeping arc back to Lisbon.

At 100 leagues, that arc would have put Portuguese ships in Spanish waters during routine navigation. The entire Portuguese maritime economy would have been legally choked off by a line on a map.

So 370 leagues was the compromise. It gave Portugal the airspace to execute the Volta do Mar. And it accidentally sliced through the easternmost bulge of South America.

[TALKING HEAD]

The fate of a continent was dictated by the need to catch a specific wind off the coast of Africa.

But there's a problem. And it's a big one.

[B-ROLL: Astrolabe illustration, declination tables]

In 1494, Portuguese mariners could measure latitude precisely. They used the mariner's astrolabe to measure the angle of the sun, then cross-referenced those readings with declination tables prepared by the Jewish astronomer Abraham Zacuto.

But the Tordesillas line is a north-south line. It separates east from west. That's a boundary of longitude. And longitude was impossible to measure in 1494. You need a clock that keeps perfect time for months on a pitching deck. That clock wouldn't exist until John Harrison built his marine chronometer nearly 250 years later.

Patricia Seed, in Ceremonies of Possession, captures what this means.

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen — "No physical points determined when you turn to head off in a different direction, only a mathematical calculation" — Seed, p.141]

They partitioned the physical earth based on a coordinate they couldn't actually reach. And they knew it. The treaty mandated a joint scientific survey, two to four caravels, to meet at the Grand Canary Island within ten months and physically find the line.

It never happened. Both sides quietly abandoned it because it was a fool's errand.

## ACT 2: THE MACHINE (2:45-6:15)

[TALKING HEAD]

So you have an unmeasurable, unverifiable line drawn by men who couldn't find it on a map. And somehow that line created a functioning colonial empire. The question isn't what happened. It's how did the machine actually work.

### The Dual Monopoly (2:45-3:45)

[B-ROLL: Romanus Pontifex text — "invade, search out, capture, vanquish, and subdue all Saracens and pagans... reduce their persons to perpetual slavery" — Boxer p.21]

The answer starts 39 years before Tordesillas. In 1455, Pope Nicholas the Fifth issued a papal bull called Romanus Pontifex. The language is not subtle. It authorized the Portuguese crown to, and I'm reading directly, "invade, search out, capture, vanquish, and subdue all Saracens and pagans, and reduce their persons to perpetual slavery."

In economic terms, this document granted Portugal a legally recognized monopoly over the entire African coast. No other European nation could trade, settle, or even navigate south of Cape Bojador without Portuguese permission.

[TALKING HEAD]

Now combine that with Tordesillas. Romanus Pontifex gave Portugal the eastern shore of the Atlantic, Africa. Tordesillas gave them the western shore, Brazil. They controlled both ends of the supply chain. The source of enslaved labor and the destination.

[B-ROLL: Map showing dual monopoly — Africa to Brazil closed loop]

Spain couldn't access Africa directly. They had to use a licensing system called the asiento, a monopoly contract where a contractor, usually Portuguese, paid the Spanish crown a lump sum for the right to supply a specific number of enslaved people per year. Portugal traded African labor for Spanish American silver.

It was a closed loop. And it was legally airtight.

### The Franchise Model (3:45-5:00)

[TALKING HEAD]

But having the legal rights on parchment in Lisbon is one thing. Actually occupying a massive, largely unmapped territory thousands of miles across the ocean is another. Portugal didn't have the manpower or the treasury to do it themselves.

So they franchised it.

[B-ROLL: Map showing 15 captaincy strips along Brazilian coast]

King Joao the Third divided the Brazilian coastline into 15 horizontal strips, each extending from the coast to the theoretical Tordesillas line inland. He awarded these captaincies to 12 private lords called donatarios, mostly lesser nobility and military veterans, who had to colonize them at their own expense.

The whole system ran on two legal documents, both drafted by the letrados, the university-trained lawyers who were the administrative backbone of the Portuguese empire.

First, the Carta de Doacao, the deed. It conveyed the territorial rights and physical limits of the captaincy.

Second, the Foral, the operational charter. This laid out the exact tax rates the lord owed the crown, the royal monopolies the crown retained, specifically brazilwood and spices, the judicial powers the donatario held over settlers, and even who could issue the death penalty.

[TALKING HEAD]

The genius of this system, from the crown's perspective, was that it shifted 100 percent of the capital expenditure and risk onto private contractors. If a captaincy failed, the crown lost nothing. If it succeeded, the crown got a guaranteed stream of tax revenue from sugar production.

Most failed. Out of 15, only two captaincies actually worked, Pernambuco and Sao Vicente. The rest went bankrupt, were abandoned, or were destroyed by indigenous resistance.

[B-ROLL: Portrait/illustration of Duarte Coelho]

One of the two successes was a man named Duarte Coelho. He'd served in the Portuguese maritime empire in Asia, married into a wealthy family, then liquidated his personal capital to fund the captaincy of Pernambuco. He paid for ships, weapons, supplies, and the expensive machinery to build sugar mills. He went all in.

And before those captaincies existed, the Portuguese hadn't even tried to settle Brazil. They'd used the same low-footprint model they used in Africa, small coastal trading posts called feitorias, basically fortified warehouses for loading brazilwood. It was only when French ships started brazenly ignoring the Tordesillas line and logging dye wood themselves that the crown panicked. They needed boots on the ground to protect their claim. The French threat is what actually forced the pivot to settlement.

### The Cargo (5:00-5:45)

[TALKING HEAD]

But the transition from coastal trading posts to massive sugar plantations required labor on an astronomical scale. And this is where the dual monopoly becomes operational.

[B-ROLL: Quote on screen — "The ship below deck is laden with brazilwood and above deck is full of purchased young boys and girls which cost the Portuguese little since most of them were offered voluntarily" — German merchant, 1514, in Schwartz p.12]

This is from a German newsletter dated 1514. We are entirely within the first decade of serious Portuguese activity on the Brazilian coast. Brazilwood below deck. Human beings above.

The transatlantic slave trade wasn't a later adaptation. It was operational within fourteen years of Cabral's landing. The dual monopoly, Africa on one side, Brazil on the other, was functioning as a closed loop within years of the Portuguese arriving on the coast.

### They Did It Twice (5:45-6:15)

[B-ROLL: Full globe showing BOTH Tordesillas and Zaragoza lines]

And they didn't stop at one line. In 1529, the Treaty of Zaragoza extended the division to the Pacific. The entire world was now split between two countries. Spain then colonized the Philippines, which were strictly speaking on the Portuguese side of the line. They violated their own treaty.

## ACT 3: THE GHOST IN THE DATA (6:15-8:00)

[TALKING HEAD]

So we've talked about wind patterns, unmeasurable lines, venture capital captaincies, and the logistics of human cargo. And you might be asking, so what? Why does a mathematical ghost from 1494 matter today?

It matters because the line didn't fade away. It's still in the data.

Now, someone might reasonably argue that Tordesillas was an unenforceable fantasy. Historians admit there was "no means of determining precisely where the line ran." And Portuguese settlers comprehensively breached the agreement for centuries. Fair point. But watch what happens when you test the data.

[B-ROLL: Laudares regression discontinuity graph — zoomed, annotated]

This is the study I mentioned at the beginning. Humberto Laudares mapped the modern coordinates of where the theoretical Tordesillas line would cross modern Brazil. Then he compared the economic data of municipalities on each side.

The method is clever. He excluded towns sitting right on the line, where historical jurisdictions get muddy, and only compared towns firmly on one side to towns firmly on the other. Same geography, same rainfall, same soil. The only difference was which side of the 1494 line they fell on.

[TALKING HEAD]

And the numbers are stark. On the Portuguese side, an average of 2,628 enslaved individuals per municipality. On the Spanish side, 1,184. More than double. Entirely determined by which side of a 15th-century mathematical calculation the land happened to fall on.

Because Portugal held both shores of the Atlantic, they had a massive comparative advantage in the slave trade. The Portuguese side became structurally reliant on plantation slavery in a way the Spanish side in that region did not.

And that extreme historical reliance on enslaved labor created deep systemic inequalities in land ownership, capital accumulation, and political power that never vanished when slavery was abolished. Those structures ossified. They became permanent.

[B-ROLL: Graph showing 0.04 Gini point difference at 48.7 degrees west]

Today, in 2010, municipalities on the historical Portuguese side show a difference of 0.04 Gini points. To put that in perspective, that's roughly ten percent of Brazil's entire national inequality score. A localized difference. Created by a line. Drawn by men who couldn't find it.

At 99 percent confidence. 260 years after the treaty was formally scrapped.

## ACT 4: THE AFTERLIFE (8:00-9:15)

[TALKING HEAD]

In March 2023, Pope Francis officially repudiated the Doctrine of Discovery. He formally renounced the papal bulls we've been discussing, Romanus Pontifex and Inter caetera. It was widely celebrated as a historic reckoning.

And it changes absolutely nothing in international law.

[B-ROLL: Vatican 2023 statement on screen — "The Catholic Church therefore repudiates those concepts that fail to recognize the inherent human rights of indigenous peoples"]

The papal bulls were the starting gun. They provided the initial justification when the empires were weak and needed the Vatican's blessing. But the legal framework outgrew the Pope centuries ago.

Think of it this way. The papal bulls were a wooden mold. The concrete poured into that mold was secular international law, bilateral treaties, court precedents, property registries. Once the concrete cured, they knocked the mold away. The Vatican saying "we repudiate the mold" in 2023 doesn't touch the skyscraper built on top of it.

[TALKING HEAD]

The Portuguese themselves proved this in 1750. Their bandeirantes, frontier explorers and slavers, had pushed thousands of kilometers past the Tordesillas line into the Amazon basin. So a Brazilian-born diplomat named Alexandre de Gusmao negotiated the Treaty of Madrid. He used a secular Roman law principle called uti possidetis, as you possess, you shall possess. Physical occupation replaced papal authority. The operating system had upgraded itself to survive without the church.

[B-ROLL: Map showing China's nine-dash line in the South China Sea]

And that operating system is still running. Legal scholar Christopher Rossi calls it the Treaty of Tordesillas Syndrome. When modern superpowers face a vast, essentially unoccupable space like the deep ocean, their instinct isn't to share the global commons. It's to draw arbitrary, historically dubious lines to claim what can't actually be fenced in.

China's nine-dash line across the South China Sea, claiming jurisdiction over five trillion dollars in annual commercial activity and one-third of all global maritime traffic, is the same mechanical instinct as drawing a line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands.

In 2016, the Hague Tribunal ruled against China under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. China dismissed the ruling and continued building artificial islands. And right now, ASEAN and China are negotiating a Code of Conduct for the South China Sea, with a deadline of July 2026. They're still trying to agree on where the line goes.

Same operating system. Same instinct. Different century.

And it isn't just about oceans. In December 2025, Brazil's Senate passed a constitutional amendment requiring indigenous peoples to prove they occupied their land as of October 5th, 1988, to have it legally recognized. The Doctrine of Discovery was formally repudiated. The legal structures it built are still producing new consequences.

## CLOSE (9:15-10:15)

[TALKING HEAD]

If you see the pattern, you understand something bigger.

This isn't about Portugal and Spain. It isn't about one treaty signed in a small Castilian town.

It's about what happens when legal fictions become real borders. When a line that nobody can measure creates inequality that everybody can.

In 1494, two countries drew a line through a world they'd never mapped. In 1884, fourteen countries did the same thing to Africa at the Berlin Conference. The borders they drew are still there. You can see them on a language map. You can measure them in income data.

[B-ROLL: Side-by-side — Tordesillas line on South America, Berlin Conference lines on Africa]

And right now, humanity is preparing to establish permanent bases on the moon. International consortiums are drafting the logistics to mine near-earth asteroids. If the operating system is still running, and the evidence says it is, then the question isn't whether someone is writing a new Treaty of Tordesillas for space.

The question is who ends up on the wrong side of the line.
