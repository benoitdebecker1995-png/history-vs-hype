# Example Article: Age of Invention — The Forgotten Golden Age
**Author:** Anton Howes (historian of innovation, 46K Substack subscribers)
**Source:** Age of Invention Substack
**Why this is relevant:** History newsletter with primary source quotes, comparative analysis, forgotten-history angle

---

In the sixteenth century's middle decades, England appeared an unlikely candidate for accelerated innovation. It seemed "a technological backwater, with little trade, few industries," plagued by various social and political difficulties. The economy relied overwhelmingly on raw material production.

Yet within a century, sustained economic growth was underway. By the 1650s, England had assembled critical masses of inventors and scientists, with London becoming an internationally recognized innovation hub.

Italian geographer Giovanni Botero examined this period in his 1590s treatise on European and Asian powers' relative strengths (translated 1601 as *The Traveller's Breviat*). His work provides statistical comparisons of populations, geographies, industries, militaries, and political systems — offering snapshots of what stood out internationally during England's crucial transformative century.

## England's Recognition

Botero acknowledged England's "most swift ships, excellent well furnished with ordinance" and navigation feats. Francis Drake had circumnavigated the globe; Matthew Baker revolutionized shipbuilding. London emerged as a trade hub, "never without resort of Portuguese, Spanish, French, Flemish, and Easterling merchants."

Beyond shipping, Botero documented England's traditional exports: abundant grain, livestock, horses, and fish. The population enjoyed high living standards. English people possessed "tall, strong and manlike stature," making them suitable pikemen. England exported animal skins, coal, beer to the Low Countries, plus tin, lead, and especially wool. Woollen cloth represented its primary industry.

## The Low Countries' Prominence

Regarding innovation, Botero highlighted the Low Countries instead. These "rich, plentiful and exceeding populous" territories had "invented the art of printing, restored music, framed the chariot, devised the laying of colours in oil." Industries included glass-working, tapestry-making, various cloth production, clocks, dials, and compasses.

The Netherlands led in weaving, printing, and instrument-making. Botero noted lead, copperas, coal, stone, madder, woad, flax, and hemp resources. Dutch fishing — salmon, cod, especially herring — generated astounding wealth. Antwerp, Europe's commercial center, teemed with multilingual foreign merchants.

This dominance reflected historical patterns. The Low Countries had long been Europe's most urbanized region. The Dutch Republic's seventeenth-century "Golden Age" remains famous today as an acknowledged "efflorescence" — temporary innovation and economic growth bubbles that eventually stagnated or declined.

## The Saadi Empire

Botero identified another overlooked region: "The Xeriffe" (sharif — Prophet Muhammad descendants). By 1600, Saadi Empire rulers in Morocco, centered on Fez, claimed this title.

Botero described cities of marble and alabaster with brass lamps. Fez contained "200 schools of learning, 200 inns, and 400 water mills, every one driven with four or five wheels," generating substantial ruler revenues. The city featured "600 conduits, from whence almost every house is served with water." Inhabitants were "very thrifty, given to traffic [commerce], and especially to the making of clothes of wool, silk, and cotton."

This remarkable city was wealthy, populous, somewhat industrialized, and trade-oriented. Under Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur "the Golden" and successors, it became a regional learning center. Their library represents a major surviving Islamic manuscript collection on literature and science. The Saadi Empire militarily contended with Portugal, wielding substantial gunpowder arsenals used conquering Sub-Saharan Africa. It maintained friendly commercial and diplomatic relations with England and the Dutch Republic, opposing Spain collectively.

## The Overlooked Efflorescence

Remarkably, the Saadi Empire never appears as an acknowledged efflorescence in scholarly literature. It features nowhere in Long Divergence debates — the centuries-long economic reversal between northwestern Europe and the Islamic world. Recent major works, like Jared Rubin's examination, focus exclusively on Ottoman comparisons, omitting Morocco entirely.

This may reflect English-language source availability or Turkish historians' prominence. Fez's wealth descriptions might be exaggerated, or straightforward explanations for economic decline may exist. Nevertheless, fully understanding Britain's Industrial Revolution causes requires seriously considering the Saadi Empire's rise and fall as another efflorescence.
