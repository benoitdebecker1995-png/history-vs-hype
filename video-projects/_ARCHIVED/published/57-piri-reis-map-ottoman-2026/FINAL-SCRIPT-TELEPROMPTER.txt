[1 — COLD OPEN]

This is the most important thing written on the Piri Reis Map — a paragraph, in Ottoman Turkish, tucked in the corner. For five hundred years, almost nobody has read it.

You've probably seen this map. People have called it the most accurate map of the sixteenth century. Proof of a seafaring civilization tens of thousands of years old. Proof the Earth was visited by aliens. It's the one document the whole lost-civilization case keeps coming back to. Graham Hancock brought it up again on Lex Fridman in October 2024, saying it's part of a class of ancient maps that came out of the Library of Alexandria.

But the man who drew this map already told us where it came from, and what's on it. He wrote it down on the parchment, in his own hand. So today we're going to read it.

One thing first — if you like Graham Hancock and the stories he tells, this isn't an attack on the stories. I'm just trying to explain why the scientific community doesn't agree with him. It's about one document, and what it says when you actually read it.

The answers are on the map. People just don't read it.

[2 — THE CORNER: WHERE EVERY LINE CAME FROM]

Start in the corner. The most important thing on this map is the paragraph there — historians number it inscription six. I'm going to call it the source list, because that's what it is.

"I have used twenty maps and mappaemundi… eight such cağferiyes… one of the Arab maps of India, four maps made by the Portuguese… and finally, a map drawn by Columbus."

Eight Ptolemaic world maps. One Arab map of India. Four recent Portuguese charts. One drawn by Christopher Columbus. Piri Reis says it himself — he took other people's maps and brought them to one scale. He didn't find this map. He put it together from other maps.

Here's Hancock's claim. The maps Piri Reis copied weren't ordinary maps. They were survivals from a lost civilization, preserved for thousands of years in the Library of Alexandria.

But Piri Reis names his sources, and none of them are ancient. Eight Ptolemaic textbook maps. Four recent Portuguese charts. A map drawn by Columbus, within living memory.

Hancock got the Library of Alexandria from Charles Hapgood, in 1966. And Hapgood got it, in part, from the map itself — the source list really does say the oldest charts go back to the time of Alexander the Great. Alexander founded Alexandria; its library was the wonder of the ancient world. So Hapgood put the maps on its shelves. But that last step is his, not Piri Reis's. McIntosh checked the map and everything else Piri Reis wrote, and the claim simply isn't there:

"…he never makes such a statement in any of the map inscriptions."

And there's one word in that same line worth stopping on. Piri Reis says those eight maps go back to the time of Alexander — and he gives them a name. Cağferiye.

It sounds like it should mean something: the title of some old, mysterious class of map, old enough to carry lost knowledge. It isn't.

Every scholar who can read the original reaches the same conclusion. It's a spelling mistake.

Cağferiye is the Arabic word for geography — jughrafiya — spelled wrong. In Arabic, the letter for the "gh" sound is identical to another except for one dot on top. Drop the dot, swap one more letter, and jughrafiya turns into cağferiye. A word that means nothing.

Those eight cağferiyes were ordinary geography maps — copies of Claudius Ptolemy's atlas, reprinted all over Renaissance Europe. Nothing ancient about them.

People call them ancient anyway, because of one more mix-up — the same one that put Alexander's name on the map. There are two famous Ptolemys, and they lived three hundred years apart. Claudius Ptolemy, the mapmaker, worked around 150 AD. Ptolemy the First was one of Alexander the Great's generals. Piri Reis confused the two — he thought his maps reached all the way back to Ptolemy the First, in Alexander's day. They didn't. But that one slip is where the time of Alexander on the source list comes from — and it's the line Hapgood built his library on.

And none of this is new. The first scholar to make sense of this map, Paul Kahle, worked it out back in 1933.

"Dja'fariye is a mistake… for dja'grafiye, geography."

Kahle could read the original Ottoman. Hapgood couldn't — and he wrote his book thirty years later anyway.

[3 — THE WESTERN COAST: CUBA, NOT BIMINI]

That tells us where the sources came from. Look at what's actually drawn on the map.

The Caribbean section is the one Hancock points to most often. In 2019, he said this island, east of Florida, shows the Bimini Road — a line of stone blocks off the Bahamas, drawn while they were still above water in the Ice Age.

"Underwater off Bimini I have scuba-dived on a road of great megaliths exactly like those depicted above water on the Piri Reis map…"

Piri Reis labelled that coast himself. Right where Hancock points — the island he reads as the Bahamas — there are two place-names: Porta ghande and Kaw Punta Orofay. Both are Columbus's, from his second voyage. McIntosh checked every surviving chart from the period: these two names appear on only one map on Earth. This one.

Which puts the spot Hancock calls the Bimini Road in Cuba, not the Bahamas — copied, name for name, from a map by Christopher Columbus. Not twelve thousand years old. The work of a man who died in 1506.

There's one small mystery here: how an Ottoman admiral ended up with a map by Columbus. The inscription says it came off a Spanish prisoner captured by Piri Reis's uncle, who claimed he'd sailed with Columbus three times. He may have exaggerated. But the place-names hold either way.

And there's one detail that pins this section to Columbus's lifetime, not the Ice Age. Look at how Cuba is drawn. On the 1513 map, Cuba isn't an island — it's attached to the mainland. That's Columbus's mistake. He was so sure Cuba was the edge of Asia that on June 12th, 1494, he sent a notary from ship to ship to make his whole crew swear to it. Anyone who ever took it back would have his tongue cut out.

[on screen: the Act of June 12, 1494, quoted in Kahle 1933 — "under a penalty of 10,000 maravedis and the cutting out of the tongue." NLM c4d04eaf]

The map copies that error, exactly. And you can watch it get corrected. Piri Reis made a second map in 1528, and by then ships had sailed all the way around Cuba. So on the 1528 map, Cuba is an island. The map kept up with what explorers were learning. A 12,000-year-old map wouldn't carry Columbus's personal blunder — and wouldn't quietly fix itself fifteen years later.

So the "ancient maps" weren't ancient. They were Columbus's first rough charts of the Caribbean, from around 1495 — and the originals are lost. Which means this corner of an Ottoman map is the closest thing left on Earth to Columbus's own lost cartography.

[4 — THE SOUTHERN COAST: BRAZIL, NOT ANTARCTICA]

It's the part Hancock and Hapgood read as Antarctica, mapped before the ice.

Two coasts, drawn as one. The top is Brazil. Piri labelled it — Cabo Frio, Rio de Janeiro, Cananéia, the Portuguese names, running south in the right order, right where they sit on a real map. The Portuguese sailed that coast and named it.

Then it bends into a huge empty continent across the bottom of the map. That part isn't a discovery. Every mapmaker in the 1500s drew a giant southern landmass down there — Ptolemy's geography said one had to exist, to balance the globe — and Piri ran the coast of Brazil straight into it. That invented continent is what Hapgood calls Antarctica. And it's where Piri wrote inscription ten:

"This country is barren. Everything is desolate… large serpents are found here… the Portuguese infidels did not land on these shores… these shores are also said to be very hot."

The Portuguese named the coast they sailed — Brazil. They never landed on the empty stretch below it, because there was barely anything there. Hot, barren, half-invented. Not an ice cap.

And Piri Reis wasn't the only map to do this — they all did. That's the giveaway. No two of them drew that southern continent the same way, because none of them had actually seen it. So the lost-civilization writers kept the handful whose guesses happened to resemble Antarctica, Piri Reis among them, and ignored the dozens that didn't. Real maps of a real coast would agree with each other. These don't. That isn't evidence — it's shopping for it.

[5 — WHY NO ONE READ IT]

The shape was never the real story. This map sat forgotten in a palace library for four hundred years — not hidden, not suppressed, just ignored. And being ignored is the only thing that saved it.

Piri Reis was drawing this map for an empire whose government — the Porte, the Sultan and his court — had no use for what he did. In the same century, Spain and Portugal each ran a government office where every voyage's findings got added to a single master chart, kept up to date by paid cartographers. The Ottoman state had nothing like that, and didn't want it. As Svat Soucek puts it, Piri Reis's maps —

"represented a world that lay beyond the horizon of the Porte's ambition."

The map was rolled up and shelved — and there it stayed, in a storeroom nobody had properly catalogued, until 1929, when the palace was being turned into a museum and the director found it. It reached Paul Kahle — who, by luck, had spent the previous years on Piri Reis's other book, the sailing manual. He knew the hand the moment he saw it. And he caught what no one had in four centuries: the map says, in writing, that it was partly copied from a chart by Columbus.

Kahle got plenty wrong, too — and over the next seventy years, McIntosh and others corrected him, point by point.

[6 — CLOSER]

As for Piri Reis — around 1553, in his eighties, he lost a sea battle. The governor of Egypt reported him to the Sultan, and Suleiman had him executed. He died never knowing that the map he'd drawn — the one that answered every question people would spend the next five centuries asking — was still sitting where he'd left it. Soucek calls it —

"a strange map whose mutilated remains lay forgotten in the library of Topkapı Palace."

That's where the man's story ends. The map's was only beginning.

So what does the Piri Reis map actually say? It tells you where every line on it came from, and what every piece of land really is. Piri Reis wrote it down himself in 1513, on the parchment. We just didn't read it.
