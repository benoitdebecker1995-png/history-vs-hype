## SECTION 1: WHAT ARE THE SAPODILLA CAYES? (0:50 – 1:50)

`[MAP: Zoom into Sapodilla Cayes — 14 islands at the southern tip of the Belize Barrier Reef]`

The Sapodilla Cayes are fourteen small islands — sand cays and mangrove islands — at the southern end of the Belize Barrier Reef. About 36 miles east of Punta Gorda, in Belize's Toledo District. The largest is barely 300 metres across.

They sit inside the Sapodilla Cayes Marine Reserve — 39 square miles of coral gardens, endangered sea turtles, manatees. But this isn't about conservation. It's about who controls the maritime zones around them. Fishing rights. Seabed resources. Where three countries' exclusive economic zones meet.

`[TALKING HEAD]`

For about 160 years, only Guatemala and Britain claimed these islands. Honduras made no formal claim. Then in 1982, Honduras wrote them into its constitution — Article 11 explicitly lists the "Cayos Zapotillos" as Honduran territory.

`[TEXT ON SCREEN: Honduras Constitution 1982, Article 11 — "Cayos Zapotillos"]`

That turned a two-way dispute into a three-way dispute. And in November 2022, Belize took Honduras to the ICJ to settle it.

Guatemala then applied to intervene — in December 2023 — because the Sapodilla Cayes are also part of their broader claim against Belize in Case 177. If the ICJ rules on the Cayes without Guatemala present, it could prejudice their bigger case.

`[DOCUMENT: ICJ press release — Guatemala's application to intervene, dated December 2023]`

Belize's Ministry of Foreign Affairs described today's ruling as "a step forward that advances the process leading to finality."

## SECTION 2: WHAT THE ICJ RULED (1:50 – 3:30)

**[TRANSITION BRIDGE]**
That's the dispute. Now here's what the Court decided — and it came down to one question: does Guatemala have a legal interest that could be affected by this case?

`[DOCUMENT: Key paragraph from ICJ judgment on screen]`

`[TALKING HEAD]`

The Court ruled unanimously that Guatemala does have a legal interest that could be affected. The judgment states that Guatemala's interest in the sovereignty over the Sapodilla Cayes is "real and concrete."

This means Guatemala can submit written observations and appear at oral hearings. But as a non-party — the final judgment won't be binding on Guatemala.

`[TEXT ON SCREEN: "The Court unanimously decides that the Republic of Guatemala is permitted to intervene as a non-party" — ICJ Judgment, March 19, 2026]`

Now — Honduras fought this hard. They raised three objections. First, that Guatemala "failed to identify a sufficiently precise legal interest." Second, that Guatemala's application was "redundant" — because Guatemala already has its own case against Belize where it can raise these same claims. Third, that Guatemala had "abused court procedures."

`[TALKING HEAD]`

The Court rejected all three.

On the legal interest question, the Court found that Guatemala's claim to the Sapodilla Cayes — which overlaps with both Belize's and Honduras's claims — is specific enough to qualify. On redundancy, the Court recognized that a ruling on who owns these islands in one case could directly affect Guatemala's position in the other. And on abuse of procedure — the Court didn't buy it.

Guatemala's Foreign Ministry called this historic. Their statement said: "In its eighty-year history, today's ruling marks the fourth time the Court has authorized this type of intervention." Four times in eighty years. That's how rare this is.

It's a seat at the table, not a hand in the decision. Guatemala gets a voice — it can protect its interests, present its evidence, argue its position. But it can't be forced to accept the outcome. Which actually means the Court will need to be more careful in its final ruling — because anything it says about sovereignty over these islands now has to account for a third country watching from the room.

## SECTION 3: WHAT THIS TELLS US ABOUT 2027 (3:30 – 5:00)

`[TALKING HEAD]`

This doesn't mean Guatemala is winning their bigger claim. Intervention in a maritime case is procedural, not territorial.

But it does mean the Court recognizes Guatemala's interests in this region as legally relevant. And that matters for 2027.

In my last video, I analyzed three ICJ precedents — Nicaragua v Colombia, Cameroon v Nigeria, and Gabcikovo-Nagymaros.

`[B-ROLL: Quick flash of three case names on screen]`

All three pointed the same direction: the ICJ almost never voids boundary treaties. Today's ruling doesn't change that pattern. But it adds a new data point.

The Court found Guatemala's interest in the Sapodilla Cayes "real and concrete." That's language about a handful of islands. Whether the Court uses similar language about Guatemala's claim to 12,000 square kilometres of mainland Belize — that's a different question entirely.

`[TALKING HEAD]`

Here's what I think this signals. The Court is being procedurally inclusive — letting Guatemala participate so it can't later claim it was prejudiced by being excluded. That's standard international court behavior. It doesn't predict how the Court will rule on the substance.

What it does tell us is that these two cases — Belize v Honduras over the Cayes, and Guatemala v Belize over the mainland — are now formally linked. The same judges who just acknowledged Guatemala's interest in fourteen islands will eventually rule on whether Guatemala has a claim to half a country.

The deadlines confirm this is moving fast. Guatemala must submit its written statement by May 19th, 2026. Belize and Honduras have until July 20th to respond.
