# Article Writing Style Bible

**Source:** NotebookLM analysis of 9 authors (Harari, Diamond, Bryson, Pinker, Dawkins, Mukherjee, Frankopan, Graeber, Rosling), March 2026. Updated March 2026 with Minto, Zinsser, Axios Smart Brevity, Perell POP frameworks.
**Purpose:** Personal style reference for newsletter article writing. Consult before drafting or editing any article.
**Persona:** The "Calm Prosecutor": emotionally low, intellectually high. Present evidence and reach final verdicts. No hype.

---

## Phase 1: Structural Engineering (The Minto Pyramid)

### Top-Down Logic
State the summarizing idea (the verdict) BEFORE the supporting details as a default. However, "bottom-up" storytelling may be used when delaying the verdict creates dramatic irony, massive payoff, or necessary atmospheric grounding. Minto's top-down logic is the default, not a prison.

If your article is about a 500-year-old treaty causing modern poverty, state the 10% income gap in the very first paragraph.

### SCQA Introduction
Begin every article with:
- **Situation:** Non-controversial anchor the reader knows (1-2 sentences)
- **Complication:** The tension, the ghost in the data ("but the men who drew it couldn't measure it")
- **Question:** Implicit or explicit
- **Answer:** Your Shiny Dime, the verdict, immediately

This ensures you and the reader are "standing in the same place" before you lead them through the evidence.

### MECE Support
Ensure all supporting arguments are Mutually Exclusive and Collectively Exhaustive. No overlap, no gaps.

## Phase 2: Visual Packaging (Axios Smart Brevity)

Readers spend an average of 26 seconds on a story. Use visual hierarchy to trap the eye.

### The Core 4
1. **Muscular tease:** Subject line of 6 words or fewer
2. **One strong lede:** First sentence tells the reader something they don't know
3. **Axiom anchors:** Bold headings like **Why it matters:**, **The big picture:**, **By the numbers:** for skimmers
4. **Go deeper:** Historical nuance AFTER the decision point to continue

### The 350-Word Rule (Benchmarked 2026-03-25)
No section should exceed 350 words (warn). Over 500 words is a fail. Niche benchmark: Howes averages 1,400w/section, Wyman 593w, Greer uses no sections at all. 350w is conservative for history deep-dives. If a section has three data points, use bullets, not "blobs of text." Visual hierarchy traps the skimmer's eye.

## Phase 3: The Style Scrub (Pinker and Zinsser)

### Classic Style (Pinker)
Treat the prose as a "window onto the world." The reader and writer are equals looking at a scene together. Eliminate metadiscourse unless the phrase does genuine navigational work in a complex argument shift.

**Kill on sight:**
- "In this article" / "As we will see"
- "Let's look at..." / "Now we turn to..."
- "Here's the part usually left out"
- "But watch what happens when..."
- "The question isn't X, it's Y"
- "It is worth noting"

**The test:** Would you say this to a companion standing next to you at the crime scene? You wouldn't say "I'm going to show you something interesting." You'd point at the blood.

### Kill Zombie Nouns (Zinsser)
Convert all nominalizations back into active verbs. Verbs have more vigor than nouns.

| Wrong | Right |
|-------|-------|
| "The implementation of the treaty" | "The kings struggled to draw the line" |
| "The establishment of colonial administration" | "Portugal franchised its empire" |
| "The utilization of enslaved labor" | "They enslaved millions" |

### One-Syllable Power
Prefer short, Anglo-Saxon words over long, Latinate ones. Short words are strong words.
- "Japanese surrenders." not "The capitulation of the Japanese military forces."
- "Sales cratered." not "Revenue experienced a significant decline."
- "I quit." not "I tendered my resignation."

### Strip Qualifiers
Strip hedge words on confident claims: "a bit," "sort of," "rather," "in a sense," "to some extent," "it could be argued." The prosecutor doesn't say the evidence is "a bit conclusive." But for genuinely uncertain claims, hedging is appropriate — match the language to the actual confidence level.

### Em Dashes (Anti-AI Tactical Choice)
Em dashes may be used sparingly (2-3 per article) for conversational rhythm and parenthetical asides. Excessive use reads as AI-generated. This is a platform perception rule, not a writing craft principle — Pinker, Bryson, and Mukherjee all use em dashes freely. Only mandatory use: blockquote attributions.

## Phase 4: The Author-Specific Techniques

### The Calm Prosecutor's Ten Rules

1. **Direct the reader's gaze toward concrete evidence** rather than your own professional activities. (Pinker)
2. **Expose "moral confusion"** by identifying where historical actors held two contradictory beliefs simultaneously to justify underlying violence. (Graeber)
3. **Push the chain of historical causation back to "ultimate explanations"** like geography rather than stopping at proximate human decisions. (Diamond)
4. **Identify the "imagined orders" and shared fictions** that allowed historical strangers to cooperate on a massive scale. (Harari)
5. **Anchor abstract geopolitical revisions in the sensory memory of a single physical artifact**, like a map or an heirloom. (Frankopan)
6. **"Unburden the story" of human suffering before moving into analysis**, treating the naming of an event as a literary act first. (Mukherjee)
7. **Use "revealing metaphors" to shock the reader into a new way of seeing** facts buried in dry, technical literature. (Dawkins)
8. **Collapse the complexity of a long evidence dump with a punchy "verdict sentence"** of eight words or less. (Bryson)
9. **Distinguish between "long-term trends" and "immediate drama"** to neutralize the reader's instinct to over-dramatize history. (Rosling)
10. **Overcome the "Curse of Knowledge"** by explaining evidence as if your reader is as intelligent as you but happens to lack your specific data. (Pinker)

### The Mukherjee Anchor (Micro-Tragedy)
Ground abstract claims in a visceral human moment. A kindergarten teacher waking up with a headache. A convict abandoned on a beach to prove a line is real. The human cost arrives BEFORE the analysis.

### The Harari/Graeber Pivot (Imagined Order)
Identify the shared fiction (the treaty, the border, the currency) and expose the moral confusion inherent in the historical act. "Most everywhere, one finds that the majority of human beings hold simultaneously that (1) paying back money one has borrowed is a simple matter of morality, and (2) anyone in the habit of lending money is evil."

### The Bryson Scale (Narrative Data Transformation)
Never present a number without a spatial analogy. Don't say "1872 census data"; describe the "heavy paper ledger" being opened by a sweating official. After every decimal point, a hammer sentence of 5 words or less.

### The 8-Word Verdict
Every long build of evidence must land on a verdict sentence short enough to feel like a gavel strike:

- **"They left."** (Pinker/Wilkerson, collapsing centuries of migration)
- **"They are inventing a myth."** (Graeber, the prosecutor's gavel)
- **"All life is one."** (Bryson, 3.8 billion years in four words)
- **"Care, not cure."** (Mukherjee, redefining medicine)
- **"There is no justice in history."** (Harari, shutting down moral narrative)
- **"The map was a chokehold."** (History vs Hype)
- **"He was blind."** (History vs Hype)
- **"The ink was enough."** (History vs Hype)

**Mechanical rule:** Long sentences carry evidence. Short sentences carry verdicts. Never 4+ consecutive sentences of similar length.

### The Diamond Chain (Ultimate Causes)
Push the chain of causation back to geography or biology. "Why did Europeans colonize the Americas and not vice versa?" is more powerful than "What happened when Europeans arrived?" The article should always reach the deepest causal layer available.

## The Author's Voice (Derived from Interview + Writing Samples, 2026-03-28)

These qualities define the newsletter's voice. They are non-negotiable.

### 1. Pattern-Thinking, Not Timeline-Thinking
The author never explains history in isolation. Every event connects to something the reader already knows. Entry points are comparisons ("did you know the nine-dash line works on the same principle?"), not definitions ("The Treaty of Tordesillas was...").

### 2. Skepticism Before Excitement
The author's first instinct with a surprising finding is to verify it. The joy comes after confirmation. Write the discovery journey: I assumed X, I checked, the evidence says Y.

### 3. The Complexifier, Not the Debunker
The author's instinct isn't "that's wrong." It's "that's incomplete." The channel adds nuance rather than tearing down. Not "this is false" but "this is more complicated than you've been told."

### 4. Teacher's Instinct
Always thinking about what the reader will remember tomorrow, not just understand now. "The most important part is..." "If you remember only one thing..."

### 5. Honest About Limitations
Never pretend certainty. "We have to keep in mind..." "History is never 100%." "This study has some issues." Attribution before controversy: "One side claims..." not "The Crusades were..."

### 6. Plain Words, Academic Rigor
"Solidified" not "ossified." "Disappeared" not "vanished." Don't simplify the argument. Simplify the words carrying it. Academic terms only when they're actual terms of art.

### 7. Inclusive Address
"You know..." "Most people think..." "You might have heard..." The reader is a smart friend who doesn't have the specific data. "We" and "you" throughout, not just in designated personal sections.

### 8. The "I Knew X, But Y" Signature
The author's natural structure for presenting evidence that challenges assumptions. Setup with what was already known, pivot with "but," land on what the evidence showed. Use 3-4 times per article, but vary the syntax (see article-writer.md Rule 14 for 5 variants). The "full setup" version ("I knew X. But Y.") should appear max once per article. Other first-person moves include direct opinion, research narration, co-investigator address, real-time reaction, and question-framing.

## Human Qualities AI Cannot Replicate

Use these deliberately in every article. They are your anti-slop ingredients.

### 1. Detection of Moral Dissonance (Graeber)
Identify the felt social tension where humans hold two contradictory beliefs simultaneously.
- Example: "Most everywhere, one finds that the majority of human beings hold simultaneously that (1) paying back money one has borrowed is a simple matter of morality, and (2) anyone in the habit of lending money is evil."

### 2. The Discovery Journey (Author's Own)
Document the transition from "I already knew" to "but the evidence changed how I think about it." This mirrors how the reader should process the information. AI can't fake genuine surprise at primary sources.

### 3. Pre-emptive Ethical Strike (Diamond)
Identify the moral anxiety a reader might feel and disarm it with empathy BEFORE it becomes a barrier.
- "In case this question immediately makes you shudder at the thought that you are about to read a racist treatise, you aren't."

### 4. Physical Experience Anchoring (Author's Own)
Ground abstract history in places visited, documents read, comment sections witnessed. Not "history shows" but "when I walked through Santa Marta" or "the comment section on my Belize video."

### 5. References to Prior Work
The author has a body of work (videos, research, reader interactions). Mentioning it proves the newsletter comes from a person, not a content mill. At least 2 references per article.

### 6. Imaginative Mourning for the Void (Harari)
Create specific, poignant examples of people and events that MUST have existed but were lost to history.
- "The foragers may have had their all-conquering Napoleons who ruled empires half the size of Luxembourg; gifted Beethovens who lacked symphony orchestras..."

### 7. The Confession of Uncertainty
Acknowledge what the evidence doesn't prove. "We're not sure" (Harari). "It's a bit of a puzzle" (Bryson). This builds more trust than performing certainty.

## Opening Techniques

### The Author's Default: Related Precedent
The author naturally starts with something the reader already knows, then connects it to the article's topic. This should be the default opening unless another technique is stronger:
- "Why does Brazil speak Portuguese? The answer starts with a headwind."
- "Did you know the nine-dash line works on the same principle as a treaty from 1494?"

### Harari/Pinker Techniques (Use When They Fit)

| # | Technique | Example | When to use | Used in newsletter? |
|---|-----------|---------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1 | The Disarming Definition | "About 70,000 years ago, organisms belonging to the species Homo sapiens started to form even more elaborate structures called cultures." (Harari) | When the concept needs anchoring at the largest possible scale | Not yet |
| 2 | The Necker Cube Flip | "Writing is an unnatural act." (Pinker) | When the reframe IS the opening | **Never used — try next** |
| 3 | The Cosmic Context | "14 billion years ago, matter, energy, time and space came into being." (Harari) | When the zoom-out creates productive disorientation | Not yet |
| 4 | The Categorical Comparison | "There were humans long before there was history." (Harari) | When what's missing from the record is the point | **Never used — try next** |
| 5 | Confirming Popular Myths | Validate the reader's wrong belief, then demolish it (Rosling) | Myth-busting articles | Not yet |
| 6 | The Micro-Tragedy | Start with one REAL person on the day everything changed (Mukherjee) | When a named individual exists in the sources | Crusades (Nicetas) |
| 7 | Direct Existential Address | "Welcome. And congratulations." (Bryson) | When the reader is an heir to the event | Not yet |

**Rotation note:** SCQA has been the default for 5/6 articles. Actively choose from techniques 1-7 for the next few articles to build variety into the corpus.

## The Counterintuitive Reframe (One Per Article)

Every article needs one "I never thought about it that way" moment. Test it:

> "Most readers assume _____, but the evidence shows _____."

If you can't fill the first blank with a REAL belief readers hold, the reframe is an observation, not an inversion. Observations inform. Inversions transform.

| Author | The Reframe | Sentences needed |
|--------|-------------|-----------------|
| Graeber | Barter didn't come first, credit did. Standard history is precisely backwards. | 4 |
| Rosling | Chimps score better than educated humans on global development questions. | 3 |
| Diamond | Necessity is NOT the mother of invention; invention is the mother of necessity. | 3 |
| Harari | The Agricultural Revolution was "history's biggest fraud," more people alive under worse conditions. | 3 |
| Frankopan | The Mediterranean is not the center of civilization. Central Asia is. | 2 |
| Mukherjee | Cancer is not an invader. It's a distorted version of our normal selves. | 2 |
| Dawkins | Organisms don't use genes to reproduce. Genes use organisms to survive. | 3 |
| Pinker | Bad writing comes from expertise, not stupidity: the Curse of Knowledge. | 3 |
| Bryson | Your atoms don't know you exist. Yet they keep you alive anyway. | 5 |

**Pattern:** 2-4 sentences is the sweet spot. State the assumption. Invert it. Land the verdict.

## Evidence as Narrative (Not Citation Dumps)

### The Rule
Never present evidence as bibliography. Evidence arrives as story, as surprise, as discovery.

### Four Conversion Rules

| If you're writing... | Do this instead |
|---------------------|-----------------|
| A system/mechanism | Follow a PERSON surviving that system. "Duarte Coelho liquidated his life." |
| A study/paper | Lead with the finding as felt experience. Name the researcher AFTER the punch, not before. |
| A papal bull/law | Treat the institution as a character with moral contradictions. "The Vatican became the back office for a slave monopoly." |
| A navigation technique | Follow a terrified human. "A Portuguese captain didn't sail home; he fled west." |

### Five Models

**1. The Hiss of the Universe (Bryson)**
Instead of "Penzias and Wilson discovered cosmic background radiation," tell the story of them scrubbing bird shit off an antenna for a year before realizing the noise WAS the discovery.

**2. The Carpet Salesman (Mukherjee)**
Instead of "Papanicolaou developed the Pap smear," tell the story of a failed carpet salesman assigned to study guinea pig menstrual cycles.

**3. The Academic Gossip (Harari)**
Instead of "Language evolved for social bonding," ask whether physics professors discuss quarks at lunch (they gossip about the dean).

**4. The Salt and Stars (Bryson)**
Instead of "Bob Evans discovered many supernovae," make the reader imagine 1,500 dining tables covered in salt, then finding one extra grain.

**5. The Ghost Who Discussed Credit (Graeber)**
Instead of "Madagascan culture projects monetary concepts onto the past," tell the story of interviewing a ghost who only wanted to talk about money.

**Pinker's law:** If you name the researcher before the finding, you've failed.
**Howes's law:** If a paragraph explains a system, rewrite it to follow a man surviving that system.
**Bryson's law:** If you use a decimal point, the next sentence must be a hammer under 5 words.

## Three Grounded Endings (Kill the TED Talk Leap)

Stay at the crime scene. Never trade the precise lethality of your evidence for speculative escalation.

### 1. The Loop (Graeber)
Return to the opening artifact or person. Close the circle. "The ink was enough."

### 2. The Micro-Tragedy (Mukherjee/Bryson)
Return to a named person who represents the system. "We no longer leave convicts on beaches. We are still living in the architecture they were left to build. The line is still there."

### 3. The Unsettling Question (Howes/Harari)
Ground it in evidence, not speculation. "The question is whether we can ever uninstall it."

**NEVER:** Jump from specific evidence (Brazilian census data) to speculative futures (moon bases, asteroid mining). That's rhetorical whiplash. Unearned escalation.

## Phase 5: Final Review (The CRIBS Audit)

Before outputting, check every passage:

| Letter | Question | Action |
|--------|----------|--------|
| **C** | Confusing? | Rewrite for the intelligent non-expert |
| **R** | Repeated? | Cut the weaker version |
| **I** | Interesting? | Keep, but verify it earns its place |
| **B** | Boring? | Delete or rewrite as narrative |
| **S** | Surprising? | Double down. This is where the reader leans in. |

**Perell POP rule:** Trust primary sources. "Children above deck, brazilwood below" needs no feature-style tricks. "If this is the ONLY thing the reader sees, is it exactly what I want to stick?"

## Transition Vocabulary (Author + Harari/Pinker)

### Author's Natural Connectors (Use These First)
- "On top of that..."
- "At the same time..."
- "This brings us to..."

### From Harari/Pinker (Use When They Fit)
- "In fact..." (pivots from myth to truth)
- "The upshot is..." (collapses data into verdict)
- "Consider for example..." (abstract to visceral)
- "To be fair..." (steelman/counter-evidence)
- "What was the secret of success?" (question as bridge)

### NEVER Use
- "Consequently" / "Thereby" / "Moreover" / "Furthermore" (academic fog)
- "Now we turn to..." / "Let's look at..." (metadiscourse)
- "It is worth noting that..." (filler)
- "So when X happened, you'd think Y" (tells reader what to conclude before evidence lands)

## Section-to-Section Bridge Techniques (Harari/Pinker)

Bridges connect the end of one section to the start of the next. The reader should feel a conversation, not channel-surfing.

### 1. "Actual Instance" (Preferred)
Treat two time periods or subtopics as instances of the same phenomenon. Don't telegraph the outcome -- let the reader discover it.
- **Example:** "The data doesn't make the history more tragic. It makes it harder to look away from. In 2023, the Vatican tried to look at it directly." → next section reveals they failed.
- **Why it works:** Reader arrives at the conclusion themselves. Stronger than being told what to feel.

### 2. "Silent Pattern" Wallop
End a historical section by naming a quiet pattern. Open the next with data that proves it.
- **Example:** "The line was imaginary, unenforceable, and routinely ignored. It should have disappeared. It didn't." → next section: economist's numbers.
- **Why it works:** The short sentence ("It didn't.") creates suspense. The data pays it off.

### 3. Cause-Effect Handoff
End with a consequence, open with the mechanism. One sentence. No drama.
- **Example:** "The ones that survived needed labor. And Portugal already had the supply chain."
- **Why it works:** Moves the argument forward without the reader noticing a section break.

### 4. Scale Flip (Harari's "Necker Cube")
End a small story, open by scaling the concept to civilization-level.
- **Example (Harari):** Monkey steals banana → "Ever since the Cognitive Revolution, Sapiens have thus been living in a dual reality."
- **Why it works:** The trivial story earns the reader's attention for the abstract claim.

### Anti-Pattern: The Signposted Disappointment
"So when X happened, you'd think Y would change." This tells the reader what to conclude before they see the evidence. Cut the signpost. Let evidence land on its own.

## The Calm Prosecutor's Cheat Sheet

Tape these rules next to your screen:

1. **Verdict First:** Give the answer in the first paragraph.
2. **No Signposting:** Point at the tree; don't say you're about to point at it.
3. **The 8-Word Rule:** Collapse long evidence builds with a verdict of 8 words or less.
4. **The Axiom Anchor:** Use bolded headings like **Why it matters:** to guide skimmers.
5. **Ultimate Causes:** Push the chain of causation back to geography or biology (Diamond).
6. **Unburden the Story:** Tell the human tragedy before you analyze the data (Mukherjee).
7. **Kill the AI Voice:** Strip all hedging, all "tapestries." Present facts as undeniable verdicts.
8. **The CRIBS Audit:** Is it Confusing, Repeated, Interesting, Boring, or Surprising?
9. **Audience First:** Imagine a smart, busy friend at a bar. Don't bore them.
10. **The Shiny Dime:** Hyper-focus on the smallest viable idea that breaks a reader's worldview.

## HARD RULE: Real Names Only

- **Real name with source = powerful** (Mukherjee's Carla Reed, Diamond's Yali, Graeber's Father Graeme)
- **Invented name or scene = slop** (fabricated census takers, imagined conversations)
- **NotebookLM research habit:** Always ask for named individuals at the edges of big events: clerks, cartographers, translators, local officials. The names are in the footnotes.
- If no real name exists, the data alone is enough. Never fill the gap with fiction.

## Forbidden Phrases

**AI slop:** "Delve," "tapestry," "nuanced," "multifaceted," "shed light on," "navigate the complexities," "a testament to," "pivotal," "crucial," "landscape" (non-literal), "underscores the importance," "played a crucial role"

**Metadiscourse:** "Here's the part," "But watch what happens," "The question isn't X, it's Y," "Let's look at," "Now we turn to," "It is worth noting," "In this article," "As we will see"

**Filler:** "Let's dive into," "Without further ado," "In the tapestry of history," "This raises important questions," "Throughout history," "A complex interplay of"

**Trust-killers:** "a bit," "sort of," "rather," "in a sense," "to some extent," "it could be argued"

**Formatting:** Em dashes in prose (use commas, periods, colons, parentheses instead)

## Sentence Rhythm Quick Reference

From Gary Provost's principle, observed across all 9 authors:

> Write short sentences. Write medium-length sentences. But also write sentences that carry the reader forward through cascading clauses that build and build until the accumulated weight of the evidence demands a conclusion that can only be delivered in the shortest possible form. Like this.

- **Build** with medium/long sentences (evidence, context, nuance)
- **Land** with short ones (verdict, insight, reframe)
- The short sentence is where the insight lives
- Never write 5 sentences of the same length in a row
