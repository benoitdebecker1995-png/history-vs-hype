# Voice Profile — History vs Hype

## 1. Word Choice

### Prefer simpler verbs, but don't strip vivid ones

| AI default | Creator's version |
|------------|-------------------|
| "represent" | "are" |
| "gained significant traction" | "became quite popular" |
| "resulted in the partitioning of" | "divided" |
| "discovered a collection of" | "found a bunch of" |
| "prohibited" | "forbade" |
| "predating" | "comes before" |
| "revealed no geological scarring" | "show that there is no geological scarring" |
| "never existed as a physical landmass" | "was never actually there" |

**Rule:** If a simpler verb exists AND the simpler version loses nothing, use it. But don't strip vivid or precise verbs just because a plainer word exists — "obliterating" is fine when the intensity is earned. The goal is casual precision, not vocabulary flattening.

**Exception:** Don't over-simplify when the original phrasing does argumentative work. "Not randomly inventing a large number" is better than "not making it up" because "randomly" and "large number" set up the contrast with the careful counting method that follows. Simplify the verb, keep the specificity.

### Casual precision

The creator is precise about facts but casual about language. Combine accurate content with relaxed phrasing.

| AI default | Creator's version |
|------------|-------------------|
| "significantly more valuable" | "a lot more valuable" |
| "a collection of documents" | "a bunch of documents" |
| "gained significant traction" | "became quite popular" |
| "unique legal status" | "crazy status" |
| "military dictatorships" | "dictatorships" (when context is clear) |

**Rule:** Drop intensifiers like "significantly", "fundamentally", "notably". Replace with "quite", "actually", "a lot more". These are the creator's natural hedges.

## 2. Explaining Things

### Explain jargon when it helps accessibility

When a technical or specialist term appears that a general audience wouldn't know, define it in plain language:

- "Bathymetric surveys — these are ocean floor scans"
- Institutions: always spell out full name + brief description of what they are

**Rule:** Explain terms that would lose a wider audience. Don't define words everyone knows (treaty, colony, border). The goal is accessibility, not hand-holding. Use judgment — if the audience for this topic would know the term, skip the definition.

### Define terms at first mention, not at point of use

When a term needs explaining, define it where the concept FIRST appears, not later when it's used in a specific claim. Don't patch a missing definition with a parenthetical at the usage point — move the definition earlier so both passages read clean.

- Bad: "Mexico got 62% of the Western Polygon, roughly 5,092 square nautical miles. (The treaty calls it the 'Western Polygon,' but it's the same wedge of seabed.)"
- Good: 'This is called the "donut hole" — the treaty calls it the Western Polygon.' [first mention] → "Mexico got 62% of the Western Polygon" [later use, no explanation needed]

**Rule:** Define early, use cleanly later. Keep definitions to ~8 words max — an appended clause, not an explanatory aside.

### Introduce cultural references by function, not location

When referencing something the viewer might know (a quote, a symbol, a document), explain what people USE it for before listing where it appears. Function before form.

- Bad: "It's on bumper stickers, rifle grips, t-shirts. Supposedly said by King Leonidas..."
- Good: "It's used as an illustration of Spartan defiance — the moment King Leonidas supposedly told... You'll find it on bumper stickers, rifle grips, t-shirts."

This applies to **titles and epithets** too. Don't drop a title and assume the viewer knows what it means — explain what someone DID to earn it before naming it.

- Bad: "Ancient critics called him the 'Father of History.' They also called him the 'Father of Lies.'"
- Good: "He's considered the first person to systematically collect evidence and try to write down what actually happened — that's why ancient critics called him the 'Father of History.' They also called him the 'Father of Lies' — because he regularly includes things that are clearly exaggerated or impossible."

### Concrete size comparisons

Never leave a number abstract. Compare it to something the viewer can picture:

- AI: "an island of Bermeja's reported size"
- Creator: "an island the size of Birmingham"

**Rule:** When mentioning a size, distance, population, or quantity — consider adding a comparison to something familiar. Not every number needs one — use comparisons when the scale is genuinely hard to grasp, not as a default reflex.

### Time comparisons must do argumentative work

Don't just name a century ("that's like looking back at the 1500s"). Name a specific event everyone knows, AND use the comparison to reinforce the point you're making:

- Bad: "430 years — that's like looking back at the 1500s"
- Better: "430 years — that's like looking back at Columbus reaching the Americas"
- Best: "That's like a historian today writing about Columbus reaching the Americas — and we already know how much gets wrong about that."

The comparison should illustrate AND argue. Make it do double duty. But DON'T spell out the conclusion — set up the implication and let the viewer connect the dots. "There's still a lot of mythos surrounding those events, even with modern standards" is better than "and we already know how much gets wrong about that."

### "You" walk-throughs for mechanisms

When explaining HOW something works, use second person and walk through it step by step:

> "You basically throw a piece of rope overboard with something heavy at the end. You start counting the time and you count how much of the rope actually gets unraveled. Then you multiply the distance of the rope by the time you've been sailing and you then approximately know how much distance you have traveled."

**Rule:** Technical explanations = "you [do X], you [see Y], you then [know Z]". Make the viewer do it in their head.

### Fill in the WHY

The creator naturally adds causal explanations that AI scripts skip:

- AI: (just states the 1902 border change)
- Creator: "It would explain why they redrew it — because the Hala'ib Triangle is a lot more valuable"

**Rule:** After stating what happened, add WHY it happened — even if the script didn't include it. Also: when citing biographical details about a historical figure, explain HOW we know those details (name the source). Don't present ancient biography as established fact without attribution.

## 3. Signposting

### Flag importance explicitly

The creator tells viewers WHY a fact matters before stating it:

- "It's important to mention that no African representatives were invited"
- "It's very important to note here that the Germans didn't pressure the French"

**Note:** STYLE-GUIDE.md lists "It's important to note that..." as forbidden filler. The creator's version is different — it's not filler, it's a genuine signal that the next fact changes the interpretation. **Allow "It's important to mention/note that..." when it genuinely flags a paradigm-shifting fact. Don't use it as padding.**

### Connect to broader patterns

Don't just state an isolated fact. Connect it to a pattern the viewer can recognize:

- "Like so many conflict-oriented things in the world, this one stems from a colonial power"

**Rule:** When a specific case is an instance of a larger pattern, name the pattern. The viewer should feel "oh, this happens everywhere" — not just "this happened here."

### Date-first anchoring (flexible)

Dates can get their own weight, but don't always need to be isolated as a separate sentence. It depends on whether there's narrative buildup before the date.

- **With setup (date flows in):** "And that clash was bound to happen sooner or later. 5 September 1492, Columbus accidentally stumbled upon an unknown continent and the clash was about to begin."
- **Without setup (date anchors):** "October 3rd, 1940. The Vichy government enacted the statute."
- **AI default (date buried):** "enacted by the Vichy government on October 3rd, 1940" — too passive

**Rule:** Give dates prominence — don't bury them at the end of a clause. But whether the date gets its own sentence or flows into one depends on the narrative momentum. Big reveal moments = standalone date. Normal narration = date woven in naturally.

### Attribute numbers to sources

Don't float statistics without attribution:

- AI: "approximately 229 ethnic groups found themselves divided"
- Creator: "According to a study by [researcher], 229 ethnic groups were divided"

**Rule:** Numbers need a source name. The viewer should know WHO counted, not just the count.

## 4. Sentence Structure

### Clarity over length

AI tends to pack too much into dense, passive compound sentences. The fix is clarity, not mechanical splitting.

- AI (too dense): "The borders established during this three-month conference were drawn using rivers, latitude lines, and other geographic markers, with minimal consideration for existing ethnic, linguistic, or cultural boundaries."
- Creator (clearer): "The borders drawn at this conference — they were mainly based on latitude lines and other geographic markers. They didn't consider existing ethnic, linguistic or cultural boundaries."

**Rule:** Split when a sentence is genuinely confusing or packs unrelated ideas. But longer sentences are fine when they build momentum — "5 September 1492, Columbus accidentally stumbled upon an unknown continent and the clash was about to begin" works as one sentence because the ideas flow together.

**Anti-pattern:** Don't over-fragment into staccato one-to-three-word sentences for dramatic effect. "One source. Written 40 years after the fact. From oral testimony." is four fragments doing one idea's job. Combine when the beats belong together: "One source, written 40 years after the fact, based on oral testimony."

### Active voice, people as subjects

- AI: "this law was not enacted under German pressure"
- Creator: "The Germans didn't pressure the French into drafting this law"

**Rule:** Make people/nations the subject. "France signed" not "the treaty was signed." "The Germans didn't" not "it was not enacted under."

### Narrate, don't list

When summarising events, use verbs and action, not noun-phrase lists.

- Bad: "a massive Persian invasion, a Greek force holding a narrow pass, and Spartans dying in a final stand"
- Good: "a massive Persian army invaded Greece, a Greek coalition force marched out to hold them at a narrow pass, and Spartans and Thespians stayed behind to hold up the Persian army"

**Rule:** Summaries should still tell a story. Nouns listing things that happened should become verbs showing things happening.

### Name the pathway, not "everyone knows"

Don't assume universal knowledge. Name the specific way people encountered something.

- Bad: "That's the version everyone knows."
- Good: "If you've seen the movie, read the graphic novel, or played any of the games — that's this version."

**Rule:** This includes the viewer who HAS seen it (recognition) rather than lecturing them (assumption). It also avoids alienating viewers who haven't.

## 5. Delivery Patterns

### Introducing quotes

Pattern: **Name → signal → source → quote → interpret**

> "Bismarck famously said — in [source]: '[quote]'. We can interpret this as [plain language meaning]."

**Rules:**
- Name the person first
- Signal a quote is coming ("famously said", "wrote that", "argued")
- Cite the source briefly
- Deliver the full quote verbatim
- Then translate the IMPLICATION (not paraphrase the content)

### Setup lines before quotes: flexible by purpose

The creator uses different setups depending on WHY the quote is being used:

- **To illustrate a point:** "The best illustration of X is..." → quote
- **To invoke authority:** "Hear what X said about this..." or "Experts have studied this and came to X conclusion" → quote
- **To reveal a document:** "Here's what the treaty actually says:" → quote

**Rule:** The setup should tell the viewer what the quote will DO — illustrate, prove, surprise, or contradict. Don't front-load a verdict the quote should earn ("And they fought brilliantly" before a quote about tactics). But naming the source or the function first is fine.

### Transitions

The creator tries to make transitions logical and structural — the end of one section naturally sets up the next:

> "...they wanted to control how to divide the land. That division wasn't done by looking at who lived there, but by what geographic markers they could use to create new countries."

**Rule:** Prefer seed-planting transitions where section A's last sentence raises section B's topic. But with dense material, explicit bridges are fine — sometimes you need to connect ideas that don't flow naturally. The goal is structural logic, not performative seamlessness. Avoid empty bridges ("Now let's look at...") but functional ones ("That treaty created a problem:") are fine.

### Dramatic turns / debunks

The creator does NOT do hard dramatic cuts. Instead: **anticipate viewer resistance → zoom out to broader principle → come back to the specific case:**

> "You might think that even after so much evidence, there must be something there. However, this is not how cartography works in a broader sense. Sometimes a lot of people just repeat what they've heard before. In this case, that repeating happened for over 500 years."

**Pattern:** "You might think [viewer's instinct]" → "However, this is not how [field] works" → broader principle → back to the specific → landing line.

### Steelmanning

Find the other side's STRONGEST argument, present it genuinely, then either counter or admit uncertainty:

> "Defenders of the regime might argue that 70% of Jews in France survived. That's an important statistic. It's important to give the other side credit and look at their strongest argument — and then say why I don't believe it, or if it's more open, just admit they have a good point and there's no conclusive answer."

**Pattern:** "Defenders of [X] might argue [strongest point]" → "That's an important [stat/point]" → counter with evidence OR openly admit no conclusion.

**NOT:** "If we are being fair..." (this phrase came from AI scripts and feels unnatural to the creator).

### Closings

The creator values endings that land on real human consequences rather than abstract geopolitics. What matters is what happened to actual people — infrastructure gaps, displacement, neglect.

**Good closing instincts:**
- Zoom into real people affected by the system
- Connect the specific case to a broader pattern ("This isn't the only case")
- End with an image or fact that lingers, not a thesis restatement

**NOT:** "We've looked at [thing] because [reason]" (recap/summary — this is AI "In conclusion" energy). NOT dramatic final lines. NOT "and that's the real story of..."

**For closing structures** (Loop, Unsettling Question, Micro-Tragedy), see ARTICLE-WRITING-STYLE-BIBLE.md.

### Trust the viewer to feel the surprise

When a fact is inherently surprising (the famous quote belongs to someone nobody knows), don't add editorial commentary explaining why it's surprising ("most people have never heard of him"). Set up the expectation, deliver the contradiction, and let the viewer connect. The structure does the work.

- Bad: "Herodotus gives it to Dienekes. Most people have never heard of him."
- Good: "Herodotus attributes that quote not to Leonidas, but to a soldier called Dienekes."

The "not X, but Y" structure carries the surprise. Adding commentary over-explains it.

### Frame perceptions, then test them with evidence

Don't editorialize about groups as if it's established fact. Instead, frame how something IS perceived, then use evidence to affirm or challenge that perception.

- **Less precise:** "Sparta was a militaristic society — fighting to the death is what they were trained for."
- **More precise (perception → evidence):** "This is how we think about Sparta. But the 300 Spartans represented about 4% of Sparta's hoplite force. The 700 Thespians represented one hundred per cent of theirs."

**Rule:** The creator tries to be as objective as possible. Name the perception ("this is how X is perceived"), then let evidence affirm or disprove it. Stats are powerful tools for this — the numbers carry the contrast without the script making character judgments. But this is about intellectual honesty, not neutrality — the creator still reaches verdicts, they just earn them with evidence first.

### The script must survive its own methodology

If a video is about source criticism, the script cannot treat unsourced popular narratives as established fact. If a video debunks oversimplification, the script cannot oversimplify. Every claim in the script should pass the same standard the video applies to its subject matter.

## 6. Style Guide Overrides

These VOICE-PROFILE patterns override specific STYLE-GUIDE.md rules where the creator's natural voice conflicts:

| Style Guide says | Voice Profile says | Resolution |
|------------------|-------------------|------------|
| FORBID "It's important to note that..." | Creator naturally says "It's important to mention that..." | **ALLOW** when flagging a genuinely paradigm-shifting fact |
| APPROVED: "So, I read/checked/found..." | Creator says this feels cringe, came from AI influence | **DE-EMPHASIZE** — use sparingly (max 1x per script), not as a signature move |
| APPROVED: "If we are being fair..." | Creator says "steelman yes but more natural" | **REPLACE** with "Defenders might argue..." or "even though [concession]..." |
| FORBID "However" as too formal | Creator naturally uses "However" in speech | **ALLOW** "However" in limited use (2-3x per script) |
| Signature: "Here's what [X] actually says" | Creator uncertain whether this is natural to them | **KEEP but limit** to 1-2x per script, only for genuine document reveals |

## 7. Creator's Cognitive Patterns (Observed 2026-04-01)

These patterns describe how the creator processes and evaluates text. Each has direct implications for draft quality.

### Distinction-first thinking

The creator naturally splits concepts that look similar but aren't. "Checked" vs "verified." "Divided" vs "split." "Marine data" vs "ocean floor surveys." A word that blurs two different ideas will get flagged.

**Implication:** Never use a word that collapses a meaningful distinction. Before writing a verdict or summary, ask: is this word precise enough, or does it merge two things the creator would separate? When in doubt, use the more specific term even if it's longer.

### Audience-position reading

Every critique comes from the reader's chair, not the writer's. "This is not explained." "Comes out of nowhere." "Too many short sentences." The creator evaluates text by simulating a first encounter.

**Implication:** Every draft should pass a "cold reader" test. Not just jargon (already covered in Section 2), but any concept, name, number, or term that a reader hits before they have context to understand it. If a reader would pause and think "wait, what's that?" — it needs setup.

### Iterative precision

The creator doesn't arrive at ideas in one shot. They circle: rough idea → correction → refinement → landing. Each pass gets more logically precise. The final iteration is closest to intent but still shorthand.

The creator's mission is to bring the historical academic discipline to accessible YouTube/Substack. This means adding nuance where it matters — a disclaimer, an explanation that nothing is 100%, intellectual honesty about not knowing enough, or flagging that something is interpretation — without hedging every single claim. Clean statements stand where they're accurate enough. Corrections happen where they count.

**Implication:** When the creator offers multiple passes at a phrase, don't take the first OR the last version literally. Extract the idea that survived all iterations — that's what they actually mean. The last version shows direction; your job is to write the publishable form.

### Systems over events

The creator's instinct is to land on the mechanism, not the outcome. "Nobody found the island" is an event. "Cartographic trust was never stress-tested until money was on the line" is a system. The channel's DNA (HOW > WHY) shows up in how the creator evaluates closers and verdicts.

**Implication:** Verdict sentences and closers should name the SYSTEM that failed or operated, not just what happened. "France went further" (event) is weaker than "The student became the model" (system reversal). Always ask: what's the mechanism?

### Literal-truth scrutiny

The creator holds their own prose to the same evidence standard as the subjects they cover. "Nobody checked" gets flagged because people DID check the map — they read it and copied it. They just never verified the source data. Rhetorically effective but literally false = rejected.

**Implication:** Every verdict sentence must survive literal reading. If a reader could respond "well actually that's not quite true," rewrite it. Rhetorical punch must come FROM precision, not at the expense of it.

## 8. Working With Creator Feedback

### Creator shorthand = direction, not copy

When the creator suggests phrasing during a humanify or editing pass, treat it as the IDEA to express, not text to paste in. The creator has said they struggle to write out their thoughts. Their suggestions are directional — extract the core idea and rewrite it to publication standard.

- Bad: Creator says "everyone trusted the map. Nobody bothered to verify its information until geopolitical interests were at stake." → paste it verbatim into the article
- Good: Extract the idea (blind trust → only verified when stakes forced it) → "And for 382 years, every cartographer trusted the last one's work. It took billions of barrels of oil to make someone finally sail out and look."

**Rule:** Never paste creator shorthand directly into a draft. That's the opposite of the job. Take the direction, write it properly.

## Summary Formula

**Write like someone explaining history at a dinner table — precise about facts, casual about language, always telling you WHY it matters, always connecting the specific to the universal, always ending with what it means for real people.**
