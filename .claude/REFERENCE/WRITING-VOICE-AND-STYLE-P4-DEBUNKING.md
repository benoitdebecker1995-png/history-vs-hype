# PART 4 — DEBUNKING FRAMEWORK (script-side)

Your scripts are public history communication performed at scale. The same psychological mechanisms that cause comment section backfire also cause viewer resistance to myth-debunking videos. When you debunk nationalist myths or ideological narratives, viewers experience **identity-protective cognition** — their brains register factual corrections as threats to group belonging.

This framework comes from cognitive psychology + public history pedagogy applied to script structure.

## 4.1 Myth-first vs truth-sandwich

**Research:** Repeating a myth to debunk it makes the myth more familiar, which brains mistake for truth over time.

### The Truth Sandwich

1. **Lead with truth:** State what the evidence actually shows.
2. **Name the myth:** Identify the false claim.
3. **Explain the error:** Show WHY the myth is wrong (mechanism, not just contradiction).
4. **Restate truth:** Reinforce the correct understanding.

The "Both Extremes Wrong" opening already does this well. The key addition: when debunking a SINGLE myth (not two extremes), don't open with the myth. Open with the evidence.

| Wrong (myth-first) | Right (truth sandwich) |
|--------------------|------------------------|
| "People say Napoleon was short. But actually..." | "Napoleon was 5'7\" — average for his era. So where did the 'short' myth come from?" |
| "The myth says medieval people thought the earth was flat." | "Medieval scholars knew the earth was round. Bede wrote about it in 725 CE. So why do people think they didn't?" |

This applies to SINGLE-MYTH videos. Versus / two-extremes format already avoids this trap.

### Performance data (myth-first vs narrative-first)

For non-territorial topics, myth-first opening = 30.3% retention vs 22.4% for narrative-first.

**Section title rule:** Use fact-first headlines. Place myth in subordinate clause.

- Section title: "The Real Columbus Debate" (fact-first) ✅
- NOT: "Debunking the Flat Earth Myth" (myth-first) ❌

## 4.2 The seven debunking principles

### 1. Alternative explanations over negation

Simply negating a myth leaves a mental gap. People prefer an incorrect model to no model at all.

- **Bad approach:** "Columbus wasn't arguing about the earth's shape. That's wrong."
- **Good approach (fill the gap):** "Columbus wasn't arguing about shape. He was arguing about SIZE. Here's what he actually miscalculated: [detailed alternative with sources]"

Every debunking section needs a REPLACEMENT explanation. Don't just say what DIDN'T happen — explain what DID happen and WHY.

### 2. Fact-first headlines

State the correct fact first. Place myth in subordinate clause.

- **Bad:** "Many people think Columbus argued the earth was round against flat-earth priests. That's completely wrong. Actually..."
- **Good:** "The 1486 Council of Salamanca debated the SIZE of the earth, not its shape. While popular culture shows Columbus defending a round earth, the evidence shows..."

Open sections with THE CORRECT FACT, not the myth. Lead with evidence, not refutation.

### 3. Self-affirmation to reduce identity threat

When national identity is tied to a historical myth, corrections trigger defensive motivated reasoning.

**Acknowledge shared values FIRST:**

For nationalist territorial claims:

> "National heritage matters — it shapes who we are. That's exactly why getting the historical record right is so important. When we look at the 1899 treaty documents..."

For ideological myths:

> "We all want to believe our side fought for the right reasons. I get that. But if we're committed to honest history, we need to look at what the primary sources actually say..."

Start contested sections by affirming the VALUE behind the myth. Separate person from claim. Then pivot to facts. Never mock the belief; critique the evidence.

### 4. Toulmin model for explicit argumentation

Make logical bridges EXPLICIT. Don't leave warrants unstated.

The model: **Claim → Evidence → Warrant (the logical bridge — OFTEN UNSTATED, make it explicit!) → Backing.**

- **Bad (implicit warrant):** "Columbus thought the earth was 20,400 miles around. The scholars at Salamanca said 24,901 miles. They were right."
- **Good (explicit warrant):** "Columbus thought the earth was 20,400 miles around. The scholars at Salamanca calculated 24,901 miles. **The key principle here is that** sovereignty decisions in 1486 were based on navigational feasibility, not theology. If the ocean was that wide, his ships couldn't carry enough supplies. **That's why** the commission rejected him — not ignorance, but better math."

After presenting evidence, add: "The key principle here is..." or "That's why..." or "What this means is..."

### 5. Seixas Big Six — historical thinking concepts

Use as invisible scaffolding. Don't say "Now I'm going to explain historical significance." Just DO IT.

- **Historical Significance:** Don't just tell what happened — explain WHY it matters. "This isn't just about one border. It set the legal precedent for how [consequence for many people over long time]."
- **Evidence (Sourcing & Corroboration):** WHO created the source, WHEN, FOR WHAT PURPOSE. Show 2+ sources corroborating.
- **Continuity & Change:** What stayed the same? What changed? At what pace?
- **Cause & Consequence:** Move beyond "great man" personalization. "Three structural factors drove this..."
- **Historical Perspective (Avoid Presentism):** Treat the past as a "foreign country" with its own norms. "By 1486 standards, this wasn't controversial."
- **Ethical Dimension:** Make moral judgments while understanding context. "We can condemn the genocide while recognizing the colonial framework made it systemically possible."

### 6. Source credibility suspicion (expose vested interests)

When debunking, explain WHY the myth was created.

**Flat earth example:** Don't just say "Irving made it up." Explain: "Irving needed a villain for his Columbus hero story. Draper needed ammunition against the Catholic Church after Papal Infallibility. White needed to legitimize secular education. Each had a REASON to propagate the myth."

Identify WHO benefits. Explain WHEN and WHY they created/propagated. Show the myth's UTILITY (political, cultural, identity).

### 7. KISS principle (avoid overkill backfire)

Too many counter-arguments backfire. Simple myths are more "cognitively attractive" than complex corrections.

**Rule: 3 KEY POINTS MAX per section.**

- **Bad (overkill):** "Here are twelve reasons the claim is wrong: 1) Treaty violation 2) Court precedent 3) Population data..."
- **Good (lean):** "Three pieces of evidence matter: 1) The 1899 treaty explicitly set the border here. 2) The 1966 independence referendum confirmed it. 3) The ICJ ruled on this exact question in 2007. Everything else is noise."

Identify your 3 STRONGEST pieces of evidence. Cut the rest. Quality of evidence > quantity.

## 4.3 Concede-pivot and steelmanning

Covered in detail in 2.5. Quick reference:

- Concede valid points before rebutting: "That's fair. But..."
- Steelman BEFORE analysis (Devereaux pattern, not in the middle).
- "Defenders might argue..." (replaces "If we are being fair").
- "Three possible explanations" framework when uncertain.
- Grain of truth acknowledgment (Atun-Shei) for myths with some basis.

## 4.4 Identity-stake awareness

Not all viewers are equally resistant. Anticipate identity stakes in your topic.

### Low identity stake topics

Ancient civilizations (Egypt, Rome, Greece), medieval history in general, scientific discoveries.

**Approach:** Teach freely. Complexity is welcomed. Viewers want to learn.

### Medium identity stake topics

Colonial history (but not "their" colony), historical figures (but not national heroes), economic systems in theory.

**Approach:** Lead with evidence, acknowledge complexity, show multiple perspectives.

### High identity stake topics

Territorial disputes involving viewer's nation, national founding myths, religious narratives tied to identity, ideological movements viewer participates in.

**Approach:**

1. **Self-affirmation FIRST:** Acknowledge shared value.
2. **Procedural fairness:** Show you examined BOTH sides' evidence.
3. **Source transparency:** Make methodology visible.
4. **Historiographic gaze:** Treat competing narratives as artifacts to study, not sides to pick.

**Example — high stake opening:**

> "If you're from [Country X], this history is deeply personal. I get that. Your schools taught one version. [Country Y]'s schools taught another. Rather than picking a side, I'm going to show you the documents both sides cite, and walk through what they actually say. By the end, you'll have the evidence to make your own assessment."

### When to use this framework

- **Always:** territorial dispute videos, national founding myth debunking, ideological narrative corrections, colonial history reframing.
- **Optional:** ancient history, scientific discoveries, medieval Europe (unless religious).

The rule: if the myth is tied to GROUP IDENTITY, this framework is mandatory.

## 4.5 Forbidden debunking framings

- **Conspiracy framing without documentation.** Don't claim hidden cabals or secret coordination unless you have the documents.
- **Absolutist language.** "X is occupying Y." "Z destroyed the culture." Use evidence-graded language.
- **"Both sides" false balance** when the documentary record clearly shows asymmetry.
- **Mocking the belief.** Critique the evidence. Never mock people for holding the myth.
- **Single-cause explanations** where multi-cause is more accurate (see 2.6).
