# PART 7 — TOOLKIT (newsletter workshop)

Patterns extracted from human-written articles. Used in the article workshop draft-critique-rewrite loop.

## 7.1 Patterns extracted from human-written articles

Source: NotebookLM Article Workshop notebook (2026-04-14). 19 real human articles. Authors: Patrick Wyman, Bret Devereaux, Anton Howes, David Graeber, Erik Hoel, Freddie deBoer, Scott Sayare, Tim Urban, Skip Hollandsworth, Laura Preston, Eula Biss, Linda Kinstler, Summer Hammond, plus Lopate anthology (Fitzgerald, Didion, Sanders, Orwell, Suleri, Baldwin, White, Woolf, Hazlitt, Dillard, Selzer, Rodriguez, Chesterton, Benjamin, Rich, Mencken, McCarthy, Lamb) + Best American Essays 2025.

### Sentence rhythm patterns (additional)

**Long-Long-Short (Time-Collapse):** Flowing sentences that mimic duration, then a short sentence that collapses it.

> "In the shallows, the dark, water-soaked sticks and twigs, smooth and old, were undulating in clusters on the bottom against the clean ribbed sand, and the track of the mussel was plain. A school of minnows swam by, each minnow with its small individual shadow, doubling the attendance, so clear and sharp in the sunlight. [...] There had been no years."
> — E.B. White

**Voice-fit:** HIGH. Long sentences accumulate evidence; short sentence delivers verdict. The rhythm IS the argument.

**Staccato for Catalog/Accumulation:** Ultra-short fragments that overwhelm through sheer volume. (See 6.7 "Accumulation/Catalog.") Use for lists of consequences, not lists of names.

**Flowing Compound for Momentum:** Voice-fit LOW. Massive cascading sentences (Woolf-style) too literary for the Calm Prosecutor.

### Closings (additional, beyond 6.6)

**The Image That Lingers:** End on a concrete, visual moment with no summary or interpretation.

> "Often I think I detect a glimmer of recognition in Bourgoin's eyes, but it is instantaneously suppressed, or shunted aside, giving way to a blank and slightly quizzical stare. More than anything, he appears to be astonished."
> — Sayare

No interpretation means the reader must provide their own. The image stays in their head. **Template:** [Final concrete image or detail from the sources]. [No commentary. No thesis restatement. Stop.]

**The Ironic Reframe:** A twist that recasts the entire essay.

> "Owing to the press coverage of the case, we all know very well that he is 'X.' And he himself is unlikely to forget it."
> — Linda Kinstler

Perfect for myth-busting format. End by showing that the myth-makers accidentally proved the opposite of what they intended.

### Structural moves (article-specific)

**The Counterintuitive Reframe (full version):** State a widely accepted truth. Invert it. (Already covered in 6.5.)

**Steelmanning Before Critique (Devereaux):** Move steelmanning to the TOP of each section, not the middle.

**The Necker Cube Flip (Biss):** Take an ordinary observation and abruptly shift the moral or philosophical scale.

> "A white woman came along the path with a child on a tricycle, trailing glittering red streamers from the handles, spangled by sunlight. I looked through the woman's smile into her skull, and thought, How do you live with yourself? The answer came to me instantaneously. She lives with herself, my mind told me, in exactly the same way you do."
> — Eula Biss

The reader is invited to judge, then caught in their own judgment. The moral frame flips from "them" to "us" in one sentence.

**The Digression That Earns Its Place (Hazlitt):** Wander away from the main thread. Return with something that reframes the whole essay. The digression must change how the reader understands the main thread when they return.

### Word choice (additional)

**Verbs That Carry Physical Weight:** Replace generic verbs with ones that create visceral images.

> "...the carnivore knife lips the blood, turning in it in a kind of ecstasy of plenty, a gluttony after the long fast."
> — Richard Selzer

Voice-fit MEDIUM. One or two physically weighted verbs per article.

**Technical Terms Made Casual (Devereaux):** Translate jargon into modern slang without losing precision.

> "I suspect that Gundabad didn't just have a really good gym, juice bar, and supply of PEDs."
> — Bret Devereaux

Devereaux is the closest voice-match — academic depth, casual delivery, mechanism-focused.

**Metaphors from Everyday Life (Hoel, Louis):** Anchor abstract systems in physical experiences everyone shares.

> "It's not morally legitimate for the exact same reasons that polluting a river for a competitive edge is not legitimate."

> "For more than ten years we lived with a steady decline that we could no more arrest than we could rope a glacier."

Use physical metaphors for SYSTEMS, not just measurements.

**Unexpected Word Substitutions (Selzer, Didion):** Voice-fit LOW. Too literary. But ONE unexpected substitution per article, placed at a key moment, can be the line readers remember.

### Top 5 highest-impact techniques (ranked by voice-fit + frequency)

1. **The Verdict That Reframes** — power comes from REFRAMING, not brevity. (See 1.4 / 6.4.)
2. **The Question Bridge** — end sections with 2-3 specific, investigative questions. The next section answers them. No "Let's look at..." needed.
3. **Research Narration** — expand first-person from "I learned X" to "I called/searched/read X and here's what happened."
4. **Steelmanning Before Analysis** — move steelmanning to the TOP of each section.
5. **The Scale Flip Transition** — End system sections with a verdict. Start human sections with a name. Delete the bridge.

### Closest voice-match writers

1. **Bret Devereaux** — academic depth + casual delivery + mechanism-focused + treats reader as peer + steelmans before critique + "we" naturally + makes technical terms accessible. **Gap:** more playful, more metadiscourse than Calm Prosecutor allows.
2. **Patrick Wyman** — personal grounding of abstract themes + concrete physical details + systems over events + verdict closings + Anglo-Saxon word choice. **Gap:** more autobiographical than channel allows.
3. **Anton Howes** — history newsletter format + primary source quotes + evidence-as-narrative + named individuals from footnotes + casual academic tone. **Gap:** more purely historical, less confrontational.
4. **David Graeber** — counterintuitive reframes + "everything you know is backwards" structure + verdict sentences + uses establishment sources against the establishment. **Gap:** more polemical and ideological. But the STRUCTURAL technique (use the establishment's own documents to disprove the establishment's narrative) is channel DNA.

### The single biggest correction to the original rules

**Distinguish between two kinds of metadiscourse:**

1. **Performative metadiscourse** ("In this article, we will explore...") — genuinely useless filler. Kill it.
2. **Functional metadiscourse** ("We're going to look at three problems with this treaty") — essential navigation for dense material. Audience reads evidence-heavy content. They need signposts.

**Fix:** "Kill on sight" → "Kill unless the phrase does genuine navigational work in a complex argument shift." This is what Pinker actually recommends, what Devereaux actually does, and what the audience actually needs.
