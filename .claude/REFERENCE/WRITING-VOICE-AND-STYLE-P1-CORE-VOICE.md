## 1.2 Word choice (simpler verbs, casual precision, drop intensifiers)

### Prefer simpler verbs, but don't strip vivid ones

| AI default | Creator's version |
|------------|-------------------|
| "represent" | "are" |
| "gained significant traction" | "became quite popular" |
| "resulted in the partitioning of" | "divided" |
| "discovered a collection of" | "found a bunch of" |
| "prohibited" | "forbade" |
| "predating" | "comes before" |
| "revealed no geological scarring" | "show that there is no geological scarring" |
| "never existed as a physical landmass" | "was never actually there" |
| "the implementation of the treaty" | "the kings struggled to draw the line" |
| "the establishment of consular courts" | "Britain set up its own courts" |
| "the expulsion of the Jesuits" | "Pombal expelled the Jesuits" |
| "the utilization of enslaved labor" | "they enslaved millions" |

**Rule:** If a simpler verb exists AND the simpler version loses nothing, use it. But don't strip vivid or precise verbs just because a plainer word exists — "obliterating" is fine when the intensity is earned. The goal is casual precision, not vocabulary flattening.

**Exception:** Don't over-simplify when the original phrasing does argumentative work. "Not randomly inventing a large number" is better than "not making it up" because "randomly" and "large number" set up the contrast with the careful counting method that follows. Simplify the verb, keep the specificity.

### Casual precision

The creator is precise about facts but casual about language. Combine accurate content with relaxed phrasing.

| AI default | Creator's version |
|------------|-------------------|
| "significantly more valuable" | "a lot more valuable" |
| "a collection of documents" | "a bunch of documents" |
| "gained significant traction" | "became quite popular" |
| "unique legal status" | "crazy status" |
| "military dictatorships" | "dictatorships" (when context is clear) |

**Rule:** Drop intensifiers like "significantly", "fundamentally", "notably". Replace with "quite", "actually", "a lot more". These are the natural hedges.

### Anglo-Saxon over Latinate

Prefer short, blunt, earthy words. Short words are strong words.

- "Japanese surrenders." not "The capitulation of the Japanese military forces."
- "Sales cratered." not "Revenue experienced a significant decline."
- "I quit." not "I tendered my resignation."
- "They lied." hits harder than "They misrepresented."

When choosing between a Latinate and Anglo-Saxon word, pick the Anglo-Saxon unless the Latinate word is a genuine term of art.

### Verbs that carry physical weight

One or two physically weighted verbs per article ("the treaty carved," "the census erased," "the border severed") add texture without breaking voice. Before publishing, scan each paragraph for generic verbs (was, had, made, got, put). Replace ONE per paragraph with something physically specific.

### Trust-killing hedge words

`a bit`, `sort of`, `rather`, `in a sense`, `to some extent`, `it could be argued`, `essentially`, `basically`, `kind of`. The prosecutor doesn't say the evidence is "a bit conclusive." But for genuinely uncertain claims, hedging is appropriate — match the language to actual confidence level.

### Formal connectors that sound stiff when spoken

`Nevertheless`, `Subsequently`, `With regard to`, `Moreover`, `Furthermore` (only OK occasionally as a direct evidence-add), `It should be noted`. "However" is permitted 2-3x per script — the creator uses it naturally.

### Em dashes in newsletter prose (anti-AI tactical choice)

Em dashes may be used **sparingly (2-3 per article max)** for conversational rhythm and parenthetical asides. Excessive use reads as AI-generated. This is a platform perception rule, not a writing craft principle — Pinker, Bryson, and Mukherjee all use em dashes freely. Mandatory use: blockquote attributions only. Use commas, periods, colons, parentheses instead.

## 1.4 Sentence rhythm and the stumble test

> **EXPLANATORY DEFAULT (`/voice-discovery` 2026-06-05 — canonical: `VOICE-PROFILE.md`):** This creator leans **more explanatory than the staccato/8-word-gavel emphasis below suggests** — that emphasis is over-applied to him. His default is a **plain, complete, *explaining* sentence**; the short punch is rare and reserved for a clean *landing*; even turns can breathe. His signature shape is **flowing parallel clauses → em-dash → a verdict that carries the catch** ("…fielded their own armies — everything a state does except the one thing they never managed: unite"). He picks the **dash-compound or full-sentence verdict over the staccato two-beat every time** ("That's not a tribe — that's a functioning state," NOT "That is not a tribe. That is a state."). Read the staccato/fragment guidance below as *available rare devices*, not the baseline texture. **BUT the lean has a hard ceiling — bar-talk, not prose.** His governing image is "me talking to people in a bar": plain, simple explanation, historical integrity intact, *tight.* He flags "too much prose," over-citing, and clunky transitions as standing problems, and picks the *tighter* paragraph when offered. So explanatory ≠ wordy. **When in doubt: explain the mechanism plainly like you're telling a smart friend in a bar — then cut everything that isn't that.** Never staccato-gimmick; never purple prose; the target is the lean middle.

### The Stumble Test

Scripts are read aloud via teleprompter. They must sound natural when spoken. If a line would make a presenter stumble, pause awkwardly, or mentally translate written prose into speech, rewrite it. The "Would I say this?" Test: would you actually say this out loud explaining to a friend? If not, rewrite.

### Clarity over length

AI tends to pack too much into dense, passive compound sentences. The fix is clarity, not mechanical splitting.

- **AI (too dense):** "The borders established during this three-month conference were drawn using rivers, latitude lines, and other geographic markers, with minimal consideration for existing ethnic, linguistic, or cultural boundaries."
- **Creator (clearer):** "The borders drawn at this conference — they were mainly based on latitude lines and other geographic markers. They didn't consider existing ethnic, linguistic or cultural boundaries."

**Rule:** Split when a sentence is genuinely confusing or packs unrelated ideas. But longer sentences are fine when they build momentum — "5 September 1492, Columbus accidentally stumbled upon an unknown continent and the clash was about to begin" works as one sentence because the ideas flow together.

**Anti-pattern:** Don't over-fragment into staccato one-to-three-word sentences for dramatic effect. "One source. Written 40 years after the fact. From oral testimony." is four fragments doing one idea's job. Combine when the beats belong together: "One source, written 40 years after the fact, based on oral testimony."

### Vary sentence length deliberately

4+ consecutive sentences of similar length = monotone. Mix long mechanisms with short punches. Long sentences carry evidence; short sentences carry verdicts. (This matches the creator's *unscripted* rhythm — long causal sentences that accumulate, then a short, plain close.)

> **Script (creator):** the "land" is usually a **plain short sentence or a dash-compound verdict that carries the catch** ("That's not a tribe — that's a functioning state"), NOT a clipped fragment or a two-beat gavel. Vary length, yes — but the short landing is a *full sentence*, not staccato. See VOICE-PROFILE.md. **Article:** the short-punch / fragment landing below is freely available.

**The Provost principle, observed across Bryson, Pinker, Harari, Wyman:**

> Write short sentences. Write medium-length sentences. But also write sentences that carry the reader forward through cascading clauses that build and build until the accumulated weight of the evidence demands a conclusion that can only be delivered in the shortest possible form. Like this.

- **Build** with medium/long sentences (evidence, context, nuance).
- **Land** with short ones (verdict, insight, reframe).
- The short sentence is where the insight lives.

### The verdict punch (after complex explanations, land the catch)

> **Script (creator) — leads here:** after a complex explanation, land **what it meant / why it matters**, in a **plain full sentence or a dash-compound verdict** — *not* a clipped ≤8-word gavel. His signature is flowing parallel clauses → em-dash → a verdict that carries the catch ("…everything a state does except the one thing they never managed: unite"). The ≤8-word hammer below is a **rare device reserved for a clean landing**, never the default texture (the staccato/gavel emphasis is over-applied to him — see VOICE-PROFILE.md). **Article:** the ≤8-word gavel is a standard, freely-available move (see "The 8-Word Verdict" below).

The principle is **consequence-driven**: never just state the fact, land what it meant. The verdict's power comes from **reframing**, not brevity (see the refinement under "The 8-Word Verdict" below).

| Long explanation | Verdict (script: full/dash-compound preferred · article: gavel OK) |
|------------------|---------------|
| "Both sides quietly dropped the joint survey. They'd divided the planet using a measurement that didn't exist yet." | (already rhythmic) |
| Long legal analysis of intertemporal law, British Foreign Jurisdiction Act, administrative overreach... | "Britain's overreach became its legal weapon." |
| "A British surveyor estimated 157 miles through dense jungle, costing around 145 pounds, around 18 million in today's money." | "Britain never built it." |

**Bryson's law (article-side, rare device script-side):** if you use a decimal point, the next sentence must be a hammer under 5 words.

### Fragments for emphasis (rare landing device, not the default)

> **Script (creator):** the **default is full, complete sentences.** A fragment is a **rare** device — reserved for a proper-noun reveal landing ("…Saddam Hussein.") or a single isolated beat. **Never fragment triplets**, never compressed-prose fragments. "Same pattern. Different players." is the **Same X. Different Y.** flourish family the creator explicitly cuts (see `/polish` category 1 + 6) — keep it out of scripts. A lone "Britain never built it." after a long build is fine; three fragments in a row is not.

USE a fragment (sparingly) for a clean landing:

- "They won."
- "Zero."
- "Britain never built it."
- "Not 'declared.' Restored."

DON'T use fragments for compressed prose, and DON'T stack them ("Same pattern. Different players." / "Not 1916. Fifteen years." read as the staccato-flourish family — combine into a flowing sentence):

| Wrong (compressed prose) | Right (flowing sentence) |
|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| "A sitting judge. 1963. No valid union." | "A sitting judge ruled in 1963 that there was no valid union." |
| "Population: 4.5 million. GDP: $2.1 billion." | "Somaliland has a population of 4.5 million and a GDP of $2.1 billion." |

### Historical present tense (1-2 key moments per script)

For 1-2 key moments per script, shift from past to present tense to make history feel live.

| Past (standard) | Historical Present (impact) |
|-----------------|---------------------------|
| "In 1494, two countries drew a line..." | "It's 1494. Two countries draw a line through a world they've never mapped." |

Signal back to past tense with a status phrase: "That was [year]. [Consequence]."

> **REFINEMENT (`/voice-discovery` 2026-06-05):** The creator confirmed historical present **only for a single sharp dramatic MOMENT** — a confrontation, a signing, an action beat ("It's June 1923. The delegate stands up and says…"). He **rejects** present tense for **decade/era scene-setting** ("Go back to the 1970s. The Kurds are in open revolt…") — period framing stays past tense. The test is **moment (present OK) vs. era-framing (past only).**

### The 8-Word Verdict (cross-medium, especially article)

Every long build of evidence must land on a verdict sentence short enough to feel like a gavel strike:

- **"They left."** (Pinker/Wilkerson, collapsing centuries of migration)
- **"They are inventing a myth."** (Graeber)
- **"All life is one."** (Bryson, 3.8 billion years in four words)
- **"Care, not cure."** (Mukherjee, redefining medicine)
- **"There is no justice in history."** (Harari)
- **"The map was a chokehold."** (History vs Hype)
- **"He was blind."** (History vs Hype)
- **"The ink was enough."** (History vs Hype)

**Important refinement:** The verdict's power comes from REFRAMING, not brevity. "Bourgoin was in fact a fan" works because it recategorizes everything before it — not because it's short. Stop counting words. Start asking: does the verdict change the reader's relationship to the evidence they just read? Best verdicts are 5-15 words and include a concrete image or mechanism, not just an abstraction.

### Antecedent clarity (article-side)

Script-side: every "this/that/it/they" must have an unambiguous referent in the immediately prior sentence. Articles relax this — readers can re-read, scripts can't. But the rule still bites at **paragraph-break boundaries**: when a sentence after a paragraph break opens with "This..." or "That decision..." or "It...", the reader's eye has just reset. The referent must be either (a) named in the new sentence, or (b) clearly the last subject of the prior paragraph.

*Anti-pattern:* Paragraph ends with "Pakenham, Hochschild, and Anghie all reach the same conclusion." Next paragraph opens "That changes how we read the General Act." — Which "that"? The fact of agreement, or one of the three scholars' specific arguments? Fixed: "Their shared conclusion changes how we read the General Act."

### Anaphora (named rhythm)

Anaphora: three or more full sentences with a parallel opening stem, building accumulating emphasis. NOT staccato — staccato prohibits fragment triplets. Anaphora is full grammatical sentences. Use anaphora when the parallel structure IS the argument — when the repetition makes the cumulative case more directly than synthesis would.

*Example:* "He wrote rules that required a doctor in the room. He wrote rules that made confessions extracted under torture inadmissible. He wrote rules that said no one could be tortured twice for the same evidence."

Same frequency cap as all rhythm devices: max once per article, used as a deliberate device, not a default.

### Frequency cap (meta-principle for all rhythm devices)

Any rhetorical or emphasis device — staccato, anaphora, declarative two-beat, parallel question, em-dash compound, blockquote-then-decoder, "I knew X but Y" first-person move, named-rhythm beat — used 3+ times in a single article becomes filler unless it's a deliberate refrain. Devices lose force with repetition. The article-writer Rule 11A flags one specific instance ("three I-knew-X-but-Y with identical rhythm"), but the principle is general: vary the palette. If you notice yourself reaching for the same device a third time, switch.

## 1.5 Defining terms

### Pattern: `[Technical term] — [plain language definition in same breath]`

| Wrong | Right |
|-------|-------|
| "The principle is called estoppel." | "The principle is called estoppel — a legal rule that says you can't benefit from an agreement for decades, then suddenly claim it never existed." |
| "The Basij beat protesters." | "The government responds with the Basij — volunteer militias loyal to the Supreme Leader." |
| "The bonyads control 40%." | "The bonyads — religious foundations that operate like massive conglomerates — control huge chunks of the economy." |
| "Bathymetric surveys" | "Bathymetric surveys — these are ocean floor scans" |

**Key phrases for explanations:** "In simpler terms...", "Translation:", "The Court is saying:", "In other words..."

### Define at first mention, not at point of use

When a term needs explaining, define it where the concept FIRST appears, not later when it's used in a specific claim. Don't patch a missing definition with a parenthetical at the usage point — move the definition earlier so both passages read clean.

- **Bad:** "Mexico got 62% of the Western Polygon, roughly 5,092 square nautical miles. (The treaty calls it the 'Western Polygon,' but it's the same wedge of seabed.)"
- **Good:** 'This is called the "donut hole" — the treaty calls it the Western Polygon.' [first mention] → "Mexico got 62% of the Western Polygon" [later use, no explanation needed]

**Rule:** Define early, use cleanly later. Keep definitions to ~8 words max — an appended clause, not an explanatory aside.

### Use judgment on what to define

Don't define words everyone knows (treaty, colony, border). The goal is accessibility, not hand-holding. Define terms that would lose a wider audience.

### Terms that always need introduction

- **Historical/political:** Dynasty names (Qajar, Pahlavi), treaty names (Anglo-Russian Convention), political movements (National Front, Tudeh), military units (Cossack Brigade, IRGC, Basij)
- **Economic:** Concession names (D'Arcy), organizations (bonyads, AIOC), economic terms (nationalization vs confiscation)
- **Legal/constitutional:** Constitutional concepts (Velayat-e Faqih), institutions (Guardian Council, Majlis), legal terms (bast, estoppel)
- **People:** Every historical figure on first mention. Include role, time period, why they matter.

### Introduce cultural references by function, not location

When referencing something the viewer might know (a quote, a symbol, a document), explain what people USE it for before listing where it appears. Function before form.

- **Bad:** "It's on bumper stickers, rifle grips, t-shirts. Supposedly said by King Leonidas..."
- **Good:** "It's used as an illustration of Spartan defiance — the moment King Leonidas supposedly told... You'll find it on bumper stickers, rifle grips, t-shirts."

This applies to **titles and epithets** too. Don't drop a title and assume the viewer knows what it means — explain what someone DID to earn it before naming it.

- **Bad:** "Ancient critics called him the 'Father of History.' They also called him the 'Father of Lies.'"
- **Good:** "He's considered the first person to systematically collect evidence and try to write down what actually happened — that's why ancient critics called him the 'Father of History.' They also called him the 'Father of Lies' — because he regularly includes things that are clearly exaggerated or impossible."

### Abbreviation expansion

Full name on first use, abbreviation thereafter.

- "The AU responded" → "The African Union responded" (first use) → "The AU said..." (later)
- "The ICJ ruled" → "The International Court of Justice ruled" (first use) → "The ICJ ruled..." (later)

## 1.6 Hedging vs confident assertion

The Calm Prosecutor reaches verdicts. Strip hedge words on confident claims, but match language to actual confidence level on genuinely uncertain claims.

| Wrong (over-hedged) | Right (confident) |
|---------------------|--------------------|
| "This suggests..." (when evidence shows) | "The truth is..." / "The reality is..." |
| "It appears that..." (when documented) | "[Historian name] found..." |
| "Some historians believe..." (when consensus exists) | "[Historian] documents..." / "The consensus is..." |
| "One might say..." | (cut entirely; just say it) |

**Rule:** Confident assertions when the evidence is clear. Genuine hedging only when the evidence is genuinely ambiguous. Don't perform certainty you don't have, but don't manufacture uncertainty out of academic politeness.

### Epistemic humility — the credibility wall-off

Admit what you DON'T know → then deliver what you DO know. The contrast makes definitive claims land harder.

| Instead of (omniscient) | Use (wall-off) |
|-------------------------|----------------|
| "The delegates chose this phrasing because..." | "I can't tell you exactly why the delegates chose this phrasing — the archives are incomplete. What I CAN tell you is what the phrase meant in international law." |
| "The evidence clearly demonstrates..." | "All these options are just speculation. However, what IS clear from the evidence is..." |
| "The reason the treaty failed was..." | "We don't fully understand why this negotiation collapsed. But we DO know what happened next." |

**Key distinction:** admitting the EVIDENCE has limits = evidence honesty (good). Admitting YOU haven't done the research = research failure (bad — never ship "I haven't looked into this"; either look into it, or don't make the claim).

**Frequency:** 1-2 per script. Place where evidence is genuinely speculative — distinguish between "the evidence shows X" (definitive) and "my reading is X" (interpretation).

### Confession of uncertainty (article-side)

Acknowledge what the evidence doesn't prove. "We're not sure" (Harari). "It's a bit of a puzzle" (Bryson). This builds more trust than performing certainty.

## 1.7 Active voice and direct address

### Active voice (80/20 target)

Make people/nations the subject. Passive is permitted ONLY for systemic critique — making the system the villain.

- **AI:** "this law was not enacted under German pressure"
- **Creator:** "The Germans didn't pressure the French into drafting this law"

- **AI:** "the treaty was signed by the Shah"
- **Creator:** "the Shah signed"

**Permitted passive:** "Evacuations were only recommended" (system blame) — the system IS the actor.

**Avoid nominalization** — "the [noun] of" is almost always weaker than an active verb (see 1.2 table for examples).

### Direct address ("you," not "one")

| Wrong | Right |
|-------|-------|
| "One might wonder..." | "You might wonder..." |
| "Many people believe..." | "You might have heard..." |

The reader/viewer is a smart friend who doesn't have the specific data. Use "we" and "you" throughout, not just in designated personal sections.

### "You" walk-throughs for mechanisms

When explaining HOW something works, use second person and walk through it step by step:

> "You basically throw a piece of rope overboard with something heavy at the end. You start counting the time and you count how much of the rope actually gets unraveled. Then you multiply the distance of the rope by the time you've been sailing and you then approximately know how much distance you have traveled."

**Rule:** Technical explanations = "you [do X], you [see Y], you then [know Z]". Make the viewer do it in their head.

### Contractions

| Default | Emphasis |
|---------|----------|
| "It's not about oil" | "It is NOT about oil" |
| "They didn't care" | "They did NOT care" |
| "wasn't" not "was not" | Expand only for emphasis |

Use contractions throughout. Expand only for emphasis.

## 1.8 Cognitive patterns (how the creator processes prose)

Observed 2026-04-01. Each pattern has direct implications for draft quality.

### Distinction-first thinking

The creator naturally splits concepts that look similar but aren't. "Checked" vs "verified." "Divided" vs "split." "Marine data" vs "ocean floor surveys." A word that blurs two different ideas will get flagged.

**Implication:** Never use a word that collapses a meaningful distinction. Before writing a verdict or summary, ask: is this word precise enough, or does it merge two things the creator would separate? When in doubt, use the more specific term even if it's longer.

### Audience-position reading

Every critique comes from the reader's chair, not the writer's. "This is not explained." "Comes out of nowhere." "Too many short sentences."

**Implication:** Every draft should pass a "cold reader" test. Any concept, name, number, or term that a reader hits before they have context to understand it — if a reader would pause and think "wait, what's that?" — it needs setup.

### Iterative precision

The creator doesn't arrive at ideas in one shot. They circle: rough idea → correction → refinement → landing. Each pass gets more logically precise. The final iteration is closest to intent but still shorthand.

**Implication:** When the creator offers multiple passes at a phrase, don't take the first OR the last version literally. Extract the idea that survived all iterations — that's what they actually mean. The last version shows direction; your job is to write the publishable form.

### Systems over events

The creator's instinct is to land on the mechanism, not the outcome. "Nobody found the island" is an event. "Cartographic trust was never stress-tested until money was on the line" is a system. The channel's DNA (HOW > WHY) shows up in how the creator evaluates closers and verdicts.

**Implication:** Verdict sentences and closers should name the SYSTEM that failed or operated, not just what happened. "France went further" (event) is weaker than "The student became the model" (system reversal). Always ask: what's the mechanism?

### Literal-truth scrutiny

The creator holds their own prose to the same evidence standard as the subjects they cover. "Nobody checked" gets flagged because people DID check the map — they read it and copied it. They just never verified the source data. Rhetorically effective but literally false = rejected.

**Implication:** Every verdict sentence must survive literal reading. If a reader could respond "well actually that's not quite true," rewrite it. Rhetorical punch must come FROM precision, not at the expense of it.

### Creator shorthand = direction, not copy

When the creator suggests phrasing during a humanify or editing pass, treat it as the IDEA to express, not text to paste in. The creator struggles to write out their thoughts. Their suggestions are directional — extract the core idea and rewrite it to publication standard.

- **Bad:** Creator says "everyone trusted the map. Nobody bothered to verify its information until geopolitical interests were at stake." → paste verbatim into the article.
- **Good:** Extract the idea (blind trust → only verified when stakes forced it) → "And for 382 years, every cartographer trusted the last one's work. It took billions of barrels of oil to make someone finally sail out and look."

**Rule:** Never paste creator shorthand directly into a draft. Take the direction, write it properly.

### The script must survive its own methodology

If a video is about source criticism, the script cannot treat unsourced popular narratives as established fact. If a video debunks oversimplification, the script cannot oversimplify. Every claim in the script should pass the same standard the video applies to its subject matter.
