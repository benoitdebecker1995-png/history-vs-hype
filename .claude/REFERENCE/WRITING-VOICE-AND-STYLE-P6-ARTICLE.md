# PART 6 — ARTICLE WRITING (article-side)

For newsletter articles. Persona: same Calm Prosecutor as scripts (1.1) — emotionally low, intellectually high. Present evidence and reach final verdicts. No hype.

## 6.1 Article-specific voice differences from script

The voice is the same. The medium constraints differ.

| Dimension | Script | Article |
|-----------|--------|---------|
| Reader/viewer attention | Audio + visual; ad-libs add 32% | Pure text; first sentence does heavy lifting |
| Sentence rhythm | Optimized for read-aloud cadence | Optimized for visual rhythm + skim-ability |
| Em dashes | Free use | 2-3 per article max (anti-AI tactical) |
| Document reveal | `[B-ROLL: document]` + read-aloud | Blockquote the document text |
| Structural defaults | Hook → context → turn → close | SCQA pyramid (verdict first) |
| First-person | Sparing in scripts | Wider toolkit (5 modes — see 6.8) |
| Length per "section" | Topic-dependent (see 3.3) | 350-word soft cap, 500-word hard cap |
| Closings | Image that lingers / universal application | Loop / Micro-tragedy / Unsettling Question |

## 6.2 The 10 article rules

The Calm Prosecutor's Ten Rules. Tape these next to your screen.

1. **Direct the reader's gaze toward concrete evidence** rather than your own professional activities. (Pinker)
2. **Expose "moral confusion"** by identifying where historical actors held two contradictory beliefs simultaneously to justify underlying violence. (Graeber)
3. **Push the chain of historical causation back to "ultimate explanations"** like geography rather than stopping at proximate human decisions. (Diamond)
4. **Identify the "imagined orders" and shared fictions** that allowed historical strangers to cooperate on a massive scale. (Harari)
5. **Anchor abstract geopolitical revisions in the sensory memory of a single physical artifact**, like a map or an heirloom. (Frankopan)
6. **"Unburden the story" of human suffering before moving into analysis**, treating the naming of an event as a literary act first. (Mukherjee)
7. **Use "revealing metaphors" to shock the reader into a new way of seeing** facts buried in dry, technical literature. (Dawkins)
8. **Collapse the complexity of a long evidence dump with a punchy "verdict sentence"** of eight words or less (or longer if it reframes — see 1.4). (Bryson)
9. **Distinguish between "long-term trends" and "immediate drama"** to neutralize the reader's instinct to over-dramatize history. (Rosling)
10. **Overcome the "Curse of Knowledge"** by explaining evidence as if your reader is as intelligent as you but happens to lack your specific data. (Pinker)

### Phase 1: Structural Engineering (Minto Pyramid)

**Top-Down Logic:** State the summarizing idea (the verdict) BEFORE the supporting details as a default. Bottom-up storytelling may be used when delaying the verdict creates dramatic irony, massive payoff, or necessary atmospheric grounding. Top-down is the default, not a prison.

If your article is about a 500-year-old treaty causing modern poverty, state the 10% income gap in the first paragraph.

**SCQA Introduction:** Begin every article with:

- **Situation:** Non-controversial anchor (1-2 sentences)
- **Complication:** The tension, the ghost in the data ("but the men who drew it couldn't measure it")
- **Question:** Implicit or explicit
- **Answer:** Your Shiny Dime, the verdict, immediately

Ensures you and reader are "standing in the same place" before you lead them through evidence.

**MECE Support:** All supporting arguments are Mutually Exclusive and Collectively Exhaustive. No overlap, no gaps.

### Phase 2: Visual Packaging (Axios Smart Brevity)

Readers spend an average of 26 seconds. Use visual hierarchy to trap the eye.

**The Core 4:**

1. **Muscular tease:** Subject line of 6 words or fewer.
2. **One strong lede:** First sentence tells the reader something they don't know.
3. **Axiom anchors:** Bold headings like **Why it matters:**, **The big picture:**, **By the numbers:** for skimmers.
4. **Go deeper:** Historical nuance AFTER the decision point.

**The 350-Word Rule:** No section should exceed 350 words (warn). Over 500 = fail. If a section has three data points, use bullets, not "blobs of text."

### Phase 3: Style Scrub (Pinker + Zinsser)

**Classic Style (Pinker):** Treat prose as a "window onto the world." Reader and writer are equals looking at a scene together. Eliminate metadiscourse unless the phrase does genuine navigational work.

**Test:** Would you say this to a companion standing next to you at the crime scene? You wouldn't say "I'm going to show you something interesting." You'd point at the blood.

**Kill zombie nouns (Zinsser):** Convert nominalizations back into active verbs. (See 1.2 table.)

**One-syllable power:** Anglo-Saxon over Latinate. (See 1.2.)

**Strip qualifiers:** Hedge words on confident claims (`a bit`, `sort of`, `rather`, `in a sense`, `to some extent`, `it could be argued`). Match language to actual confidence level.

### Author Voice qualities (non-negotiable)

1. **Pattern-Thinking, Not Timeline-Thinking** — every event connects to something the reader already knows. Entry points are comparisons, not definitions.
2. **Skepticism Before Excitement** — first instinct with a surprising finding is to verify it. Joy comes after confirmation. Write the discovery journey: I assumed X, I checked, evidence says Y.
3. **The Complexifier, Not the Debunker** — instinct isn't "that's wrong." It's "that's incomplete." Adds nuance rather than tearing down.
4. **Teacher's Instinct** — always thinking about what the reader will remember tomorrow, not just understand now. "The most important part is..." "If you remember only one thing..."
5. **Honest About Limitations** — never pretend certainty. "We have to keep in mind..." "History is never 100%." Attribution before controversy.
6. **Plain Words, Academic Rigor** — "Solidified" not "ossified." Don't simplify the argument. Simplify the words carrying it.
7. **Inclusive Address** — "You know..." "Most people think..." "We" and "you" throughout, not just in personal sections.
8. **The "I Knew X, But Y" Signature** — natural structure for evidence challenging assumptions. Setup with what was known, pivot with "but," land on what evidence showed. Use 3-4x per article, vary syntax (see 6.8 for 5 first-person modes). The "full setup" version ("I knew X. But Y.") max once per article.

## 6.3 The 9 opening techniques

Default has been SCQA for 5/6 articles. Actively rotate through these to build variety.

### Author's Default: Related Precedent

Start with something the reader already knows, then connect it to the article's topic.

- "Why does Brazil speak Portuguese? The answer starts with a headwind."
- "Did you know the nine-dash line works on the same principle as a treaty from 1494?"

### Technique 1: The Disarming Definition (Harari)

> "About 70,000 years ago, organisms belonging to the species Homo sapiens started to form even more elaborate structures called cultures."

**When:** the concept needs anchoring at the largest possible scale.

### Technique 2: The Necker Cube Flip (Pinker)

> "Writing is an unnatural act."

**When:** the reframe IS the opening.

### Technique 3: The Cosmic Context (Harari)

> "14 billion years ago, matter, energy, time and space came into being."

**When:** the zoom-out creates productive disorientation.

### Technique 4: The Categorical Comparison (Harari)

> "There were humans long before there was history."

**When:** what's missing from the record is the point.

### Technique 5: Confirming Popular Myths (Rosling)

Validate the reader's wrong belief, then demolish it.

**When:** myth-busting articles.

### Technique 6: The Micro-Tragedy (Mukherjee)

Start with one REAL person on the day everything changed.

**When:** a named individual exists in the sources. (Crusades opening with Nicetas.)

### Technique 7: Direct Existential Address (Bryson)

> "Welcome. And congratulations."

**When:** the reader is an heir to the event.

### Technique 8: The Provocative Quote / Authority Pivot (Graeber)

> "Back in the 1930s, Henry Ford is supposed to have remarked that it was a good thing that most Americans didn't know how banking really works, because if they did, 'there'd be a revolution before tomorrow morning'."

The quote does two jobs — borrows credibility AND sets up the reframe.

**Voice-fit:** HIGH. Channel's competitive advantage is primary source quotes with page numbers. Opening with one is the strongest possible signal of what follows.

### Technique 9: The In Medias Res Object (Wyman)

> "This is a foundational text of American Bro Culture: two large men shooting the shit about lifting weights..."

The reader is disoriented by the unexpected object, then hooked by the writer's confident assertion that this trivial thing is actually foundational. Curiosity gap without clickbait.

**Voice-fit:** HIGH. Perfect for document-reveal articles. Open with the artifact (the treaty, the map, the census page) before explaining what it means.

### Additional opening techniques (lower voice-fit, use sparingly)

**Autobiographical Micro-Scene (Wyman):** Drop reader into a specific memory with sensory details. "For the first eighteen years of my life, I lived in the very center of Washington state, in a city called Yakima."

**Startling Confession (Sanders, Suleri):** Blunt, vulnerable, or darkly absurd admission in the first sentence. Voice-fit MEDIUM — works when the confession is intellectual, not emotional. Template: "I [assumed/expected/thought] [wrong thing]. [Short sentence that shows why.]"

**Journalistic Date-Stamp (Urban):** Hyper-specific date + cultural touchstones. "June 18, 2016. Obama was president, the Cavs were on their way to beating the Warriors..." Adds texture SCQA openings lack.

**Deep-Time Framing (Romines):** Voice-fit LOW. Use sparingly — only when deep time IS the mechanism (tectonic plates creating borders, ancient trade routes).

## 6.4 Verdict sentences

Every long build of evidence must land on a verdict sentence that feels like a gavel strike. (Full theory in 1.4.) Examples:

- **"They left."** (Pinker/Wilkerson, collapsing centuries of migration)
- **"They are inventing a myth."** (Graeber)
- **"All life is one."** (Bryson, 3.8 billion years in four words)
- **"Care, not cure."** (Mukherjee, redefining medicine)
- **"There is no justice in history."** (Harari)
- **"The map was a chokehold."** (History vs Hype)
- **"He was blind."** (History vs Hype)
- **"The ink was enough."** (History vs Hype)
- **"Bourgoin was in fact a fan."** (Sayare — recategorizes everything before it)

**Mechanical rule:** Long sentences carry evidence. Short sentences carry verdicts. Never 4+ consecutive sentences of similar length.

**Refined rule:** Stop counting words. The verdict's power comes from REFRAMING. Best verdicts are 5-15 words and include a concrete image or mechanism, not just an abstraction.

## 6.5 Anti-slop checklist

Use these deliberately in every article. Anti-slop ingredients AI cannot replicate.

### 1. Detection of Moral Dissonance (Graeber)

Identify the felt social tension where humans hold two contradictory beliefs simultaneously.

> "Most everywhere, one finds that the majority of human beings hold simultaneously that (1) paying back money one has borrowed is a simple matter of morality, and (2) anyone in the habit of lending money is evil."

### 2. The Discovery Journey (author's own)

Document the transition from "I already knew" to "but the evidence changed how I think about it." This mirrors how the reader should process the information. AI can't fake genuine surprise at primary sources.

### 3. Pre-emptive Ethical Strike (Diamond)

Identify the moral anxiety a reader might feel and disarm it with empathy BEFORE it becomes a barrier.

> "In case this question immediately makes you shudder at the thought that you are about to read a racist treatise, you aren't."

### 4. Physical Experience Anchoring (author's own)

Ground abstract history in places visited, documents read, comment sections witnessed. Not "history shows" but "when I walked through Santa Marta" or "the comment section on my Belize video."

### 5. References to Prior Work

The author has a body of work (videos, research, reader interactions). Mentioning it proves the newsletter comes from a person, not a content mill. At least 2 references per article.

### 6. Imaginative Mourning for the Void (Harari)

Specific, poignant examples of people and events that MUST have existed but were lost to history.

> "The foragers may have had their all-conquering Napoleons who ruled empires half the size of Luxembourg; gifted Beethovens who lacked symphony orchestras..."

### 7. The Confession of Uncertainty

Acknowledge what the evidence doesn't prove. "We're not sure" (Harari). "It's a bit of a puzzle" (Bryson). Builds more trust than performing certainty.

### The Counterintuitive Reframe (one per article)

Every article needs one "I never thought about it that way" moment. Test it:

> "Most readers assume _____, but the evidence shows _____."

If you can't fill the first blank with a REAL belief readers hold, the reframe is an observation, not an inversion. Observations inform. Inversions transform.

| Author | The Reframe | Sentences needed |
|--------|-------------|-----------------|
| Graeber | Barter didn't come first, credit did. Standard history is precisely backwards. | 4 |
| Rosling | Chimps score better than educated humans on global development questions. | 3 |
| Diamond | Necessity is NOT the mother of invention; invention is the mother of necessity. | 3 |
| Harari | The Agricultural Revolution was "history's biggest fraud," more people alive under worse conditions. | 3 |
| Frankopan | The Mediterranean is not the center of civilization. Central Asia is. | 2 |
| Mukherjee | Cancer is not an invader. It's a distorted version of our normal selves. | 2 |
| Dawkins | Organisms don't use genes to reproduce. Genes use organisms to survive. | 3 |
| Pinker | Bad writing comes from expertise, not stupidity: the Curse of Knowledge. | 3 |
| Bryson | Your atoms don't know you exist. Yet they keep you alive anyway. | 5 |

**Pattern:** 2-4 sentences is the sweet spot. State the assumption. Invert it. Land the verdict.

### Real names only (HARD rule — repeated from 2.7)

Real name with source = powerful. Invented name or scene = slop. If no real name exists, the data alone is enough. Never fill the gap with fiction.

## 6.6 Article-specific patterns (exemplars)

### The Mukherjee Anchor (Micro-Tragedy)

Ground abstract claims in a visceral human moment. A kindergarten teacher waking up with a headache. A convict abandoned on a beach to prove a line is real. Human cost arrives BEFORE the analysis.

### The Harari/Graeber Pivot (Imagined Order)

Identify the shared fiction (the treaty, the border, the currency) and expose the moral confusion inherent in the historical act.

### The Bryson Scale (Narrative Data Transformation)

Never present a number without a spatial analogy. Don't say "1872 census data"; describe the "heavy paper ledger" being opened by a sweating official. After every decimal point, a hammer sentence of 5 words or less.

### The Diamond Chain (Ultimate Causes)

Push the chain of causation back to geography or biology. (See 2.4.)

### Evidence-as-Narrative — five models

**1. The Hiss of the Universe (Bryson):** Instead of "Penzias and Wilson discovered cosmic background radiation," tell the story of them scrubbing bird shit off an antenna for a year before realizing the noise WAS the discovery.

**2. The Carpet Salesman (Mukherjee):** Instead of "Papanicolaou developed the Pap smear," tell the story of a failed carpet salesman assigned to study guinea pig menstrual cycles.

**3. The Academic Gossip (Harari):** Instead of "Language evolved for social bonding," ask whether physics professors discuss quarks at lunch (they gossip about the dean).

**4. The Salt and Stars (Bryson):** Instead of "Bob Evans discovered many supernovae," make the reader imagine 1,500 dining tables covered in salt, then finding one extra grain.

**5. The Ghost Who Discussed Credit (Graeber):** Instead of "Madagascan culture projects monetary concepts onto the past," tell the story of interviewing a ghost who only wanted to talk about money.

**Pinker's law:** If you name the researcher before the finding, you've failed.
**Howes's law:** If a paragraph explains a system, rewrite it to follow a person surviving that system.
**Bryson's law:** If you use a decimal point, the next sentence must be a hammer under 5 words.

### Four conversion rules

| If you're writing... | Do this instead |
|---------------------|-----------------|
| A system/mechanism | Follow a PERSON surviving that system. "Duarte Coelho liquidated his life." |
| A study/paper | Lead with the finding as felt experience. Name the researcher AFTER the punch. |
| A papal bull/law | Treat the institution as a character with moral contradictions. "The Vatican became the back office for a slave monopoly." |
| A navigation technique | Follow a terrified human. "A Portuguese captain didn't sail home; he fled west." |

### Three grounded endings (kill the TED Talk leap)

Stay at the crime scene. Never trade the precise lethality of your evidence for speculative escalation.

**1. The Loop (Graeber):** Return to the opening artifact or person. Close the circle. "The ink was enough."

**2. The Micro-Tragedy (Mukherjee/Bryson):** Return to a named person who represents the system. "We no longer leave convicts on beaches. We are still living in the architecture they were left to build. The line is still there."

**3. The Unsettling Question (Howes/Harari):** Ground it in evidence, not speculation. "The question is whether we can ever uninstall it."

**NEVER:** Jump from specific evidence (Brazilian census data) to speculative futures (moon bases, asteroid mining). That's rhetorical whiplash. Unearned escalation.

### The Calm Prosecutor's Cheat Sheet

1. **Verdict First:** Give the answer in the first paragraph.
2. **No Signposting:** Point at the tree; don't say you're about to point at it.
3. **The 8-Word Rule:** Collapse long evidence builds with a verdict of 8 words or less (refined — see 1.4).
4. **The Axiom Anchor:** Bold headings like **Why it matters:** for skimmers.
5. **Ultimate Causes:** Push causation back to geography or biology.
6. **Unburden the Story:** Tell the human tragedy before you analyze the data.
7. **Kill the AI Voice:** Strip all hedging, all "tapestries." Present facts as undeniable verdicts.
8. **The CRIBS Audit:** Is it Confusing, Repeated, Interesting, Boring, or Surprising? (See 6.9.)
9. **Audience First:** Imagine a smart, busy friend at a bar. Don't bore them.
10. **The Shiny Dime:** Hyper-focus on the smallest viable idea that breaks a reader's worldview.

## 6.7 Section bridges, scale flips, accumulation

Bridges connect the end of one section to the start of the next. The reader should feel a conversation, not channel-surfing.

### 1. "Actual Instance" (Preferred)

Treat two time periods or subtopics as instances of the same phenomenon. Don't telegraph the outcome — let the reader discover it.

**Example:** "The data doesn't make the history more tragic. It makes it harder to look away from. In 2023, the Vatican tried to look at it directly." → next section reveals they failed.

### 2. "Silent Pattern" Wallop

End a historical section by naming a quiet pattern. Open the next with data that proves it.

**Example:** "The line was imaginary, unenforceable, and routinely ignored. It should have disappeared. It didn't." → next section: economist's numbers.

### 3. Cause-Effect Handoff

End with a consequence, open with the mechanism. One sentence. No drama.

**Example:** "The ones that survived needed labor. And Portugal already had the supply chain."

### 4. Scale Flip (Harari's "Necker Cube")

End a small story, open by scaling the concept to civilization-level.

**Example (Harari):** Monkey steals banana → "Ever since the Cognitive Revolution, Sapiens have thus been living in a dual reality."

### 5. The Suspense Ellipsis (Hoel) — use sparingly

Leave the final sentence grammatically incomplete. The reader HAS to cross the section break.

> "No one knows the effects, and no one appears to care. Especially not the companies, because..."
> [Section break]
> "**OpenAI has happily allowed pollution.**"

**Voice-fit:** LOW for "Calm Prosecutor." But ONE per article could work as a strategic escalation before a major reveal.

### 6. Question Bridge (Devereaux)

Close an abstract point, pivot to direct questions that ground the next section.

> "This unpredictable 'play of probabilities' is (part of) what Clausewitz terms friction... So we should start asking questions: why is the initial battle on the Isen Ford?"

The questions shift the reader from passive absorption to active investigation. Specific, investigative — no metadiscourse smell.

### 7. Scale Flip — Macro to Micro (Aziza)

End at the widest possible lens. Open the next section tightly focused on one person experiencing the consequences.

> "It has proved to itself that the red line does not exist."
> [Section break]
> "Bisan Owda's daily dispatches are a time lapse of a young woman pushed from shock and sorrow toward a shattered rage."

Cinematic zoom from geopolitical abstraction to one human face creates emotional whiplash that makes both scales more powerful. **Action:** End system sections with a verdict. Start human sections with a name. Delete the bridge sentence.

### 8. Parallel Character Pivot (Sayare)

End one person's arc. Hard cut to a new character with a structural parallel. The reader feels the parallel without being told.

### 9. Metaphorical Handoff (Dillard)

End with an abstract concept. Open the next with a physical manifestation. (Voice-fit MEDIUM for newsletters.)

### Anti-Pattern: The Signposted Disappointment

"So when X happened, you'd think Y would change." This tells the reader what to conclude before they see the evidence. Cut the signpost. Let evidence land on its own.

### Accumulation/Catalog

Stack rapid-fire details to overwhelm. End with one sentence that gives the catalog meaning.

> "Peter. Carlos. Gary. Asel. Perry. Nikas. Healthy snapshots accompany each annal. At the Russian River. By the Christmas tree. Lifting a beer. In uniform."
> — Richard Rodriguez, "Late Victorians"

**Voice-fit:** MEDIUM. Works for listing consequences of a colonial decision (tribes split, languages lost, trade routes severed) but risks violating "narrate, don't list" rule. Use when the sheer number IS the point.

## 6.8 First-person usage (5 modes)

The "I Knew X, But Y" signature is one variant. Real articles show a richer toolkit.

### 5A. Research Narration ("I" as detective)

Narrate the LOGISTICS of how you found the evidence.

> "When I called Bourgoin on an old number I'd been given, mostly as a pro forma exercise, to be able to say that I'd attempted but failed to interview him. To my surprise, he answered."
> — Scott Sayare

**Template:** I [research action, casually described]. [Unexpected result]. [What it revealed.]

### 5B. Reader Co-Investigation ("we" as peers)

> "We're going to discuss how historically plausible each sequence of events is and, in the process, talk a fair bit more about how pre-gunpowder warfare works."
> — Bret Devereaux

This is functional metadiscourse. It's inclusive rather than performative. Works specifically when you're about to walk through evidence together.

**Template:** We're going to [specific investigative action] and in the process, [what the reader will learn].

### 5C. Opinion Delivery ("I" as authority)

Use first person to launch a confident, combative thesis.

> "Let me confess at once that his elegy filled me with great astonishment."
> — H.L. Mencken

After a long evidence build, a first-person verdict ("I don't buy it" or "I find this more convincing") is powerful precisely because it's rare in your writing.

**Template:** [After evidence build]: I [strong verb: don't buy / find unconvincing / think this misses] [the claim]. [One sentence of why.]

### 5D. Confessional Vulnerability ("I" as flawed)

Admit personal failings or uncertainty to earn trust. Voice-fit LOW for scripts, MEDIUM for newsletters. The intellectual version: "We don't know" / "The evidence is unclear" / "I changed my mind about this."

### 5E. Real-Time Reaction ("I" as nervous system)

Voice-fit LOW. Diluted version works in newsletters: "The first time I read this passage, I had to re-read it three times" — showing the human encountering the evidence.

### "I Knew X, But Y" — full setup version

Use 3-4x per article, varying syntax. The "full setup" version ("I knew X. But Y.") max once per article.

## 6.9 The CRIBS audit

Before outputting, check every passage:

| Letter | Question | Action |
|--------|----------|--------|
| **C** | Confusing? | Rewrite for the intelligent non-expert |
| **R** | Repeated? | Cut the weaker version |
| **I** | Interesting? | Keep, but verify it earns its place |
| **B** | Boring? | Delete or rewrite as narrative |
| **S** | Surprising? | Double down. This is where the reader leans in. |

**Perell POP rule:** Trust primary sources. "Children above deck, brazilwood below" needs no feature-style tricks. "If this is the ONLY thing the reader sees, is it exactly what I want to stick?"

### Transition Vocabulary (article-side)

**Author's natural connectors (use these first):**

- "On top of that..."
- "At the same time..."
- "This brings us to..."

**From Harari/Pinker (use when they fit):**

- "In fact..." (pivots from myth to truth)
- "The upshot is..." (collapses data into verdict)
- "Consider for example..." (abstract to visceral)
- "To be fair..." (steelman/counter-evidence)
- "What was the secret of success?" (question as bridge)

**NEVER use:** academic fog (`Consequently`, `Thereby`, `Moreover`, `Furthermore`), performative metadiscourse (`Now we turn to...`, `Let's look at...`), filler (`It is worth noting that...`), telegraphing (`So when X happened, you'd think Y`).

## 6.10 Script-to-article conversion

| Script element | Article equivalent |
|---------------|-------------------|
| `[B-ROLL: document]` | Blockquote the document text |
| `[B-ROLL: map/graph]` | Describe the shape/pattern in words |
| `[TALKING HEAD]` | Delete |
| `{Retention trigger}` | Delete |
| `// Production note` | Delete |
| Timestamps | Delete |
| "Here on screen" | Describe what it shows, or blockquote |
| "In this video" | Delete |
| Act breaks `## ACT 1:` | Curiosity-generating subheading |
| Staccato triples | Keep only if earning a verdict. Otherwise merge into flowing prose. |

**Critical:** Stripping tags is 10% of the conversion. The other 90% is rebuilding connective tissue, rewriting evidence as narrative, restructuring from bottom-up to top-down, and cutting all metadiscourse. If you only strip tags, you have failed.
