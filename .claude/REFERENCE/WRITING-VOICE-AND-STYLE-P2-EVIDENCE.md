# PART 2 — EVIDENCE AS NARRATIVE (cross-medium)

## 2.1 Real quotes with page numbers

The competitive advantage. Word-for-word from academic sources, with page numbers.

### Quote attribution patterns

| Pattern | Example |
|---------|---------|
| Attribution-First | "According to Chris Wickham in *Medieval Europe*, page 147..." |
| Claim-Then-Source | "The Carolingians produced ten times more manuscripts. That's Buringh and Van Zanden's analysis." |
| Block Quote Display | "Here's what [document] actually says:" [DOCUMENT on screen] |
| Historian as Witness | "As the historian John LeDonne described it: '[exact quote]'" |
| Reading Directly | "Reading directly from the letter: '[exact quote]'" |
| Finding-before-researcher | "In the Revolutionary period, oblivions proliferated in the colonies, as the legal scholar Bernadette Meyler has documented." |

### Spoken introduction (script-side)

**Wrong:** "Quote: 'Sudan is defined as...'"
**Right:** "The treaty says Sudan is defined as..."

**Wrong:** "The AU's position: 'Firmly rejects...'"
**Right:** "The African Union firmly rejected any initiative..."

### Pinker's law (article-side)

If you name the researcher before the finding, you've failed. Lead with the historical fact. Tag the academic credential AFTER the punch.

**Sometimes the researcher CAN lead — when the researcher IS the surprise.** Don't apply mechanically; apply when the researcher's name is the point.

## 2.2 Primary sources on screen / quoted in prose

**Script:** Documents shown as B-roll, not just described. Add `[SHOW: document]` markers for editor.

**Article:** Blockquote the document text. Describe shape/pattern of maps and graphs in words.

### Read verbatim + immediate translation

**Pattern:**

```
Here's what [document] actually says:
[SHOW / blockquote: exact text in original language or formal text]
"[Exact quote]"

Translation: [Plain language explanation of what this means].
```

**Example:**

> Here's what the 1825 ordinance actually says:
> [SHOW: French text on screen]
> "Les habitants actuels de la partie française de Saint-Domingue verseront à la caisse fédérale des dépôts..."
>
> Translation: The current inhabitants shall pay into the federal deposit fund...
> Notice the language: "current inhabitants." Not "the state of Haiti." They made it personal.

**Use for:** EVERY primary source shown on screen. This is the channel's core differentiator — amplify it.

### Notice this specific phrase (close-reading)

**Formula:** [Document context] → "Notice this specific phrase." → [Quote exact text] → "That [word/date] isn't random." → [Explain significance]

**Example (Belize):**

> Notice this specific phrase in the treaty. 'Boundaries that existed previous to and on the first day of January 1850.' That date isn't random. By dating the boundary to January 1st 1850, the day before the Clayton-Bulwer treaty banned new acquisitions, Britain could tell Washington: We're not acquiring new territory. We're just recognizing boundaries that already existed.

### Translation skepticism (Knowing Better pattern)

```
"These aren't his own words because his real name wasn't [English name] — it was [original name]."
"Now we have to translate it."
"It takes some linguistic gymnastics to get from [original] to [translation]."
"They picked the absolute worst most biased translation to quote."
"The [language] translation of his journals doesn't have the word [X] at all."
"When people say 'from Columbus's own journals' what they really mean is from one specific 1892 English translation of the 1530 transcripts originally written 40 years earlier."
```

### Source verification — direct challenge

```
"Let's take a look at the facts and see if we can't figure out which ones have been misunderstood, misattributed, exaggerated, and sometimes straight-up fabricated."
"What do you notice about this quote? How about the fact that it's neither the beginning nor the end of the sentence."
"In full context, the word [X] could mean [A] or [B] or [C]."
"So we're gonna have to look it up. And here it is."
```

**Script (creator) — leads here:** state the **plain number.** He is the *anti*-RealLifeLore: he chose the plain figure even on twelve million (Atlantic slave trade), over both a comparison and a repeat-for-weight. Comparison is **essentially off** in scripts — reach for one only in the genuinely rare case where the scale is otherwise impossible to picture *and* the comparison earns its place. ✅ "More than thirty million Kurds, spread across Turkey, Iran, Iraq and Syria." ❌ "…roughly the population of Canada." (See VOICE-PROFILE.md "Numbers / scale".)

**Article — the rest of this section applies freely:** never leave a number abstract; compare it to something the reader can picture. The patterns below are article-side guidance.

| AI default | Article version |
|------------|-------------------|
| "an island of Bermeja's reported size" | "an island the size of Birmingham" |
| "approximately 229 ethnic groups" | "229 ethnic groups — that's nearly the entire population of an average country, divided" |
| "a one-hundred-mile-long structure" | "a continuous, one-hundred-mile-long, mirror-clad structure called the Line that will bisect a desert tract the size of Massachusetts" |

**Rule (article):** When mentioning a size, distance, population, or quantity, consider adding a comparison to something familiar. Not every number needs one — use comparisons when the scale is genuinely hard to grasp, not as a default reflex. **(Script: default to the plain number; the comparison is the exception, not the reflex.)**

Match comparisons to the AUDIENCE. The UK/DE/CA/US audience needs European and North American comparisons.

### Time comparisons must do argumentative work

Don't just name a century ("that's like looking back at the 1500s"). Name a specific event everyone knows, AND use the comparison to reinforce the point you're making:

- **Bad:** "430 years — that's like looking back at the 1500s"
- **Better:** "430 years — that's like looking back at Columbus reaching the Americas"
- **Best:** Set up the implication and let the viewer connect the dots. "There's still a lot of mythos surrounding those events, even with modern standards."

The comparison should illustrate AND argue. Make it do double duty. But DON'T spell out the conclusion — set up the implication.

### Size/time comparison anchors (RealLifeLore patterns)

- "The contiguous territory of the Russian Federation is nearly the same size as South America."
- "More in just seven months of fighting than the Soviets suffered throughout nearly a decade of war in Afghanistan."
- "Occupied by Spain for around 500 years, a longer time than some cities in mainland Spain like Barcelona, which used to be briefly a part of France."
- "The Great Pyramid had been the tallest man-made structure for more than 3,700 years."

### Specific measurements as authority markers

**Cross-medium (script + article) — this is about precision, not comparison.** A plain, *specific* number is the creator's instinct ("370 kilometers as the crow flies," not "about 400km"); the script-side override above is on the comparison *reflex*, not on precision. Specific numbers create authority.

- "The border right here that separates the two countries is only **80 meters wide**, which makes it the shortest international border anywhere in the world."
- "There are **78,000 people** that live here, surrounded by a **6-meter high** border fence."
- "Separated by more than **370 kilometers** as the crow flies from the rest of the country."
- "For nearly all of the **684 years** of history before the city and the Oblast became Russian it was largely German."

"About 400 kilometers" < "370 kilometers as the crow flies."

### Immediate scale contextualization (Historia Civilis)

After stating any number, immediately contextualize with casual language:

- "He commanded 10 legions, which was a lot."
- "The 13th consisted of approximately 2,500 soldiers — at half strength."
- "They had been friends for 14 years. Probably longer."

Prevents dry data dumps. Makes dense information feel conversational.

## 2.4 Causal chains (consequently / thereby / which meant that)

**The Calm Prosecutor's signature.** Every causal claim must explain the mechanism, not just state correlation.

### The connectors

**Default to spoken-register connectors — this is a video, not an essay.** A viewer should hear a person reasoning out loud, not a paper being read. The causal *rigor* (always explain the mechanism) never relaxes; only the connector word should be conversational.

**Fluent / spoken (default — reach for these first):**
- **"so"** — the workhorse cause-effect connector in speech
- **"which is why"** — ties effect back to cause naturally
- **"and that meant"** / **"which meant that"** — implication of prior event
- **"because... and because"** — multiple causes leading to effect
- **"here's what that did"** / **"and the result"** — sets up the consequence
- **"that's how"** — summarizing causal outcome

**Formal (for variation only — never the default, hard cap 1-2 per script):**
- **"consequently"** — direct cause-effect
- **"thereby"** — mechanism by which effect occurred
- **"as a result"** / **"this resulted in"** — general causal connector

> **Frequency discipline:** any single connector used 3+ times reads as filler (Rule 39 frequency cap). "Consequently" three times in three sentences — as in the Kraut examples below — is a *citation* of the technique, not a *model* to copy verbatim. Vary the connector; keep the mechanism.

### Examples (Kraut, "Origins of Russian Authoritarianism")

> "The Mongols enforced their economic order upon the remaining Russian principality states, and the center of Russian political and economic power **consequently** shifted to the landlocked plains of Smolensk, Rostov, and Chernigov."

> "Landlords **consequently** started competing for the remaining peasants by offering them payment for work."

> "By controlling the entire market they also determined the price, and **consequently** kept the population drunk on a constant mass supply of cheap and widely available vodka."

> "The power structure of the Russian princely states **thereby** became heavily centralized within the power of a singular ruler figure of a state."

> "**Because** that aristocracy had a lot to lose if they failed to appease their Mongol overlords, **and because** the Mongols had brought no legal code of limited aristocratic power, the Russian princes extracted that wealth with increasing unopposed ruthlessness and brutality."

### The mechanism explanation (don't state effect; explain HOW)

- **Weak (effect only):** "Vodka made the population controllable."
- **Strong (mechanism explained):** "A peasant who wastes his money on vodka is too drunk to think of his situation and how he got into it. Too drunk to organize any protest against his circumstances. So drunk he beats his kids into being drunks like him. And so drunk he beats his wife into submission. In the end, that peasant is a peasant in service of ensuring the security of the regime that keeps him drunk."

### Fill in the WHY (creator's instinct)

- **AI:** (just states the 1902 border change)
- **Creator:** "It would explain why they redrew it — because the Hala'ib Triangle is a lot more valuable"

After stating what happened, add WHY it happened. Also: when citing biographical details about a historical figure, explain HOW we know those details (name the source). Don't present ancient biography as established fact without attribution.

### Explain implications (after every major fact, answer "Why does this matter?")

- "That's how [adjective] X was."
- "Which meant..."
- "In other words..."
- "The implication:"

**Example (bad):** "Ninety-six deputies turned up, including several who had promised the Shah they would stay away. Every one voted in favor of nationalization." → Why does it matter that they promised to stay away?

**Example (good):** "Ninety-six deputies turned up, including several who had promised the Shah they would stay away. Every one voted in favor of nationalization. Even deputies who had promised the Shah they would boycott showed up and voted yes. That's how popular nationalization was. Every single deputy. Unanimous."

### Diamond chain (ultimate causes — article-side)

Push the chain of causation back to geography or biology. "Why did Europeans colonize the Americas and not vice versa?" is more powerful than "What happened when Europeans arrived?" The article should always reach the deepest causal layer available.

### Minimum standards for every script

- ≥3 explicit causal connectors
- ≥1 comparative analysis ("While in X... in Y")
- ≥1 mechanism explanation (HOW something worked, not just THAT it happened)

## 2.5 Intellectual honesty (concede what's right)

### Concede before rebutting

- "That's fair. But..."
- "I think that's true. That's fair. I think a lot of the time [concession]. But I think it's important to remember that [rebuttal]."
- "Sure [valid point]. But [important caveat]."
- "Right off the bat this isn't really an argument for [X], just for the idea that [Y]."

Before any major rebuttal, show you understand the other side. Makes critique more powerful.

### Steelmanning patterns

The creator's natural pattern: **anticipate viewer resistance → zoom out to broader principle → come back to the specific case.**

> "You might think that even after so much evidence, there must be something there. However, this is not how cartography works in a broader sense. Sometimes a lot of people just repeat what they've heard before. In this case, that repeating happened for over 500 years."

**Pattern:** "You might think [viewer's instinct]" → "However, this is not how [field] works" → broader principle → back to the specific → landing line.

### Defenders might argue (replaces "If we are being fair")

> "Defenders of the regime might argue that 70% of Jews in France survived. That's an important statistic. It's important to give the other side credit and look at their strongest argument — and then say why I don't believe it, or if it's more open, just admit they have a good point and there's no conclusive answer."

**Pattern:** "Defenders of [X] might argue [strongest point]" → "That's an important [stat/point]" → counter with evidence OR openly admit no conclusion.

### Strongest version first

```
The strongest version of their argument goes like this:
[Present their best case charitably]

Here's why it still doesn't hold up: [rebuttal with evidence].
```

### Both sides get credit

```
Side A is correct that [valid point A].
Side B is correct that [valid point B].
Where both go wrong is [the actual issue with evidence].
```

### Grain of truth acknowledgment (Atun-Shei)

For myths that have some basis in fact:

> "As with any mythology, there is a grain of truth to the Lost Cause. When they say slavery was portrayed unfairly, what they're often complaining about is how every slaveholder in media is depicted as cartoonishly evil — even though the slave codes of the time prohibited much of what's shown."

Shows you understand WHY the myth exists, not just THAT it's wrong.

### Personal interpretation marker (Historia Civilis)

For distinguishing your analysis from pure fact:

> "Some historians have described this as a callous reaction to the loss of a friend. But I don't see it that way at all. To my eyes, it's a small act of compassion — 'Sure, follow your conscience if you must, but you're going to need your stuff.'"

Shows intellectual honesty — you're interpreting, not just reporting.

### Steelman BEFORE analysis (Devereaux pattern)

Move steelmanning to the TOP of each section, not the middle. Address the strongest counter-argument before presenting your own. Prevents the reader from building resistance.

### Admit uncertainty / flag expertise limits

- "I'm not really the right person to ask about this, maybe, because [reason]."
- "I'm genuinely not sure about this next one."
- "I don't know about this. Let me know what you think."
- "Church patristics is really not my area. I don't know very much here."

When discussing topics outside core expertise, or when genuinely uncertain, flag it. Paradoxically increases credibility.

## 2.6 Anti-oversimplification

Reject single-cause explanations. Multi-cause framing wins.

### Multi-cause attribution (Shaun pattern)

> "The bombs were dropped for a combination of many reasons and let's list some of them. First... in part to limit Soviet influence in Asia... Next... in part to force an end to the war without the United States being seen to back down from unconditional surrender... Another possible reason... was to justify their expense... Another motivation surely was revenge... Related to that last point, another motivation that influenced the use of the bombs was just basic regular racism..."

When explaining decisions with multiple motivations, resist simplification. List all factors with evidence for each.

### Reject single-cause (Fall of Civilizations)

```
"It wasn't just [X]. It was [X], [Y], and [Z] happening simultaneously."
```

### Three-factor enumeration (Kraut)

```
"There are three main factors that led to this.
First, [factor A]. [Explanation].
Second, [factor B]. [Explanation].
Third, [factor C]. [Explanation]."
```

### "Two things happened" (Knowing Better)

```
"After [event], two things happened:
One — [consequence A].
Two — [consequence B]."
```

After any major historical event. Forces clarity and creates structure.

### Three possible explanations framework (Shaun)

When evidence doesn't definitively prove one interpretation:

```
"Now [observation that needs explanation].
There are [number] possible explanations that I can imagine.
The first is that [explanation A]...
The second is that [explanation B]...
The third is that [explanation C]...
If I was forced to guess, I'd say [assessment], but I do not know for certain."
```

## 2.7 Attribution and named individuals

### Numbers need source names

- **AI:** "approximately 229 ethnic groups found themselves divided"
- **Creator:** "According to a study by [researcher], 229 ethnic groups were divided"

The viewer should know WHO counted, not just the count.

### Real names only (HARD rule for articles)

- **Real name with source = powerful** (Mukherjee's Carla Reed, Diamond's Yali, Graeber's Father Graeme).
- **Invented name or scene = slop** (fabricated census takers, imagined conversations).
- NotebookLM research habit: always ask for named individuals at the edges of big events — clerks, cartographers, translators, local officials. The names are in the footnotes.
- If no real name exists, the data alone is enough. Never fill the gap with fiction.

### Specific stakeholder quote (script-side)

**Pattern:** [Name] is [credentials/position]. [What they said with exact quote]

**Example (Essequibo):**

> "Lloyd Pereira is the Toshao, that's the chief of Wakapoa village on the Pomeroon River. When Venezuela held a referendum claiming his home, he told Al Jazeera, 'Even as a small boy, I remember hearing Venezuela saying, Essequibo is theirs. But I also know I live in Essequibo. And as an indigenous person, Essequibo is ours.'"

### Credentials before quote (Almada pattern)

For lesser-known sources, build authority first.

**Example:**

> "Jean La Rose is a Lokono woman who won the Goldman Environmental Prize in 2002 for coordinating Guyana's first indigenous land rights lawsuit. In April 2024 she wrote, 'We do not recognize borders. We recognize the river that gets us to one place. We recognize the mountain we must cross to get to one place.'"

### Evidence-as-narrative (follow a person)

Don't cite the evidence — tell the story of someone experiencing it.

**Example (Howes):** "Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, after observing the procedure in the Ottoman Empire, had her daughter inoculated during a 1721 London outbreak."

The reader remembers Lady Mary Wortley Montagu. They would not remember "smallpox inoculation was introduced in the 1720s." Named individuals are memory hooks.

**Howes's law:** if a paragraph explains a system, rewrite it to follow a person surviving that system.

## 2.8 Frame perceptions, then test them

Don't editorialize about groups as if it's established fact. Instead, frame how something IS perceived, then use evidence to affirm or challenge that perception.

- **Less precise:** "Sparta was a militaristic society — fighting to the death is what they were trained for."
- **More precise (perception → evidence):** "This is how we think about Sparta. But the 300 Spartans represented about 4% of Sparta's hoplite force. The 700 Thespians represented one hundred per cent of theirs."

**Rule:** Be as objective as possible. Name the perception ("this is how X is perceived"), then let evidence affirm or disprove it. Stats are powerful tools for this — the numbers carry the contrast without the script making character judgments. But this is about intellectual honesty, not neutrality — the Calm Prosecutor still reaches verdicts; they just earn them with evidence first.

### Trust the viewer to feel the surprise

When a fact is inherently surprising (the famous quote belongs to someone nobody knows), don't add editorial commentary explaining why it's surprising ("most people have never heard of him"). Set up the expectation, deliver the contradiction, and let the viewer connect.

- **Bad:** "Herodotus gives it to Dienekes. Most people have never heard of him."
- **Good:** "Herodotus attributes that quote not to Leonidas, but to a soldier called Dienekes."

The "not X, but Y" structure carries the surprise. Adding commentary over-explains it.
