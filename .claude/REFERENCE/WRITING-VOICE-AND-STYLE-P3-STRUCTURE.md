# PART 3 — STRUCTURE (script-side)

## 3.1 Opening / hook / turn / close

### Core principle: The 5-Second Paradox Hook (Fig Tree style)

The 1:11 Drop-Off Problem: viewers leave when you drift into background context before establishing stakes.

**Rule:** No pure context before you've:
1. Named the specific subject
2. Stated the common belief
3. Delivered the contradiction word ("But...", "Except...")

ALL of this MUST happen in the first 5 seconds. Front-load the paradox.

### Stakes immediate (first 30 seconds)

- **Belize (23K views):** "...whether Belize should even exist"
- **Vance (42.6% retention):** "I fact-checked VP Vance's claims"
- **Pattern:** What's at stake RIGHT NOW, not historical background first.

### First-person ownership early

- "So, I read that treaty." (Belize, ~25 sec)
- "I fact-checked..." (Vance, ~15 sec)

Establish research authority in the first minute.

### Niche-validated hook taxonomy (388 videos, 8 channels)

| Topic Type | Hook Pattern | Niche frequency |
|------------|--------------|-----------------|
| Territorial | **cold_fact** | 26% |
| Ideological / Fact-check | **myth_contradiction** | 26% |
| Untranslated / Document | **specificity_bomb** | 13% |
| General / Loyal audience | contextual_opening | 26% |
| `authority_challenge` | — | 0% (not used in this niche) |

### Channel signature openings

**Both-Extremes-Wrong (your signature):**

```
"[Topic] gets thrown around constantly. But here's the problem.
One side says [Extreme A].
The other insists [Extreme B].
The evidence reveals something more complex."
```

**Document-First (9-10x outlier pattern):**

```
0:00-0:05  [SHOW: Document on screen] "This is the map they ignored."
0:05-0:10  [SHOW: Different document] "This is what they drew instead."
0:10-0:20  [Split-screen comparison] Context sentence.
0:20-0:30  [Modern conflict] "That decision created [consequence]."
```

**"I went to the documents":**

```
"I went to [specific source type]. The actual [treaty/report/letters].
[Archive name] records.
[What I found]."
```

For full hook patterns with retention data, see `OPENING-HOOK-TEMPLATES.md` and `HOOK-PATTERN-LIBRARY.md`.

### The turn (15-25% runtime)

Channel data: the turn lands best at 15-25% of runtime (3.2x performance), NOT at 25-35% (2.1x dead zone).

The turn is the moment the script pivots from "here's what people think" to "here's what the evidence shows." Land it early.

### Closings

The Calm Prosecutor's closings land on real human consequences, not abstract geopolitics. What matters is what happened to actual people — infrastructure gaps, displacement, neglect.

**Good closing instincts:**

- Zoom into real people affected by the system.
- Connect the specific case to a broader pattern ("This isn't the only case").
- End with an image or fact that lingers, not a thesis restatement.

**NOT:**

- "We've looked at [thing] because [reason]" (recap/summary — AI "In conclusion" energy)
- Dramatic final lines
- "And that's the real story of..."

### Return-to-extremes conclusion (your signature)

```
"That first narrative — [Side A claim]? [How evidence complicates it].
But the second narrative — [Side B claim]? [How evidence complicates it].
[Synthesis/what the evidence actually shows]."
```

### Universal application ending

```
"If you see the pattern, you understand something bigger:
This isn't about [narrow framing]. It's about [broader principle].
That story applies way beyond [specific topic]."
```

For full templates, see `CLOSING-SYNTHESIS-TEMPLATES.md`.

## 3.2 The 10 narrative-flow rules

Mandatory for all scripts. Originally from user feedback on Iran scripts.

### Rule 1: Introduce every term before using it

Pattern: `[Term] — [plain language definition in same breath]`. See 1.5 for full list of terms that always need introduction.

### Rule 2: Bridge every transition

Don't jump topics. Every section needs a "why are we talking about this now" sentence.

**Bad:**

```
[End of section about Mossadegh's character]
Here's why oil mattered.
```

→ Random. No connection.

**Good:**

```
[End of section about Mossadegh's character]
Mossadegh's conviction would be tested on one issue above all others: oil.
Here's why it mattered — not just economically, but structurally.
```

→ Bridge connects character to next topic.

**Bridge patterns:**

- "This would be tested on..."
- "And that brings us to..."
- "Which created a problem:"
- "That's the context for what happened next."
- "To understand why this mattered, you need to know..."

> **THESIS-FORWARD RULE (`/voice-discovery` 2026-06-05 — transitions are the creator's #1 standing complaint):** A bridge must be **thesis-forward and hand directly into the next beat's subject** — name where we're going, tightly. Validated forms: thesis-forward ("What comes next is one pattern, on repeat: armed when useful, abandoned when not"); plain causal bridge naming the next subject ("Those states didn't just fade out. Someone took them apart — and it was the empire they'd served for three centuries" — chosen over an abstract question-bridge); or a thesis-pointed question → plain statement. **Two tics to avoid:** (1) vague referents in the bridge ("gets the very first one wrong" → "the very first *what*?"); (2) calling the common story flatly **"wrong"** — prefer **"oversimplified / tells only one part"** (reserve "wrong" for a specific factual error).

### Rule 3: Quotes come AFTER setup

Never drop a quote without context. Set up what the quote will show FIRST. (Full pattern in 3.7.)

### Rule 4: Explain implications

After every major fact, answer "Why does this matter?" (See 2.4.)

### Rule 5: Cut meta-commentary

Reduce "Let me show you" and "Here's what most people miss." Once or twice per script for genuine emphasis at major structural pivots — NOT every few paragraphs. Trust the viewer.

### Rule 6: Cut repetition

Say it once. Say it well. Move on.

**Signs of repetition:**

- Same fact stated twice in different words.
- Quote that just repeats what you said.
- Summary that restates the previous paragraph.

**Fix:** Delete the weaker version. Keep the stronger one.

### Rule 7: Simpler research credibility

Don't oversell your research process.

- **Bad:** "I spent three weeks with 25 academic sources. Primary documents. Declassified cables. Peer-reviewed histories."
- **Good:** "So I did some digging. Read books about the topic. Then went to the primary sources."

The evidence speaks for itself. Overselling sounds defensive.

### Rule 8: Consistent structure for repeated events

When showing a pattern, use parallel structure so the repetition is visible.

**Example (protest timeline):**

```
**2009 — The Green Movement**
- Trigger: Disputed presidential election
- Response: 3 million march
- Mechanism: Basij street violence
- Outcome: 70-110 killed

**2019 — Fuel Prices**
- Trigger: 50-200% price increase
- Response: Protests in 21 cities
- Mechanism: Internet blackout + shoot-to-kill
- Outcome: 300-1,500 killed

**2022 — Mahsa Amini**
- Trigger: Woman dies in custody
- Response: 150 cities, all provinces
- Mechanism: Executions, mass detention
- Outcome: 500+ killed
```

The parallel structure makes the PATTERN visible without saying "see the pattern?"

### Rule 9: Start sections with concrete details

Open with specific date, place, or document — not abstract statements.

| Wrong | Right |
|-------|-------|
| "The situation in Iran was complicated by foreign interference." | "December 1905. Tehran. The governor orders several sugar merchants beaten." |
| "Let's examine the economic factors." | "The concession document specified: 84% of profits to Britain." |

**Opening patterns:** Date + Place + Action / Document on screen + what it shows / Specific number or statistic / Direct quote.

### Rule 10: End with universal application

Connect the specific topic to broader meaning. (See 3.1 closing patterns.)

## 3.3 Pattern interrupts and pacing (90-second rule)

### The 90-Second "Exhibit A" Rule

Channel data and gap analysis show that videos lose viewers if the primary source is delayed. 
**Rule:** The primary source document (Exhibit A) MUST be revealed on screen within the first 90 seconds (10 seconds for Shorts). 

**Pacing Rule:** Every 90 seconds of script must contain at least ONE of:

1. **New evidence** — a quote, document, statistic not yet shown
2. **Modern connection** — why this historical point matters NOW
3. **Pattern interrupt** — visual change, tonal shift, rhetorical question, casual aside

**Pre-filming check:** Read aloud with a timer. Mark any 90-second stretch without a payoff. Cut or restructure before filming.

| Symptom | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| 2+ minutes of background context | Move to after first evidence reveal |
| Long quote block without commentary | Break into setup → quote → implication |
| Historical chronology without stakes | Add "and that decision still affects..." bridge |
| Section that exists "for completeness" | Cut. Completeness ≠ retention. |

### Section length limits (topic-specific)

| Topic Type | Avg section | Max before drop risk | Pattern interrupt every |
|------------|-------------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| Colonial | 57 words | 87 | 42 |
| General | 77 | 152 | 57 |
| Ideological | 75 | 175 | 56 |
| Legal | 77 | 152 | 57 |
| Politician | 52 | 87 | 39 |
| Territorial | 105 | 172 | 78 |

### Casual asides (1-2 per script)

Brief moments that add personality:

- "Yeah, that Kiev, the one that Russia is bombing with missiles."
- "Yeah, that's a punishment where they whip the soles of your feet."

### Zero/None for impact (≥1 per script)

- "Were any Maya leaders consulted? No."
- "How many Maya representatives are involved? Zero."
- "Three claims checked, none held up."

## 3.4 Modern relevance integration

**DATA-BACKED FINDING (42 videos, 4,200 retention data points):** Standalone modern_relevance sections average -0.010 retention delta — worse than narration (-0.005), quotes (-0.008), and primary sources (-0.009). The TRANSITION to/from modern relevance disrupts flow.

**Solution:** Weave modern stakes INTO narration. Don't interrupt with standalone "this matters today" sections.

**All topics:** Maximum gap without modern mention: 150 words. Recommended interval: every 90 words.

### How to integrate (DO)

- "...which meant the border drawn in 1884 now splits 229 ethnic groups — including the Somali, who are still fighting over it in 2026."
- "Mossadegh's nationalization playbook became the template. In 2019, Bolivia tried exactly the same thing."
- "The colonial classification system they created is still the basis for Rwanda's ID cards today."

### How NOT to integrate (DON'T)

- ❌ "And this still matters today. In 2026, we can see..."
- ❌ "Why does this matter? Because right now..."
- ❌ Any standalone paragraph that breaks narration flow.

**The test:** If you can remove the modern reference and the paragraph still makes grammatical sense, it's woven in correctly. If removing it creates a gap in the text, it was a standalone bridge — rewrite it.

### Six categories of history-to-modern bridges

Deployed every ~90 seconds. Vary across these:

1. **Direct question:** "Now, why does this even matter?" / "Are you starting to understand why...?"
2. **"To this day" persistence:** "...the consequences of which we'll still live with to this day" / "...and [country/entity] remains there until this day"
3. **Recurring pattern anchor:** "Here we are again. I told you this will be a recurring theme." (Best for colonial pattern videos)
4. **Narrative/ideological pivot:** "This narrative is being used right now by [specific modern actor]." (Best for myth-busting)
5. **Personal/physical anchor:** "I'm reminded of [historical event] every time I [modern action]."
6. **Universal synthesis:** "There's no monopoly held by any nation on [behavior]."

**HvH defaults by topic type:**

- Territorial/legal: "This [treaty/ruling/border] is still in effect right now."
- Myth-busting: "This narrative is being used right now by [specific modern actor]."
- Colonial patterns: "Here's the pattern: [mechanism]. It happened in [X]. It happened in [Y]. It's happening in [Z]."

## 3.5 Spoken-delivery rules (The Fig Tree Style)

The delivery must sound like an intimate, documentary-style conversation with a professor. Use the **5-Stage Intensity Ladder**:
1. **Context:** Baseline, conversational volume.
2. **Paradox/Conflict:** Slight pitch drop, slower pacing.
3. **Document Reveal (Exhibit A):** Deliberate, authoritative.
4. **Implication:** Urgent, direct address.
5. **Verdict:** Absolute calm, punctuated with silence.

### Rapport Techniques (Fig Tree style)

- **Direct Address:** "You might think..."
- **Rhetorical Questions:** "Should we just get into it?"
- **The "Counsel" Moment:** Looking off-camera or softening tone before dropping a major fact.
- **2-Second Pauses:** Force a `[PAUSE: 2s]` before and after major paradox reveals. Let the silence do the heavy lifting.

### Date formats

| Rule | Wrong | Right |
|------|-------|-------|
| Ordinal dates with "On" | "June 16, 2014. A farmer..." | "On June 16th, 2014, a farmer..." |
| Duration context | "In 1915" | "Six months later — May 1916" |
| Specific precision | "at some point" | "December 1905. Tehran." |

### Date-first anchoring (flexible)

Dates can get their own weight, but don't always need to be isolated as a separate sentence. Depends on whether there's narrative buildup before the date.

- **With setup (date flows in):** "And that clash was bound to happen sooner or later. 5 September 1492, Columbus accidentally stumbled upon an unknown continent and the clash was about to begin."
- **Without setup (date anchors):** "October 3rd, 1940. The Vichy government enacted the statute."
- **AI default (date buried):** "enacted by the Vichy government on October 3rd, 1940" — too passive

**Rule:** Give dates prominence — don't bury them at the end of a clause. Big reveal moments = standalone date. Normal narration = date woven in naturally.

### List formatting

**Informational lists:** Use flowing commas

| Wrong (choppy) | Right (flows) |
|----------------|---------------|
| "World War I. Foreign occupation. Famine." | "World War I, foreign occupation, famine." |

**Rhetorical beats:** Use staccato periods

| Purpose | Example |
|---------|---------|
| Building tension | "Not a promise. Not a transition. Independence." |
| Dramatic emphasis | "Iran's first democracy — crushed." |
| Key fact impact | "Britain never built it." |

**The test:** Would a presenter instinctively combine these while speaking? If yes, write them combined.

### "Here's" usage

| Usage Level | Verdict |
|-------------|---------|
| 2-4 per script | Natural |
| 5-6 per script | Borderline |
| 10+ per script | Overuse — rewrite |

**Good uses:**

- "Here's what the 1859 treaty actually says." (document reveal)
- "Here's what historians actually say." (expert evidence)

**Alternatives when overused:**

| Instead of | Try |
|------------|-----|
| "Here's what they found:" | "This is what they found:" |
| "Here's the mechanism:" | "The mechanism was this:" |
| "Here's how it worked:" | "The deal worked like this:" |

### Colons vs periods

**Colons:** Use for introducing direct quotes or formal definitions.

> "The treaty states: 'All territories south of the line...'"

**Periods:** Use for spoken pauses.

| Written feel | Spoken feel |
|--------------|-------------|
| "They had a problem: they were broke." | "They had a problem. They were broke." |
| "The result: Khomeini's faction won." | "The result was that Khomeini's faction won." |

### Spoken keyword placement (SEO)

YouTube's algorithm analyzes spoken words via auto-transcription. Say target search keywords clearly in the first 60 seconds.

**Pattern:** Weave the exact search phrase into your opening naturally.

- "Today we're looking at the **scramble for Africa** — specifically, the 1884 Berlin Conference..."
- "The **Treaty of Tordesillas** divided the entire world between two countries..."

Don't force it. If your title contains the keyword, speaking it naturally in the hook is usually sufficient.

## 3.7 Quote integration (3-step pattern)

### The pattern: Setup → Quote → Implication

1. **Setup:** State the claim in your own words.
2. **Quote:** Provide the evidence.
3. **Implication:** Explain why it matters.

**Example:**

```
SETUP: "In 2016, the Guardian Council disqualified 99 percent of reformist candidates."

QUOTE: Parsa documents exactly what happened:
"The council eliminated 99 percent of 3,000 reformists who had registered..."

IMPLICATION: "Ninety-nine percent. Before a single vote was cast."
```

### Setup lines flexible by purpose

The creator uses different setups depending on WHY the quote is being used:

- **To illustrate a point:** "The best illustration of X is..." → quote
- **To invoke authority:** "Hear what X said about this..." or "Experts have studied this and came to X conclusion" → quote
- **To reveal a document:** "Here's what the treaty actually says:" → quote

The setup should tell the viewer what the quote will DO — illustrate, prove, surprise, or contradict. Don't front-load a verdict the quote should earn.

### Introducing quotes (full pattern)

**Name → signal → source → quote → interpret**

> "Bismarck famously said — in [source]: '[quote]'. We can interpret this as [plain language meaning]."

**Rules:**

- Name the person first.
- Signal a quote is coming ("famously said", "wrote that", "argued").
- Cite the source briefly.
- Deliver the full quote verbatim.
- Then translate the IMPLICATION (not paraphrase the content).

### Quote stacking (authority stack — Shaun)

Multiple sources confirm same fact, debunking requires building credibility.

**Formula:** [State disputed claim] → [Authority 1] + credentials + finding → [Authority 2] + different credentials + confirming finding → [Authority 3 meta-commentary] → "So what's going on?"

**Example (Vance, debunking brothel infanticide):**

> "2014 DNA analysis of Yewden Villa. Balanced male to female ratio. All from different mothers. No trauma, proper burial, 97 infant deaths over 300 years of occupation. That's 0.32 per year. Normal infant mortality for the period. 2015 Cambridge University. 'We trust that this evidence disposes once and for all with the suggestion that infanticide was the norm in Roman Britain.' Bioarchaeologist Sian Halcrow in 2017 specifically called this out. 'Whole cemeteries of unwanted brothel babies. These are sensational research stories that capture headlines but misrepresent archaeological evidence.' Vance is repeating headlines that modern science has rejected."

## 3.8 Section openings, transitions, closings

### Section openings (start with concrete details)

Date / place / document — not abstractions. (See 3.2 Rule 9.)

### Transition bridges

Every section needs connection to the next.

| Pattern | Example |
|---------|---------|
| "This would be tested on..." | "Mossadegh's conviction would be tested on one issue above all others: oil." |
| "And that brings us to..." | "And that brings us to the document everyone forgets." |
| "Which created a problem:" | "Which created a problem: who actually owned the territory?" |
| "That's the context for..." | "That's the context for what happened next." |

**Kraut-style transitions:**

> "But the successor kingdoms just built the foundation. The Carolingians provided the ambition."

### Specific transition patterns (from real videos)

**Temporal jump with "Now":** [Complete section] → "Now" + [new topic/time period] → [bridging sentence].

**"So how did we get here?":** [Modern dispute framed with competing claims]. So how did we get here? [Begin historical chronology].

**"Then suddenly..." (date shift):** [Long period of status quo] → "Then suddenly, [specific date]," → [Change announced] → "What changed?"

**"But here's where it gets interesting" (LIMIT: 1x per script):** [Establish initial fact that seems to support common narrative]. But here's where it gets interesting. [Introduce evidence that complicates].

**"Look at what just happened":** [Describe sequence of actions neutrally] → "Look at what just happened." → [Reveal pattern/manipulation].

**"Which brings us to...":** [Background or context established]. Which brings us to [pivotal moment/document/question].

**Date as section break:** [Previous section conclusion]. [Specific date]. [New development]. [Causal connection].

**Contrast pair (competing perspectives):** "From Britain's perspective, [their interpretation]. [Their conclusion]. Guatemala saw it completely different. [Their interpretation]. [Their conclusion]."

**"Here's what nobody talks about":** [Geopolitical framing complete] → "Here's what nobody talks about." → [Human dimension: who lives there, who's affected].

### Macro-transition phrases (Rule 60 expansion)

For major section-to-section handoffs. Every section shift must justify WHY. "Now let's talk about..." is banned.

**Prerequisite Pivot:**

- "To understand why [main topic]... we need to [go back to / look at]..."
- "If we want to understand [concept A] we first need to understand [concept B]."
- HvH: "To understand why this clause matters, we need to look at what happened in [earlier treaty]."

**Contextual Flashback:**

- "Before we can get to [Event X], it's important to..."
- "Before all that though, there is one important event we need to know about..."
- HvH: "Before we get to the [ruling], there's a clause in the [earlier treaty] we need to see first."

**Analytical Pivot:**

- "So what's going on here then?"
- "But that general story... doesn't really explain [modern reality]."
- HvH: "So what does the treaty actually say?" / "But that version doesn't explain what happened next."

**Geographic / Faction Handoff:**

- "So let's leave [faction A] in their [situation] and discuss what [faction B]..."
- "Things were different in [Place B]."
- HvH: "That's the British position. Now let's look at what [other party] was arguing."

**Acknowledged Tangent:**

- "I need to go off on a tangent that will seem unrelated, but I promise there's a point."
- HvH: "There's a legal concept we need to understand before going further: [concept]."

**Re-Centering (after any tangent):**

- "Which brings us back to [main topic]."
- "Okay, so let's get back to our timeline."
- "Here we are again! I told you this will be a recurring theme."

### Seed-planting transitions

The end of one section naturally sets up the next:

> "...they wanted to control how to divide the land. That division wasn't done by looking at who lived there, but by what geographic markers they could use to create new countries."

**Rule:** Prefer seed-planting where section A's last sentence raises section B's topic. With dense material, explicit bridges are fine. The goal is structural logic, not performative seamlessness. Avoid empty bridges ("Now let's look at...") but functional ones ("That treaty created a problem:") are fine.

For full templates, see `CLOSING-SYNTHESIS-TEMPLATES.md`.

## 3.9 Title-to-hook matching

**Data:** Videos with 20%+ intro drops (Armenia 29.4%, Flat Earth 25.6%, Peru 22.2%) all share one trait: the first 5 seconds don't deliver what the title promised.

**The rule:** Your first 5 seconds must fulfill the title's promise. The viewer already decided to watch based on the title — fulfill it immediately, then backfill context.

| Title Promise | First 5 Seconds MUST Show | Wrong |
|---------------|---------------------------|-------|
| "The Document That..." | The document on screen | Background context about the era |
| "[Country] vs [Country]" | The conflict/map | Historical setup |
| "[Person] Claims [X]" | The claim (quote/clip) | Who the person is |
| "The Map They Ignored" | The map | Why maps matter |

**Match the energy:** If the thumbnail is dramatic, the first seconds must match. Academic calm after a dramatic thumbnail = instant bounce.

## 3.10 CTA placement

Only ~16% of viewers reach the final 10 seconds of any video. Place your strongest subscribe CTA at the **60-70% mark** — after a compelling evidence reveal when trust is highest.

**Pattern:** Deliver devastating quote/evidence → brief pause → "If you want more like this, please subscribe" → immediately continue with next point. Never break flow for the CTA.

- **Wrong:** CTA in outro (most viewers already gone).
- **Wrong:** CTA in first 30 seconds (72% of viewers skip early CTAs — no trust built yet).
- **Right:** CTA after your strongest evidence reveal, mid-video.

### Thematic CTA integration

Top creators integrate CTAs into the video's vocabulary. "Don't forget to emancipate that subscribe button" (Neoslavery). For HvH: use legal/treaty language in CTAs where natural.

## 3.11 Script-to-filmed reality

**Source: 20-project script-vs-SRT analysis.** Average script survival rate is 44%, NOT 77%.

- **Write 1.80x your target word count** — only 44% of scripted content survives to final video.
- **Ad-libs have +0.102 higher retention** than scripted content (0.351 vs 0.250).
- **Ad-lib rate is 32%** of final video content — leave room for spontaneous additions.
- **Mark expandable sections** with `<!-- EXPANDABLE -->` — these are where ad-libs naturally occur.

### Pre-filming polish checklist

- **Documentary Motion (MANDATORY):** Static documents kill retention. All documents MUST use `[ANIMATE: Ken Burns zoom]` or `[ANIMATE: Draw highlight box]`. Never use a static `[SHOW: Document]` tag.
- **Academic attributions:** Lead with story, not citations (move "According to X" to evidence display).
- **Rhetorical emphasis cut:** Remove "Do you see what this means?" phrases.
- **Setup questions cut:** Go direct to information (not "So what did they do? They...").
- **Lists as prose:** Convert numbered lists to flowing sentences.
- **Personal framing cut:** Remove "When I first read this..." statements.
- **Hedging words removed:** No "essentially," "basically," "kind of".
- **Structure tightened:** Start with point, then explain (not setup → context → point).

### Content-type retention rankings (placement decisions)

42 videos, 4,200 data points.

| Rank | Content Type | Avg Delta | Positive % | Best Position | Key Insight |
|------|--------------|-----------|------------|---------------|-------------|
| 1 | **narration** | -0.005 | 56% | Mid (safest vehicle) | Causal-chain narration holds best. Don't interrupt unnecessarily. |
| 2 | **quote** | -0.008 | 54% | Mid-Late | Pair with visual evidence on screen. |
| 3 | **primary_source** | -0.009 | 56% | Mid | Same as quotes — visual pairing essential. |
| 4 | **statistic** | -0.009 | **61%** | **Late (+0.001 = GAINS viewers)** | Highest positive rate. Late stats GAIN viewers. |
| 5 | **modern_relevance** | -0.010 | 52% | Late | Weave into narration. Standalone bridges disrupt flow. |
| 6 | **personal_authority** | -0.044 | 22% | Mid (-0.0001 = flat) | 86% clusters in intro drop zone. Mid-video is fine. |

**Actionable placement rules:**

1. Front-load a specific number in first 10 seconds — statistics have 61% positive retention rate.
2. Save strongest statistic for the closing third — late-video stats: +0.001 delta (actually gains viewers).
3. Keep narration flowing through the middle — -0.005 delta is the best of any content type.
4. "I read the treaty" is fine after minute 3 — mid-video personal_authority: -0.0001 (flat). Keep it out of the 1-3 minute danger zone.
5. Modern relevance = weave, don't bridge.
