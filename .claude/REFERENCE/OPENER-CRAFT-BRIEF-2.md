## 1. PRIMER CALIBRATION

### The core problem

The first brief found that Tripoli (gap opens ~0:08) beat No-Lassos (gap opens ~0:18) despite both being strong openers. Loewenstein requires *some* existing knowledge before a gap feels painful. But too much context before the gap is a retention killer. This section defines the minimum viable primer and the tipping point.

---

### What the sources actually say

**Made to Stick — the treasure map rule:**

> "Knowledge gaps create interest. But to prove that the knowledge gaps exist, it may be necessary to highlight some knowledge first. 'Here's what you know. Now here's what you're missing.' Alternatively, you can set context so people care what comes next. It's no accident that mystery novelists and crossword-puzzle writers give us clues. When we feel that we're close to the solution of a puzzle, curiosity takes over and propels us to the finish. Treasure maps, as shown in the movies, are vague. They show a few key landmarks and a big X where the treasure is. Usually the adventurer knows just enough to find the first landmark, which becomes the first step in a long journey toward the treasure. If treasure maps were produced on MapQuest.com, with door-to-door directions, it would kill the adventure-movie genre."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 2

This is the definitive primer rule: **a landmark, not a map.** The primer's job is to show the audience the first landmark — a single concrete detail that anchors them — not to explain the territory.

**Made to Stick — sequencing over dumping:**

> "People are tempted to tell you everything, with perfect accuracy, right up front, when they should be giving you just enough info to be useful, then a little more, then a little more. ... There is value in sequencing information — not dumping a stack of information on someone at once but dropping a clue, then another clue, then another. This method of communication resembles flirting more than lecturing."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 1 (Curse of Knowledge) and Principle 2

**Kane — 3 seconds for stage-setting, then act:**

> "Naveen Gowda reminds us that you have three seconds to present the structure and establish a promise about the effect the video will have on the viewer. Don't go slow, but also don't jump into things too quickly or rush. Instead, use the first three seconds to establish the scene. Create some kind of action or an unresolved situation. Then, when the three seconds are up, you can begin to act on the idea you want to present."
> — *Hook Point*, Brendan Kane, Chapter 3

> "As a general rule, you don't want to start too much action until after the three seconds are up. Until that point your goal is to set the stage and spark interest."
> — *Hook Point*, Brendan Kane, Chapter 3

**McKee — what kills primers: the "pointless pace killer":**

> "If we writers have a common fault in design and placement of the Inciting Incident, it's that we habitually delay the Central Plot while we pack our opening sequences with exposition. We consistently underestimate knowledge and life experience of the audience, laying out our characters and world with tedious details the filmgoer has already filled in with common sense."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 8

> "A 'pointless pace killer' is any scene in which reactions lack insight and imagination, forcing expectation to equal result."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 8

The primer becomes a "pace killer" at the exact moment it states something the viewer could have inferred. Every primer sentence should contain exactly one piece of information the viewer did NOT already have.

**1of10 — timing benchmark:**

> "0:00–0:05: Attention grab. 0:05–0:15: Clarify value promise. 0:15–0:30: Establish stakes and context."
> — 1of10, "How to Hook Viewers Fast"

Note that "stakes and context" is the *third* beat, not the first. Any context delivered before the attention grab fires is delivered out of sequence.

---

### The concrete rule

**Minimum viable primer = 1 schema activation** (the thing the audience already believes or assumes), delivered in 1-2 sentences of ≤10 words each, completed within the first 5 seconds.

**The tipping point = sentence 3 without a gap.** If the third sentence of the video has not yet named what is unknown, unresolved, or contradicted, the primer has become exposition.

**Applied to own openers:**

| Opener | Primer content | Primer length | Gap opens at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tripoli (61%) | One sentence: "In 1930, a Dutch scholar named Snouck Hurgronje examined the Arabic original of an American treaty." | ~5 words of pure setup | Second sentence (~0:08) |
| No-Lassos (58%) | Full statement of the viral claim (~5 sentences, ~40 words) | ~18 seconds | Sentence 6 (~0:18) |
| Bakassi (31%) | Abstract category + meta-observation + framing | ~25 seconds | Gap never cleanly opens in first 30s |

**Why No-Lassos's long primer still works at 58%:** The primer IS the schema activation — it states the exact belief the audience holds. Every sentence of the primer is the "existing knowledge base" Loewenstein requires. The viewer is already nodding. The gap fires hard when it arrives. The cost is ~10 seconds of delay vs. Tripoli. If the No-Lassos primer were cut to 2 sentences ("There's a claim you've probably seen online. Europeans didn't go to Africa with a lasso."), the gap could open at ~0:08 and retention would likely improve.

**The Bakassi failure is not that the primer is long — it's that the primer contains no schema activation.** "Most border disputes are about who got to the land first" does not activate any belief the viewer holds about Bakassi. There is no reference point. Loewenstein's prerequisite is not met. The gap cannot fire because the viewer has no frame from which to recognize something is missing.

**Practical rule for this channel:** If the video's topic is KNOWN (viewer has a belief to contradict), the primer can be 1-2 sentences activating that belief. If the topic is UNKNOWN (viewer has no existing frame), you need 3-4 sentences of grounding before the gap — but each sentence must be a named concrete landmark (person, place, date, document), not abstract context.

## 2. SUSTAINING THE GAP BEYOND 0:30 — NESTED OPEN LOOPS AND THE TURN

**Source gap:** *Out on the Wire* (Abel) is not in the notebook. The framework below draws on McKee (*Story*), Yorke (*Into the Woods*), and Made to Stick.

---

### The fundamental mechanism: surprise captures, interest sustains

Made to Stick draws a critical distinction that underpins this entire section:

> "To understand the answers to these two questions, we have to understand two essential emotions — surprise and interest — that are commonly provoked by naturally sticky ideas. Surprise gets our attention. Interest keeps our attention."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 2

> "Mystery is created not from an unexpected moment but from an unexpected journey. We know where we're headed — we want to solve the mystery — but we're not sure how we'll get there."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 2

The opener creates surprise. Everything from 0:30 forward requires sustained interest — which comes from *systematic gap management*, not repeated surprise. The structure is different.

---

### McKee: the Inciting Incident and the 25% rule

The first major turn — the moment that "radically upsets the balance of forces" — should land by the first 25% of total runtime. For a 10-minute video, that is **the 2:30 mark**.

> "As a rule of thumb, the first major event of the Central Plot occurs within the first 25 percent of the telling. This is a useful guide, no matter what the medium. How long would you make a theatre audience sit in the dark before engaging the story in a play? Would you make a reader plow through the first hundred pages of a four-hundred-page novel before finding the Central Plot? How long before irredeemable boredom sets in? The standard for a two-hour feature film is to locate the Central Plot's Inciting Incident somewhere within the first half-hour."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 8

> "However, if the Central Plot's Inciting Incident arrives much later than fifteen minutes into the film, boredom becomes a risk. Therefore, while the audience waits for the main plot, a subplot may be needed to engage their interest."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 8

**Applied to 8-12 minute videos:** The macro-gap opened in the first 30 seconds must be accompanied by a sub-plot engagement mechanism (a second open question, a named actor with stakes, a competing claim) to sustain interest until the inciting document or primary evidence is introduced at ~2:00-2:30. Without this bridge, viewers who stay through the opener still bleed between 0:30 and 2:00.

McKee on what makes every scene hold:

> "The effects of Turning Points are fourfold: surprise, increased curiosity, insight, and new direction. When a gap opens between expectation and result, it jolts the audience with surprise... The question 'Why?' propels [the audience] back through the story, and what it's seen so far instantly clicks into a new configuration; it experiences a rush of insight into character and world, a satisfying layer of hidden truth. Insight adds to curiosity. This new understanding amplifies the questions 'What's going to happen next?' and 'How will this turn out?'"
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapters 8 and 10

The goal is that every answered question produces insight that raises a new question. This is the chain mechanism.

---

### Yorke: top-spin and the fractal structure

Yorke's key contribution is the fractal model — every unit of the video (segment, paragraph, sentence) should mirror the arc of the whole:

> "Stories are built from acts, acts are built from scenes and scenes are built from even smaller units called beats. All these units are constructed in three parts: fractal versions of the three-act whole... What's fascinating is that micro versions of the very same structure are performing exactly the same function on a cellular level. Stories are formed from this secret ministry; the endless replication of narrative structure is going on within acts, and within scenes."
> — *Into the Woods*, John Yorke, Chapter (fractal structure section)

The practical implication: each segment of an 8-12 minute video should have its own setup, complication, and turn. The gap opened at the start of a segment should be resolved before the segment ends — but the resolution should open a new gap going into the next segment.

Yorke calls this "top-spin":

> "It's a technique some call 'top-spin' as it creates an extremely powerful narrative drive. Every scene ends on a question — partly 'Where did that come from?' but more importantly 'How are they going to get out of that?' By cutting away at the crisis point, a writer thus creates a sequence in which question is followed by (delayed) answer, which is followed by a question once again."
> — *Into the Woods*, John Yorke, Chapter 9

> "Each scene emerges from the fusion of material in the previous one — each dying like an exploding sun to create the raw material for the scene that follows."
> — *Into the Woods*, John Yorke, Chapter 9

Lee Child, quoted by Yorke:

> "You ask or imply a question at the beginning of the book and you absolutely self-consciously withhold the answer. It does feel cheap and meretricious but it absolutely works."
> — *Into the Woods*, Yorke quoting Lee Child, Chapter 9

**Note:** Yorke acknowledges this technique "feels cheap and meretricious" — the evidence-referee voice must manage this carefully. The answer: withhold the *resolution* of evidence, not the evidence itself. Show the document. Don't tell them what it means yet. This is the Calm Prosecutor version of top-spin.

---

### Made to Stick: systematic gap management as a teaching tool

> "We can engage people's curiosity over a long period of time by systematically 'opening gaps' in their knowledge — and then filling those gaps... The goal is not to summarize; it's to make you care about knowing something, and then to tell you what you want to know."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 2

The Cialdini classroom case is the clearest example in the corpus of what "gap management" produces at scale:

> "He would introduce the mystery at the start of class, return to it during the lecture, and reveal the answer at the end. In one lecture, though, the end-of-class bell rang before he had time to reveal the solution... Normally five to ten minutes before the scheduled end time, some students start preparing to leave. You know the signals — pencils are put away, notebooks folded, backpacks zipped up. This time, though, the class was silent. 'After the bell rang, no one moved. In fact, when I tried to end the lecture without revealing the mystery, I was pelted with protests.' He said he felt as if he'd discovered dynamite."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 2 (citing Cialdini)

**The structural lesson:** The mystery is introduced at the start, *returned to in the middle*, and resolved at the end. This is the three-touch rule for sustaining a macro-gap across a long video. State it at 0:30. Reference it at the 4:00 turn. Resolve it at 9:00+.

---

### Applied framework for an 8-12 minute explainer

```
0:00–0:30  MACRO-GAP OPENS
           State the unresolved question / contradicted claim.
           Do NOT resolve it. Name the document that will.

0:30–2:30  BRIDGE / SUB-PLOT ENGAGEMENT
           Deploy a secondary open question or a competing claim.
           Introduce context in clue-form (not MapQuest).
           End this segment on a new question (top-spin).

2:30       FIRST MAJOR TURN (McKee 25% rule)
           Introduce the inciting piece of evidence.
           This partially resolves the sub-gap but deepens the macro-gap.
           "Now you know X. But that raises Y."

4:00–5:00  MID-SEGMENT CALLBACK TO MACRO-GAP
           Reference the original unresolved question explicitly.
           (Cialdini's return-in-the-middle move.)
           This re-anchors viewers who may have partially drifted.

6:00–7:00  PENULTIMATE TURN
           Complicate the macro-gap. A counter-claim, a scholar who disagrees,
           a document that cuts the other way. This is Yorke's "dying sun" moment —
           the segment resolves but opens the final question.

8:30–10:00 MACRO-GAP RESOLUTION
           The primary evidence resolves the opened question.
           This is the "Aha!" that Cialdini's students stayed for.
```

**What NOT to do:** Introduce new gaps in the final 2 minutes. Viewers who have invested 8+ minutes have earned a clean resolution. Yorke: "Story as such... can only have one merit: that of making the audience want to know what happens next. And conversely it can only have one fault: that of making the audience not want to know what happens next."

## 3. PER-PATTERN EXECUTION MICRO-MECHANICS

---

### Pattern 1 — Document Ambush

*Open with a historical document or quote that contradicts the common understanding. The document IS the hook.*

**How to land:**

McKee's governing principle:

> "STORYTELLING is the creative demonstration of truth. A story is the living proof of an idea, the conversion of idea to action. A story's event structure is the means by which you first express, then prove your idea… without explanation. Master storytellers never explain. They do the hard, painfully creative thing — they dramatize. Audiences are rarely interested, and certainly never convinced, when forced to listen to the discussion of ideas."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 18

The document must be *used*, not introduced. It must do something in the sentence it appears in. Not: "The Treaty of Tripoli is a document that states..." But: "Dutch scholar Snouck Hurgronje examined the Arabic original of the Treaty of Tripoli — and what he found the State Department couldn't explain."

**Convert exposition to ammunition:**

> "Convert exposition to ammunition. Your characters know their world, their history, each other, and themselves. Let them use what they know as ammunition in their struggle to get what they want."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 15

> "Skill in exposition means making it invisible... Dramatized exposition serves two ends: Its primary purpose is to further the immediate conflict. Its secondary purpose is to convey information."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 15

**What makes a document cinematic:** The "telling detail." McKee:

> "Authenticity depends on the 'telling detail.' When we use a few selected details, the audience's imagination supplies the rest, completing a credible whole."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 9

Applied: the detail that makes the Treaty of Tripoli cinematic is not the treaty itself — it's "the Arabic original" (a variant no one has explained) and "the State Department couldn't explain" (a named authority's failure). These two details do the work. The document is framing material; the gap is the hook.

**How to flop:** McKee's "table-dusting" error:

> "The curtain comes up on a living room set. Enter two domestics: One who's worked there for the last thirty years, the other the young maid just hired that morning. The older maid turns to the newcomer and says, 'Oh, you don't know about Dr. Johnson and his family, do you? Well, let me tell you…' And as they dust the furniture the older maid lays out the entire life history, world, and characterizations of the Johnson family. That's 'table dusting,' unmotivated exposition."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 15

A Document Ambush fails when the document is introduced via table-dusting: "In [year], [document] was signed by [parties]. It stated that [content]." That is a history lesson. The document must arrive as a weapon with a target.

**The Sinatra Test check:** Made to Stick: the document should "pass the Sinatra Test" — if it proves the point in this one specific, extreme case, the viewer extrapolates everywhere. Ask: what is the most extreme thing this document establishes? Open with that.

**Don'ts:**
- Don't explain the document's significance. Dramatize it.
- Don't provide full documentary context before the gap fires.
- Don't open with the document's title. Open with its most surprising content.

---

### Pattern 2 — Inverted Verdict

*State the claim the audience holds. Then immediately contradict it with evidence. The contradiction is sentence 2.*

**How to land:** Post-dictable surprise is the operative principle. Made to Stick:

> "To be surprising, an event can't be predictable. Surprise is the opposite of predictability. But, to be satisfying, surprise must be 'post-dictable.' The twist makes sense after you think about it, but it's not something you would have seen coming."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 2

The contradiction must feel inevitable in retrospect, not arbitrary. The test: after a viewer has watched the full video, would they look back at sentence 2 and think "of course"?

**Give the gag away within 10 seconds:**

> "He advises social-content creators to consider giving away the gags at the beginning of their videos. Shareability's videos will often give away the punch line or the emotional reveal within the first ten seconds. They do so because it gives the videos a better chance of being more engaging and even going viral."
> — *Hook Point*, Brendan Kane, Chapter 3 (citing Erick Brownstein, Shareability)

Applied to Inverted Verdict: the "gag" is the contradiction. Give it immediately. "The problem is, we have the historical record" — that's the gag, delivered within 10 seconds. Do not tease the contradiction; deliver it.

**How much to reveal before killing the gap:** The verdict, not the evidence. The opener can state what the evidence will prove ("The historical record contradicts this claim") without producing the evidence itself. The evidence is the payoff that sustains retention through the middle. For No-Lassos, the opener delivers: "we have the historical record, written by the men who ran the operation" — a verdict. The specific documents (the 1444 Zurara account, the Portuguese records) arrive later. Correct dose.

**How to flop — "hiding the ball":** Save the Cat (referenced in query results, page-level citation not returned) identifies "hiding the ball" as the failure mode: withholding the contradiction long enough that the viewer gives up. The distinction between "creating a gap" and "hiding the ball" is whether the viewer can see the direction of value. If the viewer cannot tell within 10 seconds what the video is going to disprove, they have no gap to hold onto — just confusion.

Made to Stick on schema-break requirements:

> "If you want your ideas to be stickier, you've got to break someone's guessing machine and then fix it. But in surprising people, in breaking their guessing machines, how do we avoid gimmicky surprise?... The easiest way to avoid gimmicky surprise and ensure that your unexpected ideas produce insight is to make sure you target an aspect of your audience's guessing machines that relates to your core message."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 2

The contradiction must target a schema *directly connected to the video's core thesis*. A random surprising fact is worthless. A contradiction of the specific claim the video will debunk is the only valid move.

**Don'ts:**
- Don't withhold the contradiction past the 3rd sentence.
- Don't contradict and then qualify immediately ("but it's complicated"). The gap needs to breathe.
- Don't use the contradiction to introduce yourself or your methodology.

---

### Pattern 3 — Single-Sentence Contradiction

*One sentence that states something true but counterintuitive. The entire hook is self-contained in sentence 1.*

**How to land:** Kane's "Proclamation Lead" framework:

> "A Proclamation Lead begins with an emotionally compelling statement in the form of a headline. It is bold, makes a promise about an imagined benefit, and is of a relevant subject to the prospect. Also, it's often based on a prediction about a future outcome that catches people by surprise. The goal is to raise curiosity with a promise that can 'push the envelope of the incredible.' However, for the bold statement to work, the copy that follows must provide information that proves the validity of that claim/promise."
> — *Hook Point*, Brendan Kane, Chapter 3

The single-sentence contradiction must be falsifiable — the audience should be able to test it, which means the sentence must imply a mechanism. Not just "X is wrong" but "X is wrong because of Y that you've never heard of." The Y can be implied.

**Speed of detection:** Kahneman documents that violations of normality are detected at the brain level within 0.2 seconds. The schema-break does not need to be large to fire System 2. A single unexpected predicate on a familiar subject is sufficient: "[Familiar subject] [unexpected verb/outcome]."

**The proverb standard:** Made to Stick:

> "We must create ideas that are both simple and profound. The Golden Rule is the ultimate model of simplicity: a one-sentence statement so profound that an individual could spend a lifetime learning to follow it."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 1

Applied: the single-sentence contradiction should aim at proverb density — compress a complex historical reality into one sentence that is both maximally simple and maximally surprising. "In 1884, the local kings did not sell their land to Britain. They hired them." This is two sentences but proverb-level density. Each is under 10 words.

**How to flop — the wolves problem:** Made to Stick documents a band-versus-wolves commercial where the surprise was "utterly nongermane to the message":

> "There's no question that this ad was surprising and memorable... But because the surprise was utterly nongermane to the message that needed to be communicated, it was worthless."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 2

A single-sentence contradiction that is surprising but unrelated to the video's core thesis is this wolves ad. The viewer will remember the surprise but not connect it to the argument. Every single-sentence contradiction must target the guessing machine *relevant to the thesis*.

**Don'ts:**
- Don't use complex vocabulary in sentence 1 — Kahneman: "do not use complex language where simpler language will do"
- Don't hedge ("Some people argue that X...") — the Calm Prosecutor states, does not hedge
- Don't follow sentence 1 with explanation — follow it with the next clue (treasure map, not MapQuest)

### Pattern 4 — Map / Geography Contradiction

*Open with a geographic or spatial fact that violates the viewer's mental model of where something is or where something belongs.*

**How to land:** The sources do not address this pattern by name. The applicable principle is concreteness as a mobilization mechanism. Made to Stick:

> "Why does this happen? Because concreteness is a way of mobilizing and focusing your brain."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 3

Geographic specificity (place names, distances, borders) operates the same way as date specificity (named year) or person specificity (named actor). It creates an anchor that the brain can attach its model to, making the subsequent contradiction feel personal.

The "big red X" metaphor from Made to Stick directly applies:

> "Unexpected ideas, by opening a knowledge gap, tease and flirt. They mark a big red X on something that needs to be discovered but don't necessarily tell you how to get there."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 2

A map contradiction should function as a literal red X: "Here is a place you think you understand. Here is the thing about it you don't know." The gap is geographic — the viewer's mental map is wrong.

**What NOT to do:** Apply the MapQuest failure. Providing full geographic context before the contradiction fires is table-dusting in cartographic form. One named place + one contradicted assumption = the minimum viable geography contradiction.

**Not found in sources:** Specific craft advice on how to handle map/geography openers visually (when to show the map vs. voice-lead). This is a gap in the corpus. The visual timing question (map on screen at word 1 vs. word 10) has no direct sourcing here.

### Pattern 5 — Mystery Figure / Anomalous Actor

*Open with a named person doing something unexpected. The person IS the hook.*

**How to land:** Transportation via character attachment. Green & Brock establish that narrative transportation is driven in part by identification with a character. Yorke operationalizes this:

> "Characters are revealed through behavior and explanation of motivation is avoided; characters do things, and through doing them we understand them."
> — *Into the Woods*, John Yorke, Chapter 11

The mystery figure must *act* in the first sentence. Not: "Snouck Hurgronje was a Dutch scholar who studied Islamic law." But: "In 1930, a Dutch scholar named Snouck Hurgronje examined the Arabic original of an American treaty." He is doing something. The doing establishes character.

**The scene-objective rule:** McKee requires every scene to be anchored in desire:

> "What does he (or it) want? Desire is always the key. Phrase this desire... as an infinitive: such as, 'to do this…' or 'to get that…'"
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 18 (scene analysis)

Applied: the mystery figure must want something visible from the opener. "Snouck Hurgronje examined the Arabic original" — he wanted to read the authoritative text. That desire is coherent. "Snouck Hurgronje was interested in Arabic treaties" — no desire, no scene-objective, no transportation.

**The Mother Teresa principle:** Made to Stick on statistical vs. individual-person framing:

> "The Mother Teresa principle: If I look at the one, I will act. People donate more to Rokia than to a huge swath of Africa."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 5 (Emotional)

One named person activates more emotional investment than a category. "European explorers" does nothing. "Prince Henry the Navigator on horseback in Lagos" activates transportation.

**The "revelation in contradiction" move:** McKee on major characters:

> "The revelation of deep character in contrast or contradiction to characterization is fundamental in major characters... The finest writing not only reveals true character, but arcs or changes that inner nature."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 9

For evidence-referee openers: the mystery figure's unexpected action should contradict their apparent role. A king who hires rather than sells. A scholar who finds what the State Department cannot explain. A navigator who presides over a market *he built, not walked into*. The contradiction of characterization is where the gap lives.

**How to flop:** Creator-centric figure selection. Kane: creating content "for themselves instead of for their audience." If the mystery figure is interesting to the creator but the viewer has no schema for them, there is no surprise — only unfamiliarity, which produces confusion, not curiosity. The mystery figure must be attached to something the viewer already has a model for.

## 4. SENTENCE-LEVEL MECHANICS — CHECKLIST

---

### Rule 1 — Sentence 1 length: magazine cover, not encyclopedia

**Kane:**

> "Uses as few words as possible (envision a magazine-cover headline)... Doesn't make people think very hard — it's presented in a simple-to-understand manner."
> — *Hook Point*, Brendan Kane, Chapter 2

**1of10 benchmark:**

> "Overlong intros: Keep opening statements to 5 seconds maximum."
> — 1of10, "How to Hook Viewers Fast"

At a natural conversational pace, 5 seconds ≈ 10-13 words. Sentence 1 should be ≤13 words.

**McKee before/after:**

> "Not, for example: 'Mr. Charles Wilson Evans, the chief financial officer at Data Corporation in the 666 building on Fifth Avenue in Manhattan, who was promoted to that position six years ago, having graduated magna cum laude from Harvard Business School, was arrested today, accused by the authorities of embezzlement from the company's pension fund and fraud in his efforts to conceal the losses.'
>
> But with a polish: 'You know Charlie Evans? CFO at Data Cop? Ha! Got busted. Had his fist in the till.'"
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 18

The second version is seven sentences averaging 4 words each. The same information, but it lands in under 5 seconds and creates a rhythm that pulls the listener forward.

---

### Rule 2 — Nouns: spikes not nails

**McKee:**

> "To write vividly, avoid generic nouns and verbs with adjectives and adverbs attached and seek the name of the thing: Not 'The carpenter uses a big nail,' but 'The carpenter hammers a spike.' 'Nail' is a generic noun, 'big' an adjective. The solid, Anglo-Saxon 'spike' pops a vivid image in the reader's mind, 'nail' a blur."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 18

**Made to Stick — the memory test:**

> "Experiments in human memory have shown that people are better at remembering concrete, easily visualized nouns ('bicycle' or 'avocado') than abstract ones ('justice' or 'personality')."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 3

> "Abstraction is the luxury of the expert. If you've got to teach an idea to a room full of people, and you aren't certain what they know, concreteness is the only safe language."
> — *Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, Principle 3

**Applied test for every noun in sentence 1:** Can you photograph it? If no, replace it with a noun you can photograph.

"Treaty" = yes (it's a physical document). "Agreement" = borderline (too abstract). "Peace arrangement" = no (ungraspable). "The 1796 Treaty of Tripoli" = yes (specific, nameable, photographable). Use the specific noun.

---

### Rule 3 — Verbs: eliminate "is/are" and state-of-being constructions

**McKee:**

> "Eliminate 'is' and 'are' throughout. Onscreen nothing is in a state of being; story life is an unending flux of change, of becoming. Not: 'There is a big house on a hill above a small town.' 'There is,' 'They are,' 'It is,' 'He/She is' are the weakest possible ways into any English sentence."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 18

> "Perhaps: 'A mansion guards the headlands above the village.' With a Hemingwayesque shunning of Latinate and abstract terms, of adjectives and adverbs, in favor of the most specific, active verbs and concrete nouns possible, even establishing shots come alive."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 18

**Active voice:**

> "Active voice titles are effective on YouTube because they are clear, to the point, and can be easily understood... Having an active voice title also creates a sense of urgency naturally."
> — *The YouTube Formula*, Derral Eves, Chapter 16

**The present-tense urgency principle:** McKee on screenplay description: "We write screenplay in the present tense because, unlike the novel, film is on the knife edge of the now — whether we flash back or forward, we jump to a new now." For voiceover narration of historical events, past tense is conventional. But the historical scene gains immediacy when the scene is written in present tense and then recorded as past: "Prince Henry the Navigator *sits* on horseback" vs "Prince Henry the Navigator *sat* on horseback." The present tense version transports; the past tense reports. When scripting, draft in present tense, then decide whether to record in past.

### Rule 4 — Rhythm: the suspense sentence and the ease dial

**Kahneman — prosodic ease:**

> "The trouble is that there may be other causes for your feeling of ease — including the quality of the font and the appealing rhythm of the prose — and you have no simple way of tracing your feelings to their source."
> — *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Daniel Kahneman, Chapter 5

Sentence rhythm affects cognitive ease directly. Smooth prosody → ease → trust → belief. Jagged, stumbling sentences → strain → vigilance → skepticism. Read sentence 1 aloud. If you stumble, the viewer's brain has stumbled first.

**McKee — the suspense sentence:**

> "Excellent film dialogue tends to shape itself into the periodic sentence: 'If you didn't want me to do it, why'd you give me that…' Look? Gun? Kiss? The periodic sentence is the 'suspense sentence.' Its meaning is delayed until the very last word, forcing both actor and audience to listen to the end of the line."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 18

Applied: "In 1930, a Dutch scholar named Snouck Hurgronje examined the Arabic original of an American treaty." The meaning (what the scholar did) is clear by word 6 ("examined"). The payoff (what treaty) lands at word 14. This is a mild suspense sentence — the "what" is specified at the end. A harder version: "In 1930, a Dutch scholar found what the State Department couldn't explain." The explanation is the withheld end.

**McKee — stikomythia:**

> "The essence of screen dialogue is what was known in Classical Greek theatre as stikomythia — the rapid exchange of short speeches. Long speeches are antithetical with the aesthetics of cinema."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 18

For voiceover, stikomythia means sentence variation: short sentence, medium sentence, short sentence. Never three long sentences in a row. Never three short sentences in a row (it becomes staccato). The Tripoli opener follows this: short ("In 1930, a Dutch scholar named Snouck Hurgronje examined the Arabic original of an American treaty.") → medium ("The next year, the United States government printing office published what he found, what he found the State Department couldn't explain.") → short ("And, almost 100 years later, neither can anyone else.").

**Murch — thought-cut alignment:**

> "You should be right with the blinks, perhaps leading them ever so slightly... Your job is partly to anticipate, partly to control the thought processes of the audience. To give them what they want and/or what they need just before they have to 'ask' for it — to be surprising yet self-evident at the same time."
> — *In the Blink of an Eye*, Walter Murch

For scripting: each sentence should resolve one thought and open the door to the next, in the direction the viewer is already leaning. The script should *anticipate* the viewer's next question and answer it one beat before they consciously form it.

### Rule 5 — What to cut from sentence 1 and the first paragraph

**Cut state-of-being verbs** (McKee): "There is," "It is," "They are," "He was," "She is."

**Cut subordinate clauses from sentence 1** (McKee):

> "Not, for example: 'Mr. Charles Wilson Evans, the chief financial officer at Data Corporation in the 666 building on Fifth Avenue in Manhattan, who was promoted to that position six years ago...'"
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 18

One subject, one verb, one object. Every qualification becomes its own sentence.

**Cut useless tail-words** (McKee):

> "Useless words, especially prepositional phrases, float to the ends of sentences. Consequently, meaning sits somewhere in the middle, but the audience has to listen to those last empty words and for that second or two they're bored."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 18

Applied: "The Treaty of Tripoli was a peace agreement negotiated by American agents in 1796" — the meaning ("peace agreement") is in the middle. The tail ("negotiated by American agents in 1796") is context. Cut and redistribute: "In 1796, American agents negotiated a peace agreement. That document was the Treaty of Tripoli."

**Cut "we see" / "we hear" / "today we're looking at"** (McKee):

> "Eliminate 'we see' and 'we hear.' 'We' doesn't exist."
> — *Story*, Robert McKee, Chapter 18

"Today we're looking at how..." belongs in the same category. The creator does not exist in the story; only the events exist. Direct to the event.

**Cut hedging language:** Not sourced directly for audio, but follows from both McKee's authenticity principle ("The moment it lacks credibility, empathy dissolves") and Kane's concreteness rule. "Some people argue that," "It could be said that," "There is a view that" — all produce cognitive strain and signal uncertainty. The Calm Prosecutor states. He does not hedge in sentence 1.

**Cut abstract nouns that duplicate concrete nouns already present:** If you've written "the Dutch scholar named Snouck Hurgronje," you do not then need "this academic figure." The concrete noun already did the work.

## DISAGREEMENTS AND MISFITS (second-pass)

### Disagreement: "Give the gag away" (Kane/Shareability) vs. "withhold the answer" (Yorke/Lee Child)

Kane/Brownstein: "Give away the punch line or the emotional reveal within the first ten seconds."
Yorke/Lee Child: "You ask or imply a question at the beginning of the book and you absolutely self-consciously withhold the answer."

These are not as contradictory as they appear once you distinguish *type* of reveal. Kane is talking about the emotional payoff of a scene (the gag, the punch line). Yorke is talking about the *resolution* of the macro-mystery. You can give away the emotional punch of the opening scene (Lee Child's hook) while withholding the resolution of the overarching question. For this channel: show the surprising document in the opener (give the gag). Withhold what the document means for the overall argument (withhold the resolution). Both sources are right about different things.

### Disagreement: Periodic sentence (McKee) vs. front-load outcomes (1of10)

McKee's suspense sentence delays meaning to the final word. 1of10's "front-load outcomes" puts the revelation first. These are in tension for sentence 1 specifically.

Resolution: 1of10's front-loading applies to the *type of thing* that comes first (tension/curiosity before context). McKee's periodic sentence applies to *within-sentence* rhythm (the most important word lands last). You can front-load the gap in terms of sequence (curiosity before context) while still building suspense within the sentence (most important word last). "What he found the State Department couldn't explain" — curiosity comes first (the finding), suspense sentence structure (the gap is in the final word: "explain"). Both rules honored simultaneously.

### Misfit: The periodic sentence in low-pacing contexts

McKee's "suspense sentence" is ideal for dialogue and narration in fiction. For extremely spare voiceover (the Calm Prosecutor at its most minimal), the periodic structure can feel affected. If a sentence reads well as a short declarative ("He hired them, not sold them"), that serves better than forcing a periodic construction. McKee's rule is a best-practice, not a hard law. Suit it to the rhythm you're in.
