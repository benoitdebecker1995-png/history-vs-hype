Over the centuries, scholars, mystics, and theologians have wrestled with what he stands for and what lessons his story holds. This commemorates a story about the prophet Abraham. According to Islamic tradition, when Abraham was preparing his son Ishmael to be sacrificed, El tried to talk him out of it, but Abraham resisted. In fact, the story of his fall from grace told in the Quran implies that he gets God's permission to tempt humanity. The story in the Quran goes like this. Although the story of Satan refusing to bow down before Adam does not appear in most Christian sources, remarkably similar versions do show up in some pre-Islamic texts. One example is the Syriak Christian Book of the Cave of Treasures, which scholars date before the Quran. In that text, God presents Adam in a glorious manner to the angels who are then commanded to bow themselves before him. And when the prince of the lower order of angels saw what great majesty had been bestowed upon Adam, he was jealous of him from that day on, and he did not wish to worship him. And he said to his hosts, "You shall not worship him, and you shall not praise him with the angels. A similar story appears in a 4th century Coptic text from Egypt. The Archbishop Timothy of Alexandria describes God placing a crown on Adam's head and a scepter in his hand and then commanding the angels to worship him. But Satan refuses since he was a being of great pride. He drew himself up in a shameless manner and said, "It is more seemly that this human should come and worship me, for I existed before he came into being." According to this version, God orders the other angels to strip Satan naked and torture him as humiliation for his pride before casting him out of heaven. So the Quranic account of El's fall from grace is strikingly similar to several apocryphal Christian texts. These texts were circulating throughout the near east in late antiquity and they portray Satan as a being overcome with pride who refuses to bow to Adam and as a result is punished by God. Their echoes in the Quranic story suggest a shared conceptual framework and highlight how the Quran engages with, adapts, and reinterprets earlier Neareastern traditions and situates them within its own theological framework. Now, as I mentioned earlier, most modern Muslims agree that Elliss was never an angel, but rather one of the jin. Today, angels and jin are understood as distinct supernatural beings. Angels are God's obedient servants, often described as lacking free will. Jyn, on the other hand, are unpredictable. They have free will and can be either good or evil. The Quran quotes a group of jin saying, "Among us are Muslims, literally those who submit to God and among us are the unjust." So like humans, jin exist in both righteous and wicked forms. They live alongside humanity but can remain invisible or take various shapes including appearing as humans. However, while most modern Muslims accept that El was a jin, early Islamic thought was not so uniform. In fact, the idea that he was an angel had notable support in Islam's first several centuries. In the early 10th century, one of the most famous early Muslim theologians, Al Tubbery, not only affirmed that El was an angel, but he also cited a number of traditions, claiming that he was once a kind of angelic general. According to these reports, El led heavenly armies against a group of jin who had departed from paradise and made their home on earth where they were spreading corruption. After defeating them, El grew proud of his own power. And this arrogance ultimately led to his defiance of God and refusal to bow before Adam. What's really interesting though is some of these traditions that Alaburi cites give the name of Abl before his fall. He was once called Aazil. This sounds a lot like Aazel which is the name of a fallen angel linked with a group of angels called the watchers in the first book of Enoch. The Watchers teach mankind the art of warfare and sorcery among other things. So, it seems that by the early 10th century, traditions echoing the book of Enoch had entered elite Muslim discourse, shaping interpretations of Llis's backstory. These parallels suggest a broader cross-pollination between Jewish, Christian, and early Islamic thought. Some early Islamic sources suggest that angels and jin were not always seen as separate beings. In fact, some traditions cited by Alabury in the 9th century described the jin as a type of angel. But it's not an idea that's widely accepted today. For nearly 1,400 years, Muslim mystics, intellectuals, and artists have offered novel and sometimes striking interpretations of story. For example, the early Sufi mystic Monsor Al-Halaj saw El's fall as a tragedy, not a tale of arrogance, but of devotion. In his view, El refused to bow to Adam, not out of pride, but out of love for God. See, a central tenant of Islam is absolute monotheism. Only God is divine and only God may be worshiped. But according to Alhalage, El's devotion to God was so profound that he was unwilling to bow before anyone else, even if that meant defying God's commandment and being expelled from heaven. And that last part is critical. It's not that Alhalage believed that his punishment was undeserved or in any way unjust, only that it was the price of his devotion. Alhalaj's depiction of Lliss as the tragic ultimate monotheist paved the way for later mystics. Mystics such as Ain Al-Quda Alamadani propose that El and his devils can be understood as servants of God and an extension of his will. By clearly delineating God's faithful from the sinful, they are actually performing a sacred service to the divine. Now to be clear, these are pretty fringe ideas and both Alhalage and Alhamdani were actually executed for heterodoxy. But they testify to the rich diversity of mystical interpretations that the story of Abl has inspired among Muslim intellectuals throughout the medieval period and beyond. The story of his fall has been a way to explore both the nature of free will and theodysy, the problem of the existence of evil. In the first two centuries of Islam, a group called the Jobights allegedly asked a thorny philosophical question. If God is the source of all actions and nothing happens without his will or permission, does that mean he compelled to disobey? Now, our understanding of the job rights comes mostly from their critics, which makes it tricky to pin down what exactly they believed. But in any case, Islamic Orthodoxy ultimately rejected their framing of the issue and embraced the doctrine that sufficient free will exists, that God can justly punish El for his actions. The fact that God permits sinful behavior doesn't excuse the sinner, who still bears responsibility for choosing to act sinfully.
