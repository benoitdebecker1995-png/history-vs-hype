Forty million people. That's more than the entire population of Canada. More than Poland. More than California. And yet, they have no country to call their own.

The Kurds are one of the largest ethnic groups in the world without a state — and that's not an accident. It's the result of centuries of political maneuvering, broken promises, and the brutal reshaping of the Middle East after World War One.

So today, we're going to answer the question: why don't the Kurds have their own country?

## The Kurds and the Ottoman World

To understand the Kurdish question, you have to go back to the Ottoman Empire — the massive multi-ethnic state that controlled most of the Middle East, parts of Europe, and North Africa for over six centuries.

Within the Ottoman system, the Kurds weren't exactly powerless. Kurdish tribal leaders ran semi-autonomous emirates — essentially self-governing principalities — spread across the mountainous borderlands between what is now Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria. Places like Botan, Hakkari, and Baban had their own local rulers, their own armies, and considerable independence from Constantinople.

But here's the thing: that autonomy wasn't built on Kurdish national identity. It was built on tribal loyalty, religious authority, and the practical reality that the Ottomans couldn't fully control rugged mountain terrain from the other side of the empire. Kurdish leaders played ball with the Ottomans because it was mutually beneficial — not because they saw themselves as part of a unified Kurdish nation.

That started to change in the nineteenth century. As nationalism swept through Europe and began reshaping empires — the Greeks broke free, the Serbs, the Romanians — Kurdish intellectuals and tribal leaders started asking a new question: could the Kurds have something more than local autonomy? Could they have a nation?

The answer, for most of the 1800s, was no. Revolts broke out — Sheikh Ubeydullah's rebellion in 1880 was one of the most significant, rallying Kurds across the Ottoman-Persian border under a vision of Kurdish political unity. It was crushed. The Ottomans, with Persian cooperation, had no interest in allowing a Kurdish state to emerge in the middle of their territories.

## The Promise of Sèvres

World War One changed everything. The Ottoman Empire backed the losing side — the Central Powers — and by 1918, it was shattered. The victorious Allied powers moved in to carve up Ottoman territory, and they did so with extraordinary ambition.

In August 1920, representatives signed the Treaty of Sèvres. And for the Kurds, it looked — for one brief moment — like history was finally moving in their direction.

Articles 62 through 64 of the treaty specifically addressed the Kurdish question. The treaty called for a commission to study the possibility of Kurdish autonomy in predominantly Kurdish regions. More than that, it left open the door for the Kurds to appeal to the League of Nations for full independence within one year, if they could demonstrate they were capable of self-governance.

On paper, it was an extraordinary opening. After centuries of Ottoman rule and failed revolts, the international community seemed to be acknowledging that the Kurds deserved a homeland.

But Sèvres never actually went into effect. And here's why.

Mustafa Kemal — the military commander who would become Atatürk, the founder of modern Turkey — refused to accept the treaty. He launched a war of independence, defeating Greek forces in Anatolia, pushing back Allied influence, and establishing a new Turkish state on his own terms. By 1923, he had completely rewritten the post-war settlement.

## Lausanne and the Door Closes

The Treaty of Lausanne, signed in July 1923, replaced Sèvres entirely. And the Kurds weren't even at the table.

Lausanne was a negotiation between the new Turkish Republic and the Allied powers — Britain, France, Italy, Greece, and others. The Kurds, despite having been explicitly mentioned in the earlier treaty, had no seat in the room and no voice in the outcome.

The result was devastating for Kurdish aspirations. Lausanne drew the borders of modern Turkey — and in doing so, divided the Kurdish population between four different countries: Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria. There was no mention of Kurdish autonomy. No promised homeland. The provisions of Sèvres were simply erased.

Why did the Allies go along with this? Partly because Mustafa Kemal had won on the battlefield and they had little leverage. But also because geopolitical interests had shifted. Britain controlled Mosul and the oil beneath it. France had carved out Syria. Nobody wanted to create a new Kurdish state that might destabilize arrangements they'd already made.

The Kurds had been promised something in 1920. By 1923, it was gone — traded away for the stability of a new Turkish Republic and the interests of European powers.

## The Weight of What Didn't Happen

The tragedy of the Kurdish situation isn't just what was done to them — it's what almost happened and then didn't. The window opened at Sèvres, and it closed at Lausanne, just three years later.

In the century since, the Kurds have remained divided, stateless, and caught between competing national interests. They've faced suppression in Turkey, genocide in Iraq under Saddam Hussein, civil war in Syria. The closest thing to a Kurdish state today is the Kurdistan Region of Iraq — a semi-autonomous area with its own government and military, but still technically part of Iraq.

And through all of it, the ghost of 1920 hangs in the air. The promise that was made and then unmade. The map that was almost drawn differently.

Forty million people are still waiting.
